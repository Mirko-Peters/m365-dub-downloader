## Key takeaways

- Power Platform is a distributed development platform inside Microsoft 365, not a productivity tool, and treating it like Excel leads to default environment sprawl, risky connectors, flow explosions, licensing surprises and zombie apps.
- Standard Centers of Excellence often fail because they only observe and advise; governance works when it is enforced architecturally through tiered environments, mandatory ALM pipelines, DLP connector segmentation and service account ownership.
- Start by locking down the default environment, creating personal, team and enterprise tiers, retiring apps after 90 days without usage and giving the CoE real authority with executive sponsorship.

## FAQ

**Why is the default environment a governance risk in Power Platform?**

Because every user is an environment maker by default, the default environment quietly becomes the main production platform. The episode cites tenant audits where 70 to 80 percent of apps and flows live there, including one enterprise with 6,200 apps, 4,000 flows and 900 connectors, many without owners or monitoring.

**Why do Power Platform Centers of Excellence often fail to stop sprawl?**

Most CoEs fail because they only observe and advise, while makers can still take the frictionless path. A CoE can spot a zombie app but cannot retire it. Mirko argues the CoE needs veto power over environments, connectors, ALM pipeline exceptions and retirement, plus full-time staff and visible backing from the CIO or CTO.

**How should Power Platform environments be tiered?**

Use three tiers: a locked-down default environment for personal experimentation, approved team environments for departmental solutions, and restricted enterprise production environments. Production apps require architecture and security review, managed solutions, deployment through ALM pipelines and service account ownership, so makers in lower tiers cannot push changes straight into production.
