## Key takeaways

- Do not bake an IoT Hub connection string into the device image; let Azure DPS assign the hub on first boot after the device proves its identity.
- Choose TPM, X.509 or symmetric key attestation based on hardware, supplier chain and service process, and plan certificate renewal, revocation and replacement from day one.
- Design enrollment groups and allocation policies around real trust, data residency and capacity boundaries rather than portal convenience.

## FAQ

**What does Azure Device Provisioning Service actually do?**

Azure DPS handles device admission, not daily traffic. A device contacts the global DPS endpoint with its registration ID and cryptographic proof, DPS checks it against an individual enrollment or enrollment group, applies the allocation policy and returns the assigned IoT Hub. Telemetry, commands and device twin traffic then run through that hub.

**Should I use TPM, X.509 certificates or symmetric keys for DPS attestation?**

It depends on your hardware, supplier chain and service process, not on one best method. TPM ties identity to a chip and suits managed gateways. X.509 with CA-backed enrollment groups fits fleets crossing several suppliers but needs renewal and revocation plans. Symmetric keys suit controlled cases, since leaked group key material exposes the group.

**Which DPS allocation policy should I use to assign devices to IoT hubs?**

Start from business boundaries rather than automation. Static configuration fits devices bound to a known plant, customer or country. Lowest latency can work for regional hub pools, but it measures network delay, not data residency. Evenly weighted distribution expresses a planned split and does not track live telemetry load, so capacity still needs planning.
