## Key takeaways

- RBAC decides who can act while Azure Policy decides what may exist, and governance at scale needs both, layered with identity governance, resource locks and monitoring through a management group hierarchy.
- Policies should roll out audit first, then modify or deploy-if-not-exists, then deny, ring by ring and managed as code in Git with pull requests and pipelines.
- Group-based RBAC, PIM, managed identities, Key Vault with RBAC authorization and purge protection, golden paths and Entra ID Governance turn compliance into a continuous, self-enforcing system aligned with Zero Trust.

## FAQ

**What is the difference between Azure RBAC and Azure Policy?**

RBAC decides who can act, while Azure Policy decides what is allowed to exist. RBAC is identity-centric and defaults to deny; policy is resource-centric and defaults to allow. A user with Contributor rights can still be blocked from deploying a public storage account by a deny policy, so real governance needs both layers.

**How do you roll out Azure Policy deny effects without breaking production?**

Start every policy in audit mode for weeks, ideally with enforcement disabled first, to measure the blast radius. Then use modify or deploy-if-not-exists to fix existing resources, and only then switch to deny. Roll out ring by ring, from sandboxes and dev subscriptions to production, with a compliance scan at each transition.

**Why are managed identities safer than service principals with client secrets?**

Managed identities remove static secrets: Azure issues and rotates the credential, and workloads receive short-lived tokens from a local endpoint. Nothing ends up in config files or repositories. They do not fix authorization, though, so scope each identity to one environment and to specific resources, because a compromised workload inherits its permissions.
