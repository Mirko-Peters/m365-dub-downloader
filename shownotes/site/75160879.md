## Key takeaways

- Test a production change such as an extra shift, new machine or smaller buffer in a simulation first, because constraints often move rather than disappear.
- Start with one concrete decision that has an owner, build a baseline that behaves like normal operations, and model qualified coverage, setups, queues and disruptions as ranges.
- ERP, MES, Microsoft Fabric, [Power BI](https://www.m365.fm/entity/power-bi/?utm_source=podcast&utm_medium=shownotes&utm_campaign=factory-simulation) and AI each support the decision, but only a factory model with explicit rules can rehearse how the plant will respond.

## FAQ

**What is factory simulation in manufacturing?**

Factory simulation is a controlled model of how work moves through part of a factory, using today's rules, resources, orders and normal variation. You change one condition, such as an extra shift or a smaller buffer, and compare the response. It is not a glossy 3D animation, and it does not remove uncertainty.

**Why doesn't adding a shift always improve on-time delivery?**

Because the constraint often moves rather than disappears. An extra shift at a machining cell may lack qualified operators, material, quality release or maintenance cover, and downstream assembly or test may not absorb the extra parts. Machine utilization rises while customer orders still wait. Simulation tests the whole flow, not just the resource under review.

**Can generative AI create the best production schedule?**

Not on its own. Generative AI helps with language, documents and explaining results, but simulation and optimization tools work from explicit rules about machines, setups, tools and operator approvals. A sensible pattern runs approved scenarios first, then lets an assistant help planners compare documented options. People still decide what gets released to the factory.
