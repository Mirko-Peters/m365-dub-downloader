Running a Microsoft-first MSP means turning Intune, Defender and Entra ID into a repeatable security service across many customers. Intune MVP Albin Klinaku shares what separates good from great deployments, which Defender and Conditional Access features are often missed, and why phishing-resistant MFA and token protection matter now. A practical episode for MSP owners, IT admins and security architects.

## Key takeaways

- A great Intune deployment applies security baselines to all devices, blocks unwanted enrollment such as personal devices, and is built with a cloud-native mindset rather than copying the old MDM.
- In Defender, attack surface reduction rules should move from audit to block mode after reviewing the audit logs, and patch management must be in place before vulnerability management makes sense.
- For identities, organizations should move to phishing-resistant authentication with passkeys or Windows Hello for Business and make full use of Conditional Access features such as risk-based policies and token protection.

## FAQ

**What makes a great Microsoft Intune deployment?**

A great Intune deployment applies security baselines to all devices rather than a single group and blocks unwanted enrollment such as personal devices. Albin Klinaku checks enrollment settings first, because an open tenant signals a weak setup. He also looks for a cloud-native mindset instead of an attempt to duplicate the old MDM.

**When should attack surface reduction rules move from audit to block mode?**

Move attack surface reduction rules to block mode after a period in audit mode and a review of the audit logs. Albin Klinaku often sees ASR rules left on audit for a year. Audit first is sensible because block rules can break line of business apps, but staying in audit forever wastes the protection.

**Why are passkeys considered phishing-resistant MFA?**

Passkeys are phishing-resistant because they are bound to the domain where they were registered. On a fake login site the domain does not match, so authentication fails. Number matching, by contrast, can be bypassed by a man-in-the-middle attacker who steals the session token and imports it into a browser to access the account.
