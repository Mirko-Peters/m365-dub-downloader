## Key takeaways

- Before choosing tools, organizations should decide who they want to be with AI and which activities must stay human, especially relationships with clients and employees.
- AI adoption is mostly a culture and process problem: start from business strategy, and design workflows where a knowledgeable human must check AI results, which also contains hallucinations.
- Responsibility and trust cannot be delegated to a chatbot provider; the company using AI stays accountable, and confidential data needs security measures matched to its sensitivity.

## FAQ

**Is AI adoption a technology problem or a culture problem?**

According to Wiebke Apitzsch it is a culture problem. Hesitation comes from the unanswered question of who a company wants to be with AI. Once leaders agree on ethical guidelines for what AI should and should not do, technology and change follow, and a well-designed process sometimes needs hardly any change management.

**How can companies deal with AI hallucinations?**

Design the process so a knowledgeable human must review the final result and cannot just click yes. Hallucinations come from missing data, flawed data or weak prompts, and a stochastic model will never reach 100% accuracy. In her catalog example, AI shortened texts slightly over target, forcing editors to read and correct each one.

**Which work should stay human when a company automates with AI?**

Work that builds relationships with clients and employees should stay human, with AI only supporting it. Drawing on Kant's distinction between price and dignity, Apitzsch argues that anything with only a price, such as routine notification emails, can be automated, while relational work, decisions about money and new impulses remain human.
