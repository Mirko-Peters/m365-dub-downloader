The operating model, Rassie, Escalation and the end of ticket-based governance. Here is the part nobody wants to do because it sounds like process. But without an operating model, everything you build so far becomes optional the moment the right person complains loudly enough. As your governance isn't sustained by policies, it's sustained by decision rights, who is allowed to decide, who is allowed to override and who has to clean up the mess when reality disagrees. First artifact isn't another initiative. It's a Rassie that names owners in plain language. Platform team owns the landing zone, the management group hierarchy, subscription vending, shared networking, central logging destinations and the policy baselines that define non-negotiables. Security owns the security requirements, the risk acceptance criteria, the review of exceptions that change exposure and the monitoring expectations that prove controls are working. In- Teams, own the workloads inside the boundaries including data classification inside their apps, operational reliability and compliance with the baseline. They do not own the baseline itself. Finops of finance owns tagging standards for chargeback and the budget model plus escalation when spent violates expectations. Audit and risk owns evidence requirements, the cadence of reviews and the definition of what acceptable looks like for regulated workloads. If you can't point at an owner you don't have governance, you have distributed blame. Now you define decision paths. This is where most enterprises break things because they default to open a ticket. Ticket-based governance is governance theatre. It centralises friction, not control. The right model is simple. Three lanes. Self-serve lane. If it's within baseline and within budget, teams deploy without asking. That includes standard resource types, approved regions, approved SKUs and approved network patterns. The platform team does not approve normal work. Approval lane. If it increases risk or cost meaningfully, it requires approval. Not by the platform team, but by the correct owner. Cost exceptions go to Finops. Exposure exceptions go to security. Architecture deviations go to the platform team if they affect shared services or boundaries. Denied lane. Some things are just not allowed. Public endpoints in production if your model forbids them. Random regions for data residency reasons. Broad role assignments at the tenant route. If something belongs in the denied lane, you encode it as deny, not as a policy document. And yes, this will cause deployment failures. That is not a failure of governance. That is governance functioning. Now the workflow for exceptions, because exceptions are the entropy gateway. If you don't design the workflow, teams will invent one by messaging whoever they know. The workflow is. Request review. Approve. Expire. Revalidate. Remove. Request includes scope, duration, reason and a link to a ticket. No ticket, no exception. Because we need it is not traceable intent. Review includes risk classification. Is this a waiver or is it mitigated? What compensating controls exist? Who owns those controls? Review includes explicit expiry. Even mitigations get a review date. Nothing stays forever without re-approval. Expire means the system forces the question again. If the business still needs it, they ask again and the owner re-accepts the risk. If they don't ask again, the exception dies. That's how you prevent exception permanence. Revalidate means you periodically audit active exemptions and remove the ones that don't have a current business reason. Not annually. Continuously in small batches. Remove means cleanup is part of the process, not a future hope. Our make policy behave like code because policy is code. Version your initiatives and assignments. Use pull requests. Require review from the owners who will carry the risk. Deploy in rollout rings. Dev management group first. Then non-prod. Then prod. That distinction matters because policy changes are production changes. Also, treat exemptions as code adjacent artifacts. If your policy life cycle doesn't track them, you'll end up with often de-exceptions and mystery compliance gaps. Finally, stop making the platform team the help desk. The platform team's job is to maintain the guardrails and the paved roads not to click approve on every deployment. If your governance model requires a ticket for normal work, teams will root around you. They'll use shadow subscriptions, alternate tenants or unmanaged identities. The system will still run it. It will just run without your control. So the operating model is the mechanism that keeps autonomy safe.
