But you have to do the work upfront to prove it. The risks. What can go wrong? Agentex systems introduce new failure modes. That's the thing nobody wants to talk about. We talk about the value, the speed and the toil reduction. We don't talk about what happens when things break. An agent can make a decision that seems perfectly sound in the moment. But turns out to be exactly wrong. Imagine an agent sees a deployment causing high latency. It decides to roll back that deployment on the surface. That's a reasonable choice. Except the deployment wasn't actually the problem. The latency was caused by a slow database query triggered by a cache configuration that had nothing to do with the new code. The agent rolls back, removing the feature you just shipped. The latency stays. Now you've solved the wrong problem and broken the right solution. This isn't a theory. The correction rate for Agentex systems is between 40 and 73%. That means nearly half of agent decisions require a human to review or fix them. Let that sink in nearly half. That isn't a rounding error. It's a structural reality of how these systems work today. Most people see this as a failure. They ask why we would ever trust a system where half the decisions are wrong. But that's the wrong way to look at it. That correction rate exists because these systems are operating at the very edge of their capability. They are making decisions sophisticated enough that they occasionally need a human to refine them. They are built with escalation parts for a reason. That isn't a design flaw. That is exactly how the design is supposed to work. The real failure happens when you deploy agents without those escalation parts. When you give them the authority to act without any oversight. When you don't have human eyes available to catch a mistake before it becomes a disaster. That's when a 40% correction rate becomes a crisis instead of a feature. The other major risk is over autonomy. This is the temptation that kills most deployments. You get your first agent working well and it handles low risk decisions with great accuracy. So you expand its authority. You give it higher stakes. You remove the requirement for human check-ins. You try to maximize the autonomy because you think that's where the value is. Then something goes wrong. The agent hits a scenario it hasn't seen before. There is no human there to catch it. The impact is severe. And suddenly your entire program is under review because someone trusted the system too much too soon. The solution isn't to stop expanding authority. It's to expand it deliberately. Start with decisions where the agent has proven 90% accuracy over several months. Then move to slightly higher risk tasks. Measure again. Expand again. This is tiered. Autonomy. Different decisions get different levels of oversight based on proven performance. There is also an organizational risk that people underestimate. People feel threatened by agents. They feel like their expertise is being devalued. If your team doesn't buy in, they will find ways to sabotage the system. They'll ask for exceptions. They'll keep using manual workarounds. They'll push back on every decision the agent makes. The system becomes half trusted because the people running it don't actually believe in it. You can't solve this with technology. You solve it with change management. You need clear communication about what is actually happening. You need to involve the people affected in the design process. Frame the agents as augmentation, not a replacement. Make sure everyone understands how their role is changing and what their new responsibilities look like. If you skip this, you'll have a technical capability that nobody actually uses. Finally, there is the risk of governance. If you don't have clear policies, agents will eventually find a way to violate compliance. They'll access data they shouldn't. They'll make decisions that contradict your regulatory rules. They will create liability. The solution is building governance into the architecture from day one. Don't add it later. Don't write the policies after you've already deployed. Encode the constraints into the infrastructure itself. Make it impossible for an agent to violate a policy because the system won't let it. If you get these risks, write "agentic systems work". If you missed them, you've built a tool that is either too limited to be useful or powerful enough to be dangerous. There is no middle ground either you designed for these risks or you didn't.
