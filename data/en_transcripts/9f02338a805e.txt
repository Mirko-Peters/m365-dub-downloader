Row-level security is no longer just a reporting control. It is now a co-pilot control. That distinction changes everything about how you decide who sees what. In the old world, RLS was simple. You defined roles in your model and gave each one a filter rule. Sales managers in the West only saw West region transactions, while accountants in Europe only saw European cost data. These rules lived behind the scenes. When a user opened a report, RLS silently filtered the data to show only their authorized slice. It was clean, invisible and predictable, but now co-pilot can reason over that data. And this is where the shift happens. Co-pilot doesn't just look at what's on the report page, it can ask questions that drill into every single corner of the data the user is technically allowed to touch. Think about how this plays out in real life. A salesperson in the West has read access to a model, and their RLS rule limits them to West region transactions. They open a report and see their regional pipeline, which is exactly what you expected. But then they open co-pilot and ask for a year over year comparison of sales by product category for their specific region. Co-pilot takes that request and runs it through the semantic model while applying the RLS filters. It returns the comparison, and suddenly the salesperson sees insights that were never in a pre-built report because no one thought to combine those specific dimensions before. This isn't a data breach. The data is there, the user is authorized, and RLS is doing its job perfectly. However, the user now has visibility into analytical slices that no one explicitly planned for them to see. They can ask questions you didn't anticipate. They can discover performance trends that used to require a formal request to the analytics team. This is what I mean by amplification. You aren't bypassing security, but you are amplifying a user's ability to reason over the data they already own. That is incredibly powerful, but it's also why your governance has to become much more intentional. The problem is that most RLS was designed with static reports in mind. You build roles around what should be visible on a dashboard, assuming the user wouldn't ask questions outside of those boundaries. That assumption breaks the moment you introduce co-pilot. The user will ask questions that no report ever exposed, simply because they can. The real question you have to answer is whether that's okay. If a manager uses co-pilot to find product insights that weren't in the official reports, was that your intention? If the answer is yes, then your RLS is fine. If the answer is no, you have a massive gap in your strategy. This is the point where RLS stops being a technical setting and becomes a governance decision. You aren't just asking what data a user should see anymore. You're asking what predictions and analyses that user should be allowed to request. The answers to those two questions might be very different. Imagine a customer service rep who has access to order history to help resolve issues. In a standard report, they see order details for their assigned customers, which is exactly what you intended, but now they can ask co-pilot which of their customers are most likely to churn next quarter. Co-pilot routes that to your churn prediction measure and applies RLS, so they only see their own customers. They get the answer and start reaching out to ad risk accounts proactively. Is that a good thing? In most cases, yes. It's a perfect example of intended amplification where a rep uses predictive insights to do their job better. But you have to decide that on purpose. You have to design your RLS rules with that specific use case in mind to ensure the role configuration actually allows it. The inverse risk is just as real. If you aren't careful about what data a user can touch, co-pilot will find ways to reason over sensitive info you never meant to highlight. A user with basic read access to payroll data could ask co-pilot for a comparative salary analysis or ask it to flag outliers. If RLS isn't set up to stop that co-pilot will give them the answer. The architecture is forcing governance out of the abstract and into the concrete. RLS is now the control surface where you decide what users can do with AI, not just what they can see on a screen. The choices you make here will directly shape what happens when co-pilot starts orchestrating your predictive models. Object level security and AI ready models. RLS handles the rows but there is another layer of security that is now critical because co-pilot can see your entire model. This is object level security or OLS. While RLS filters rows, OLS operates at the column level to hide entire fields from specific roles. This distinction is a huge deal when you are getting ready for co-pilot access. Think about the difference in how these two work. With RLS and HR person might only see employees in their specific department. The rows are filtered but they still see every column, including names, salaries and performance ratings. With OLS you can go a step further. You can set it up so that the HR person sees everyone in their department but the salary column is completely gone. It isn't just blurred or masked. It is hidden. For that specific user, the column doesn't even exist. This is essential for sensitive data like health records, credit card numbers or disciplinary files. These aren't things you just filter. You exclude them from certain roles entirely. An HR coordinator needs names and departments but they have no reason to see salary data. OLS ensures they can't see it by making the field invisible within the semantic model itself. Here is the part people miss. Hiding a column in the report interface is not the same as OLS. I see organizations hide salary columns from reports all the time and think they are secure but those columns are still sitting in the underlying data. If a user clicks the co-pilot button and asks the question about pay, they might get a full answer. The data is there and the report just isn't showing it. Since co-pilot has access to the whole model by default, nothing stops it from reading those hidden columns unless OLS is turned on. This is where co-pilot's reasoning becomes a liability if your OLS isn't right. Co-pilot doesn't care about the report layer. It looks straight at the semantic model. If sensitive columns aren't protected, co-pilot will use them. A user might ask a simple question about what's driving headcount costs and co-pilot will pull salary data to explain the variance. That data was never supposed to be accessible but it was because you only hid it in the UI. OLS stops this at the source. You configure it at the model level and specify which columns belong to which security group. Salary data gets a specific classification and employees only see it if they have the right permission. Co-pilot inherits these rules automatically. When it looks at the model, it respects OLS just like it respects RLS. Sensitive columns stay locked away. The user can't ask co-pilot to show them because from their perspective, those columns aren't there. This is exactly why the prepped for AI designation exists. Microsoft has a formal process for marking a model this way and it isn't just a marketing label. It is a governance checkpoint. When you mark a model as ready for AI, you are telling the system that a few things are true. You've provided clear names and descriptions. You've built proper relationships and measures. But most importantly, you've confirmed that RLS and OLS are configured correctly. You've thought about who should have access and you've tested your roles against co-pilot. This certification tells your organization that the model is safe. Users can trust co-pilot to give them answers without accidentally leaking sensitive details. Your data science team can build predictive models without worrying about security gaps. Your governance team can audit the model and know exactly what access really means. The prepped for AI label is the new foundation for your access control. It isn't just saying the model works with co-pilot. It is a guarantee that the model has been audited, hardened and approved for AI-driven analysis. The prepped for AI certification. Let's move from the theory to the actual process. Microsoft has built a specific workflow in Power BI that turns prepped for AI from a vague goal into a measurable certification. You don't just declare that your model is ready for co-pilot and hope for the best. Instead, you work through a structured checklist to confirm every single element before testing it. At the end of that process, you receive a badge. That badge signals something very specific. This model has been audited and is officially safe for AI-driven predictions. The workflow starts with naming and descriptions. We've talked about this a lot, so I'll keep it brief. Every table needs a clear name, every column needs a description that makes sense to a business user, and every measure needs an unambiguous name with its DAX logic documented in plain English. This isn't just a nice-to-have feature. It is the foundation of the entire system because without these labels, co-pilot simply cannot understand what it's looking at. Next, you have to look at your relationships and measures. Your semantic model must be structurally sound, which means tables are related correctly and cardinality is explicitly defined. You need to ensure hierarchies are set up and measures are calculated consistently across the board. This is basic data architecture that has been required for good reporting since the beginning, but now it's a mandatory check for AI readiness. Then we get to the security configuration. You have to configure and test your row level and object level security to ensure they work exactly as intended. You define the roles, specify which rows or columns each row can see, and then run tests to prove those rules hold up. This step forces you to be intentional about governance. You aren't just checking a box here. You are documenting exactly who has access to what and why that access exists. Now we hit the part that is brand new and specific to AI. The fourth requirement is defining your AI schema. This is where you explicitly tell Power BI which tables and measures co-pilot is allowed to use. You don't want to expose the entire model by default, so you curate the experience. You might have technical tables used for background calculations or development measures that aren't finished yet. You hide those from the AI. Your AI schema defines the specific surface area that co-pilot is authorized to reason over. This curation is vital because it stops the AI from hallucinating. If your model has 30 tables and co-pilot can see all of them, it might try to join data that shouldn't be joined. It might find a correlation between two things that aren't actually related just because they happen to be in the same model. By restricting the AI to a curated subset, you ensure that co-pilot stays within the boundaries of what users should actually be asking about. Finally, you define your AI instructions. These are natural language guidelines that tell co-pilot how to handle confusing requests. For example, your company might have three different definitions for revenue depending on the department. A user might just ask for revenue without being specific, so your instruction would tell co-pilot to assume they mean total net revenue unless they say otherwise. Co-pilot reads that instruction and follows it every single time. The certification process walks you through every one of these steps. You confirm the names are clear, validate the security, and set the AI boundaries. Once that's done, the model is officially marked as prepped for AI. This badge tells your team that the model has passed a real governance review and is approved for predictive access. It turns the model into something data scientists can build on and something co-pilot can reason over reliably. This isn't just more bureaucracy. It's a guarantee of quality. Semantic, link labs, and governance automation. You've prepared your first model, it's certified, and it's ready for the AI. But in a large organization, you don't just have one model to worry about. If you want to scale co-pilot across the whole company, every single one of those models needs to meet the same high standards. This is where manual governance falls apart. You cannot audit hundreds of models by hand. And you certainly can't update every description one by one. You need a way to handle governance at scale. And that is exactly why semantic link labs exists. Semantic link labs is a specialized toolkit built on top of the standard semantic link. It isn't a new product, but rather an enhancement that gives you programmatic access to tasks that used to require clicking through the Power BI interface. You can manage roles, configure security filters, and update descriptions across hundreds of models using a single script. All of this happens through code in a fabric notebook, which is much faster than manual work. Take role management as an example. Usually you'd have to open Power BI Desktop, create a role, and set up the filters for every single model. If you have 50 models, you're doing that work 50 times. With semantic link labs, you write one Python script that defines the role in its filters and then you apply it to all 50 models at once. This ensures that your security is consistent across the entire environment. The same logic applies to managing users. Instead of manually adding people to roles, you can write a script that talks to your HR system. You can run a validation script that scans every model, flags anything that's missing a description, and generates a report. If your company operates globally, you need your model descriptions in multiple languages like Spanish, French, and German. This allows you to update every language version simultaneously without the manual effort. Naming standards are where this gets even more powerful. This is the shift to governance as code. You write the scripts, version them, and run them on a schedule. Because they are automated, they aren't prone to human error or the inconsistency that comes with manual work. You move from managing one model at a time to managing an entire enterprise fleet reliably. Governance is no longer just a final checkpoint. It becomes a continuous part of how your models live and breathe. Building the data pipeline. From source to prediction, the reality of raw data is that it's messy. It lives in ERPs, transactional systems, and third party databases designed for processing, not for analysis. To make this data useful, you first have to get it into fabric. Everything starts in the fabric lake house. This is your bronze layer, where data lands exactly as it exists in the source system without any transformations. You aren't worried about schema consistency or quality yet because the lake house acts as a staging ground for accumulating information from everywhere. Once the data is staged, your engineering team builds the pipelines that actually clean it. These pipelines handle missing values, standardized formats, and resolve duplicates to turn chaos into a cohesive structure. This creates the silver layer, which is a cleaner and de-duplicated version of your records. Many organizations take it a step further by building a gold layer, which is where fact and dimension tables are finally constructed for high-level analysis. On top of this, prepare data sits the semantic model. This is where the shift from engineering to meaning happens. The semantic model doesn't move data around, but it defines what that data represents by creating measures for business metrics. It establishes relationships between tables, adds hierarchies, and locks down row-level security so everyone sees only what they should. Now your predictive models can enter the picture. You open a fabric notebook and use SEMP to pull in the measures directly from your semantic model. If you're predicting customer churn, you need historical outcomes and behavioral features like transaction size or purchase frequency because the semantic model already has these measures defined. SEMPI retrieves them with the relationships and hierarchies intact. Your data science team then trains an algorithm on this grounded data. The model learns based on your specific business definitions and once it's validated, it scores every active customer for churn probability. These results are written back into the lake house as a new table called churn predictions, which includes customer IDs, scores, and timestamps. But the circle only closes when you connect those predictions back to the business. You create a new measure in your semantic model that reads from that churn predictions table. This measure aggregates the data by region or product so co-pilot can reference it just like any other metric. When a user asks which segments are at risk, co-pilot routes to this measure, applies security rules, and returns an answer grounded in your actual predictive model. This is the full pipeline engineering transforms the raw data, the semantic model adds meaning, and the predictive models run on that foundation. The results flow back through the semantic layer so users can interact with predictions through the same interface they use for regular analytics. The structural point here is that predictions don't exist in a vacuum. They aren't separate from your BI infrastructure but are integrated into it using the same definitions and security. The pipeline isn't a straight line from source to notebook but a loop that moves from the lake to the model and back to the user as an insight. This integration makes co-pilot driven predictions trustworthy. Every insight the AI surfaces has been through a structured auditable pipeline grounded in your business rules. It isn't a black box output from a random notebook but a properly engineered business capability. A fabric data agent is fundamentally different from a chatbot and understanding this distinction is the only way to make prediction work reliably at scale. A chatbot responds to natural language by generating text but a fabric data agent responds to intent by executing structured operations against your data infrastructure. When you configure a fabric data agent, you aren't building a conversational interface. You're building a query interpreter. You specify which semantic models this agent can access and you define exactly which tables and measures it has the authority to use. You write instructions that explain how it should handle edge cases and you might even provide example queries to guide its logic. If a user asks about revenue, you tell the agent to look for measures starting with revenue and filter by the user's region. These examples aren't conversation starters because they function as templates that guide the agent's reasoning when it encounters similar requests. This is where prediction enters the picture. A fabric data agent understands that some of your measures are predictive by nature. You might have a measure called predicted churn risk that returns a probability or another called forecasted revenue next quarter that returns a specific dollar amount. The agent knows these are different from standard descriptive measures. When it sees a request for a prediction, it roots to the right measure and structures the query correctly so it can interpret the results properly. This matters because predictions are incredibly easy to misinterpret. If a user asks co-pilot what churn will be next month, the request is completely ambiguous. Do they want the probability for every single customer or are they looking for the aggregate rate for the whole company? They might need it for their specific region or just one product line. A generic chatbot would simply generate a plausible sounding answer and might even invent a number to fill the gap. A fabric data agent with the right configuration understands this ambiguity and will ask clarifying questions instead of guessing. If you've given it good instructions, it can infer context from the user's role and data access. A support manager likely wants churn predictions for their own customers while a VP of retention probably needs company-wide totals. The agent learns these patterns and applies them. The measures are calculated using their original DAX definitions so there is no approximation or guessing involved. There is no separate code path because the prediction moves through the same infrastructure as everything else in your system. The agent also validates the results before the user ever sees them. It checks to see if the numbers are reasonable and if they fall within expected ranges. If something looks wrong, the agent can escalate the issue or decline to answer rather than returning garbage data. Then we have lineage and evidence. This lineage is the only way to build real trust. When a user sees a prediction and wants to know where it came from, the agent provides a trace that is both auditable and repeatable. If another user asks the same question, they get the same result because the same query executed through the same infrastructure. This is a massive departure from copilot generating predictions on its own. Left to its own devices, copilot would try to reason through the numbers and generate plausible text before moving on. A fabric data agent executing a structured query against a semantic model returns something that can actually be tested and verified. The prediction either came from the right measure or it didn't, and the security rules either applied correctly or they didn't. These are binary facts rather than a spectrum of confidence. This is how copilot finally becomes trustworthy. It doesn't happen by making the AI smarter, but by connecting it to systems that are structured and validated. The intelligence lives in your semantic models and your predictive engines. Copilot's only job is rooting users to those systems and explaining the results. The fabric data agent is the mechanism that makes that rooting reliable. Grounding copilot in your business context. Grounding is the word we use when everything works correctly and copilot's reasoning stays aligned with how your business actually operates, but grounding isn't something copilot does on its own. It is something your architecture either enables or it prevents. Most organizations get this wrong because they assume grounding is a copilot feature. It isn't. It is a baseline requirement. If grounding isn't there, your predictions will fail silently. Think about what grounding actually requires in a real-world scenario. Copilot needs context from four specific sources. The structure of your semantic model, the richness of your metadata, the configuration of your security rules, and the instructions you've provided. Each of these is a dependency. If even one of them is weak, the whole system becomes unreliable. It starts with semantic model relationships. These aren't optional extras because they are foundational to how the system thinks. When you define that a customer table relates to an order table through a customer ID, you are encoding a structural fact about your business. Copilot reads that relationship and understands that it can aggregate orders to the customer level. The joint logic is implicit. Now imagine your semantic model has unclear or messy relationships. Maybe a customer ID exists in both tables but you haven't explicitly linked them. Or perhaps you've marked a relationship as inactive because you wanted reports to use a different logic in certain cases. Copilot sees that ambiguity and is forced to guess. It might create a join that is technically valid but semantically wrong. A user asks for the average order value per customer but Copilot routes through an unintended path and returns numbers that don't match your official reports. The user loses trust and your team has to waste time investigating. It turns out Copilot was using an inactive relationship that nobody intended it to touch. The problem wasn't the AI's reasoning but the ambiguity of the model itself. Metadata quality multiplies this effect. A measure without a description is a total mystery to an AI. Copilot has to guess what it means based on the name and the formula. A measure called total amount could mean gross revenue, net revenue after returns or something else entirely. The DAX formula tells you what is being calculated but it never tells you why. A measure with a proper description stating it is net revenue minus approved returns in USD removes all that doubt. Copilot reads that description and understands the scope, the calculation method and the currency. When aggregation levels matter, the description flags it. When the measure requires specific context to be meaningful, the description explains it. Without this, Copilot makes assumptions that usually turn out to be wrong. Security misalignment creates a different kind of grounding failure. The row level security rules you've set up for reports might not cover everything Copilot can do. A user might not be able to see a column in a report because it's hidden but they can still ask Copilot about it if object level security isn't configured. The data isn't supposed to be accessible but it is. This is a grounding failure because Copilot's access isn't grounded in your actual security intent. The gap between what you meant to restrict and what is actually restricted becomes a major vulnerability. It's a subtle problem because everything seems to work without any error messages. The prediction comes back. But the user sees data they should never have accessed. Grounding requires that your security rules align with your actual intent, not just your reporting design. Instructions are the fourth and final grounding mechanism. These are the guidelines you give Copilot to help it interpret messy or ambiguous requests. If you don't provide these instructions, Copilot has to make default assumptions that might not match your business logic. With clear instructions, you can tell the AI to use net revenue when someone asks for revenue without being specific. You can tell it to only include customers who have had a transaction in the last 12 months. Now, the reasoning stays grounded in your business rules rather than the AI's own defaults. The critical insight here is that grounding isn't a property of Copilot. It is a property of the system that Copilot is querying. If your semantic model has a weak structure, grounding fails. If your metadata is sparse or your security is inconsistent, grounding fails. Copilot is just the interface and it can only be as grounded as the infrastructure sitting underneath it. This is exactly why prepped for AI certification is so important. It isn't about making the AI smarter. It is about ensuring the architecture beneath the AI is sound enough that the reasoning stays reliable. You are auditing the semantic model and verifying the relationships. You are confirming the metadata and testing the security. You are formalizing the instructions. All of that hard work ensures that when Copilot reasons over your data, it stays aligned with your business reality. Monitoring and auditing Copilot predictions. When bridging co-pilot and power BI, let's look at what actually happens in the real world when organizations try to implement this. We aren't talking about the theory in a white paper but the actual practice of building these systems. You build your models, you connect co-pilot and then things start to break. When they do, there is almost always a specific pattern to the failure that you could have avoided. The first big mistake is assuming that raw data is enough for an LLM to understand. A team will often point co-pilot at a lake house full of raw tables and expect it to start making predictions immediately. They assume the AI will just know what each table represents and how they relate to each other but it won't. Co-pilot sees columns with cryptic names like CustDD or TransDD and has no idea what they actually mean in a business context. Without a defined schema or calculated measures, co-pilot will generate text that sounds plausible even though the numbers are completely wrong. The user ends up blaming the AI but the real problem is the architecture because they expected reasoning without providing any structure. The second mistake usually happens inside the data science team. A developer builds a predictive model in a notebook using raw tables from the data lake and the model trains and generates predictions perfectly. They surface those results to the business users but they realize too late that the model used a different definition of revenue than the official company reports. Your BIT might have a measure that excludes specific transaction types but the data scientists didn't know that. The predictions are mathematically correct but semantically useless because they are predicting against a definition that nobody in the company actually uses. You can fix this by building the model on top of semantic model measures using semantic link but teams often skip this step to move faster and pay for it later in confusion. The third mistake is treating metadata as optional documentation instead of critical infrastructure. A team might create a semantic model with clear table names but they leave out the descriptions and synonyms. They think co-pilot will just figure it out from the names but that is a dangerous assumption. If co-pilot sees a measure simply named total it has to guess if that means total revenue, total cost, or total transaction count that generic name creates ambiguity that leads to hallucinations. Every table needs a business definition and every measure needs an explanation of its scope. This isn't just bureaucracy, it is the operational infrastructure that makes your predictions reliable. The fourth mistake is what I call RLS configuration theater. A team sets up row-level security for their reports and verifies that a salesperson can only see their own region on the dashboard. They assume the security is handled but they never actually test what happens when co-pilot queries that say model directly. It often turns out that the RLS rules are too loose for an open-ended query interface. A user might ask co-pilot for a comparison across regions and while the filter applies, it might not work the way you expected in a conversational context. You have to test actual co-pilot queries against your RLS rules to ensure the security model covers every possible use case. The fifth mistake is exposing sensitive columns without using object-level security. A model might contain salary information that is necessary for calculating certain high-level metrics. The team uses RLS to restrict which employees each person can see and they think the data is safe. However, because they didn't use RLS to hide the salary column itself a user can ask co-pilot a seemingly innocent question that requires the AI to look at that hidden data. Co-pilot then constructs an answer that gives away compensation insights the user was never supposed to have. Hiding a field in a report UI is not real security, but RLS is because it removes the column from the model entirely for unauthorized users. The sixth mistake is releasing these predictive models without any versioning. A model gets built and added to the semantic model and users start relying on it for their daily work. Then the data science team retrains the model with new data and the performance changes. Suddenly users are getting different predictions and they have no idea why because there is no version tracking or change log. You should version your predictive models exactly like you would version a piece of software. Track what changed. Test the new version before you release it and make it clear to your users that the models can and will evolve over time. These mistakes are not just theoretical possibilities. They are the common pitfalls that happen when teams try to build co-pilot systems without respecting the underlying architecture.
