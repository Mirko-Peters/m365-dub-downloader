Why batteries turn instability into a self-inflicted attack? When a team sees a failed call, their first instinct is almost always to try again. It feels like the responsible thing to do, and in a single isolated process, it usually works. But in a distributed system, the math changes completely. Every retry you send is just more traffic aimed at a service that already proved it couldn't handle the first request. That instinct comes from an older, simpler way of thinking. You assume a packet dropped or a tiny network blip happened, and a second attempt will clear it up. That's fine for minor hiccups. But here is the problem. Overload isn't a hiccup. If a downstream service is already saturated, your retry doesn't fix anything. It just adds another request to an overflowing backlog, then another and then another. You aren't helping the dependency recover. You are actually increasing the pressure while it's already losing control. I started seeing this differently when I stopped looking at retries as safety logic and started seeing them as low-generation. One failed call with two extra attempts isn't just one failure. Across a single user journey, that might turn into three outbound requests. When you look at a whole service fleet under pressure, that pattern creates waves of duplicate demand that all chase the same limited capacity. Because most of your clients probably share the same retry policy, they all fire on the same schedule. They line up, they surge together, and they hit your weakest point all at once. In the dot net ecosystem, this gets dangerous fast because the tools make retries so easy to set up that you often forget they exist. You register an HTTP client, you attach a resilience policy and you feel like you're covered. But if that policy retries on timeouts or board exceptions while keeping a long timeout window, you have essentially built a pressure multiplier. The call lasts longer, the sockets stay busy and the upstream request lives longer. Then the retry kicks in and extends that whole chain even further. The nasty part is that this actually looks smart during a code review. The code seems careful and defensive because nobody wants to build a system that gives up too easily. But in reality, true resilience isn't about refusing to give up. It's about refusing to let a small failure turn into a massive one. This doesn't mean you should never use retries. They are great when a fault is brief, random, and cheap to test again. A temporary network interruption or a one-off connection hiccup fits that pattern perfectly. Short-bounded retries with jitter on idempotent operations are usually fine. The trouble starts when teams apply those same rules to an overloaded API, a slow database or an identity service that is already drowning. At that point, your retry logic stops being a recovery tool and starts being synchronized harassment. Leaders need to see this clearly because the damage shows up as both instability and pure waste. Your platform starts spending more on compute, bandwidth, and queue capacity just to push the same failing work through the system over and over. While everyone thinks the system is fighting to stay alive, it is actually accelerating its own collapse. The fix starts with classification. You have to ask what kind of failure you are actually seeing. Did the dependency reject the call quickly or did it time out? Is the latency climbing for everyone? Is the response a signal to back off? Or is it a sign that one more try might actually work? You should only retry when there is a real data-backed reason to believe the next attempt has a better shot than the last one. If you don't have that, you need to cut it off early. Once retries start spreading damage, your job isn't persistence anymore. It's containment. bulkhead isolation changes the system model. If retries are what spread the pressure, you need a way to stop one failing dependency from stealing resources that belong to something else. That is exactly what bulkhead isolation does. It isn't just a tuning trick or a small optimization. It is a hard rule about which parts of your system get to consume resources when everything is under stress. Most teams assume their services are already isolated because they live in different containers or different repos. They might even be managed by different teams. But that isn't real isolation if those services still share the same outbound connection limits, the same worker capacity or the same downstream bottleneck. The architecture looks separate on a diagram, but the failure path is exactly the same. So what is a bulkhead really? It's a boundary that limits the blast radius of a failure. When one dependency struggles, the rest of the service refuses to hand over all its time and capacity to save it. If one workload spikes, another workload still has the room it needs to run. That is the shift you have to make. You stop asking how to recover a specific call and start asking what resources you are willing to let burn with it. In a .NET system, this becomes a practical design choice very quickly. If you have an HTTP client talking to a fragile external API, you give it its own limits and its own timeout policy. You might even give it its own dedicated handler settings. If one background job is processing exports while another handles payments, you cannot let them drain the same execution path without guardrails. If a specific message consumer has the potential to flood your database, you have to split it away from the flow that supports your customers. The goal here isn't elegance, it's separation under pressure. One level deeper, you realize that bulkheads don't have to map directly to services. They can map to business value instead. You can isolate by dependency so a slow search provider can't drag down your authentication service. You can isolate by capability so a heavy reporting job can't starve the checkout process. You can even isolate by tenant class, so one noisy enterprise customer doesn't crowd out everyone else on the platform. You can also isolate by traffic importance to ensure user actions keep moving while sync jobs wait their turn. This is where things change because resilience stops being a generic tech concern and becomes an explicit business policy. Shared pools are usually where this whole model breaks down. A team might say their features are separate, but the requests still land in the same app instance and compete for the same outbound calls. They hit the same queue workers and fight for the same database connections. The moment latency starts to rise, separation on a diagram means very little in the real world. Under stress, the shared pool becomes the real system boundary and everything inside that pool has the power to hurt everything else. I've seen this happen in commerce systems where checkout, profile sync and reporting all looked independent during architecture reviews. Then one downstream dependency slowed down, which caused reporting jobs to stack up and profile updates to linger. Connection pressure climbed until checkout started missing its response targets. In that moment, nothing in the business wanted those workloads treated as equals, but the platform did it anyway because nobody drew a hard line around the resources. A better design admits something that feels a bit uncomfortable. Equal access is often the wrong rule. When your platform is healthy, broad sharing feels efficient and smart, but when the platform is stressed, that same sharing becomes a permission slip for low priority work to steal from high priority work. Architects have to choose early on what gets protected and what gets constrained. That might mean reserved consumer capacity, separate queues or per dependency concurrency limits. You might need independent connection budgets or dedicated compute paths for revenue critical operations. You don't do this because the system is dramatic. You do it because the system needs structure when behavior gets messy. This is also where technical design turns into leadership. Product and engineering leaders have to agree on which paths deserve guaranteed room and which ones can wait or fail. If nobody makes that call, the platform will end up making it for you by accident. Belkheads don't fix a bad dependency, but they do something much more useful. They stop that dependency from collecting unrelated victims. Once you have created that boundary, you still need a fast-weighted aside when a call should just stop entirely. Circuit breakers stop panic before it spreads. Isolation gives you boundaries, which is a good start, but a boundary still needs a decision rule to actually function. If a dependency keeps failing and your service keeps trying to talk to it, you have only slowed the damage instead of stopping it. That is the job of a circuit breaker. It watches a dependency, judges its recent behavior, and changes how calls flow based on what it sees in real time. Think of it less like error handling and more like traffic control. When the dependency behaves normally, the breaker stays closed and requests pass through without any friction. When failures cross a threshold in a given window, the breaker opens and stops sending more work for a while to let the system breathe. After that pause, it shifts into a limited test mode where a few calls get through to see if things have improved. If the dependency responds well enough, normal flow resumes but if not, the breaker opens again in the cycle repeats. The pattern is simple. Allow, stop, test, and recover. That sounds obvious, but most teams still run the opposite model in their production environments. They keep every call alive until a timeout finally proves the dependency is sick, which means every call learns the same lesson separately while holding on to resources the whole time. A breaker turns that painful process into shared memory for the entire service. Once enough evidence shows the dependency is failing, the platform stops pretending the next 100 calls deserve a full wait and just cuts them off. That fast rejection matters more than you might think. A quick failure is annoying for a user, but a slow failure is expensive for the platform and everyone using it. If the service can reject a request in a few milliseconds instead of waiting seconds for another doomed outbound call, it saves thread time, socket lifetime, and queue pressure. The user might get a fallback or a partial response or maybe they just get a clean error message, but the service itself stays capable of handling other work. And that is the trade you are making. You are not promising everybody a full answer all the time because you are choosing platform survival over graceful denial. In most incidents, that is the right choice to make for the health of the system. Users can usually tolerate a narrow feature failure far better than a broad system stall that locks up the entire interface. The tricky part sits in the tuning of these thresholds. If the threshold is too loose, the breaker reacts late and the service keeps wasting time on bad calls that we're never going to succeed. If the threshold is too tight, it opens on minor noise and blocks healthy traffic that should have gone through. The sample window matters just as much because a short window catches sharp failures fast but can flap if traffic is uneven. A long window smooths out the noise, but it might respond too slowly when a dependency drops hard and needs immediate isolation. This is why one breaker for everything usually fails to solve the problem. Dependencies behave differently, and an internal cache API is not the same as a third-party payment gateway. A token service is not the same as a reporting endpoint, so they deserve different thresholds, different timeout budgets, and different fallback rules. When teams attach one generic policy across all outbound calls, they flatten those differences and lose control at the exact moment they need it most. In .NET, this means you should scope policies per client and per dependency path instead of using one blanket setting. Your HTTP client for identity should carry rules that match identity and your client for search should carry rules that match search. Then you compose the pieces in the right order by starting with a timeout, adding a breaker, and finishing with a fallback where the contract allows it. The order shapes the behavior, and if you get it wrong, the policy stack will just fight itself. One more thing, trips teams up when they implement these patterns. They open the breaker, but the rest of the platform still rewards unsafe behavior by calling too aggressively from upstream. Cues keep feeding work without any back pressure, and product flows assume every dependency deserves a live-round trip every single time. The breaker helps a lot, but if the wider system treats degraded mode as an accident instead of a feature, pressure will just find another route to break things. The platform keeps spending scarce capacity on work that does not need to survive the incident, and once that happens, low value requests start competing with the parts that actually keep the business moving. A healthier model starts with service quality tiers. Not everything needs the same behavior when the system is under pressure and resources are running low.
