You start with a durable division of responsibilities, platform as contract, product as execution, governance as enforced intent, everything else is just the platform reflecting your org chart back at you. And it will not flatter you. Failure story, the CCOE that became a gatekeeper, a large financial services organization stood up a cloud center of excellence with the best possible intentions. Leadership intent was clean, centralized expertise, reduced risk, stopped teams from doing random things in Azure. Make it safe. So they staffed the CCOE with smart people, architects, security leads, a few engineers who had survived prior migrations, they wrote standards, they created templates, they built a review process, what actually happened was predictable. The CCOE became the human control plane. Every subscription request became a ticket, every network change became a meeting, every exception became a negotiation. And because the CCOE owned cloud, product teams stopped owning outcomes, they started owning escalation parts. The backlog didn't just grow, it metastasized because demand scales faster than centralized approvals. Then came the first real delivery deadline, a regulatory change, a customer facing feature, something the business actually cared about. Teams did what teams always do when they're blocked by a centralized gate, they went around it. A temporary subscription appeared under the root management group because someone had permissions they shouldn't have. A temporary service principle got owner because it was faster than waiting for PIM and access reviews. A temporary firewall exception got added because the hub team couldn't get to the request this week. And now the organization had two clouds, the governed one in the landing zone and the real one where delivery happened, nothing broke technically, everything broke systemically. The invisible constraint they ignored was simple, centralized approvals don't scale and they create a market for ungoverned workarounds. The CCOE didn't reduce risk, it concentrated decision making until teams had to bypass it to ship, that bypass became the default behavior and because it wasn't designed, it wasn't observable, security couldn't monitor consistently. Finance couldn't forecast reliably, audit couldn't explain the control narrative without apologizing, then the CCOE reacted the way approval boards always react more forms, more gates, more documentation, more friction, which increased bypass behavior. That loop feeds itself. The principle that would have prevented it is the same one as your landing zones were built for in the first place, paved roads. Self-service but constrained, automation but enforced. Subscription vending that creates the right management group placement, the right R-back, the right policy inheritance, the right logging and the right network integration by default, and a rule that's uncomfortable, but necessary. If a team can't get what they need through the paved road, the road is wrong. Fix the road, don't hand them a machete. The CCOE shouldn't be a gatekeeper, it should be a platform product team, a team with a backlog, clear service boundaries, and an explicit contract. Here's what you get by default, here's how your request changed, here's how exceptions expire, here's how we measure success. Because the only scalable governance model is the one where the safest path is also the easiest path. Everything else is conditional chaos with a ticket number. Closing synthesis, the enterprise migration mindset. Most organizations want a migration plan on what they actually need is a migration mindset, because migration is not a date you announce, it's not a checklist you complete, it's not a tooling decision you delegate. Migration is an operating model shift, it is a trust contract between central IT and delivery teams, central teams stop doing ad hoc approvals and instead enforce intent through design. Delivery teams stop improvising infrastructure and instead consume paved roads that make safe work routine. That distinction matters. If leadership frames migration as move workloads to Azure, the program will measure activity, how many apps moved, how many servers shut down, how many subscriptions exist, and then the business will still be surprised, surprised by audit findings, surprised by incidents, surprised by cost variance, surprised by the fact that cloud didn't magically create agility, because cloud doesn't create agility, govern systems create agility. So the mindset is simple and brutal, platform first, then factory, then modernization, you build the landings on contract, so intent is enforceable at scale, you build the migration factory, so delivery is repeatable and validated, not heroic. And only then do you modernize, selectively, where the platform can carry it and the business case is real. Along the way, you stop pretending every workload deserves investment. Some deserve replatforming because it buys operational leverage, some deserve re-hosting because stability matters more than ideology, some deserve refactoring because they're differentiators, and some deserve deletion, because your migration capacity is finite and entropy is expensive, and you track outcomes like an adult, time to market, how long from idea to production, risk instability, do incidents decrease and does recovery get faster, cost to serve predictability, can you forecast, spend by workload, or does it surprise you? Organizational throughput, how many teams can move safely in parallel without governance collapsing? Cloud migrations are justified by outcomes, not architectures. That's the executive framing that survives, budget reviews, leadership churn, and audit season. And this is the final thought that leaders need to internalize before they sign another migration mandate. The goal isn't to get to Azure. The goal is to build an enterprise that can change faster than its market. Treat migration as enterprise transformation. Platform first, then a migration factory, then modernization when maturity earns it.
