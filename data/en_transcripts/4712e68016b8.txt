Intent classification is the very first decision the router makes. When a query comes in, the system classifies what the user is actually looking for, whether it is a temporal, relational, or transactional request. The router has made its decision and subqueries are now firing across different systems. Now those systems need to return candidates that are both precise and semantically relevant. This is where the hybrid approach becomes essential. Think of your retrieval layer as having two parallel search mechanisms running at the same time, and each one has its own distinct strengths. BM25 is the probabilistic keyword search algorithm that has been the industry standard since the 90s, and it excels at precision. If you ask for documents mentioning a person by name, BM25 finds those exact matches instantly. You want records from March of 2005, or specific transaction amounts, or precise legal terminology BM25 owns these. It is deterministic and fast, but it only works at the surface level. If a document discusses the same concept using different vocabulary, BM25 is going to miss it. VectorSearch does the opposite work by embedding your query into a semantic space. It finds documents whose embeddings are closest in that space, which allows it to find conceptual similarity across different words. You might ask about financial transfers between parties, and VectorSearch will retrieve documents discussing money movement, even if they use words like payment or disbursement instead of transfer. But here's the problem. VectorSearch can be noisy. A document that is semantically similar to your query might be totally irrelevant to what you actually need, because embedding space distance does not always align with what a user wants. Combining them yields something neither provides a loan. You run BM25 against your corpus to get a ranked list of keyword matches, and simultaneously you run a VectorSearch to get a ranked list of semantic matches. These two lists almost never align perfectly. BM25 puts exact match documents at the top, while VectorSearch scatters results based on semantic proximity. The union of these two lists contains both precision and recall, the intersection contains documents that match both criteria, meaning they are exact matches and they are semantically coherent. But you cannot just stack the results. You have to fuse the rankings. Reciprocal rank fusion handles this. RRF takes the BM25 ranking and the Vector ranking, and merges them using a formula that avoids favoring one signal over the other. If a document ranks first in BM25 and tenth in VectorSearch, RRF gives it a high weight because it is important to both methods. If a document ranks second in VectorSearch but appears nowhere in BM25, RRF places it lower because semantic similarity without any keyword connection is a weaker signal. The fused ranking reflects where the two methods agree and where they complement each other. In practice, RRF produces better results than either method alone because it balances precision and recall. Metadata filtering happens before or after retrieval depending on your architecture. Before retrieval, you narrow the search space to make things more efficient. If a user query asks about the year 2005, you filter the indexes to only show documents with 2005 dates. This reduces the candidate set before any expensive operations run. After retrieval, you filter results by permission to ensure security. If a user does not have access to a specific document, the system removes it from the results. Metadata filtering at query time is essential for compliance and security but it does add latency. Prefiltering is faster because it reduces the total workload not because the logic is more sophisticated. Re-ranking applies after the hybrid fusion is complete. You have emerged candidate set ranked by RRF and now you pass this to a crossing-coder model. This is a lightweight transformer that reads the query and each candidate together to assign a new relevant score. The crossing-coder understands query document interaction better than embedding similarity alone, which allows it to catch small nuances. A document that is semantically closed but contextually wrong will drop in score while an exact match document that is slightly off topic gets re-weighted. The re-ranker produces the final ranking and your top candidates come from this list. The cost of all this is latency. RRF itself is fast because it is just arithmetic on two ranked lists and metadata filtering is fast because it uses simple comparison logic. But re-ranking hits a neural model which typically adds 100 to 150 milliseconds for a batch of 50 candidates. That is a meaningful delay. In aggregate your search call might take 150 to 300 milliseconds instead of the 30 to 50 milliseconds you would see with pure vector search. The payoff is precision. Precision gains compound as you move downstream. Fewer bad candidates reach the LLM which means fewer tokens are wasted on irrelevant context and there are fewer hallucinations. The marginal latency cost prevents much larger problems later. Permission aware retrieval at three and a half million pages you cannot afford to surface the wrong document to the wrong person. If a breach occurs and someone accesses data they should not have seen, the audits show exactly how it happened. You can see which user it was, what they asked, and what documents were returned to them. This logging happens at retrieval time, not generation time. The moment a chunk becomes a candidate, the system logs it. The implementation requires real infrastructure. You need identity and access management, permission metadata stores, and filtering logic at the retrieval stage. You also need audit logging pipelines. This is not a feature you can just bolt on at the end. It is foundational. Without it, your entire retrieval system is a compliance risk. With it, permission aware retrieval becomes invisible to the users. They see the answers they need, and the system quietly ensures those answers come from data. They are authorized to access. Chunking performance benchmarks. Theory is one thing, but real world performance is where the model actually breaks or holds up. Here is what the research actually shows. The moment you move from architectural design to actual implementation, benchmarks become your only source of truth. They tell you whether your choices were right or if you've built a system that fails under pressure. The question you have to answer is simple. How does your chunking strategy actually perform on the content you're trying to retrieve? We've discussed recursive structure aware chunking as the go-to approach for tier one and tier two content. This isn't just a theoretical preference or a hunch. The benchmark validation is important because it proves that this method is grounded in measured, repeatable performance. When you look at the data, fixed 512 token recursive chunking achieve 69% accuracy on document-level retrieval tasks. That is your baseline. If you ask 10 questions, recursive chunking finds the document containing the answer 9 times out of 10. The beauty of this method is that the chunking is clean. It respects section boundaries and preserves context, which means it maintains narrative continuity instead of shredding the document. When you ask about a specific legal provision, the retrieval process finds that section intact rather than giving you a fragmented mess. Compare that to semantic chunking on the same corpus. Semantic chunking scores 54% accuracy, which is 15% points lower than the recursive method. But the hidden cost is even more revealing. Semantic chunking produces chunks averaging only 43 tokens, and that is far too small for most enterprise needs. It's over-segmented because the boundary detection algorithm splits the text whenever semantic similarity drops even slightly. A new topic starts, so it creates a new chunk. A new speaker joins the dialogue, so it creates another new chunk. The result is a pile of small pieces instead of coherent units, forcing the system to pull multiple fragments just to reconstruct basic context. Your LLM receives scattered evidence instead of a continuous narrative. The latency consequence is direct and painful. More chunks mean more vector database operations, and more retrieval candidates mean more re-ranking candidates to process. A query that takes 30 milliseconds with recursive chunking might take 120 milliseconds with semantic chunking because the index is larger and the candidate set is bigger. When you spread this across thousands of queries, the cumulative difference is substantial enough to slow down the entire organization. Overlap impact gives us another lever to pull. The benchmark tested overlap at 0, and then at different percentages to see what changed. A chunking strategy with 0 overlap, where consecutive chunks are completely disjoint, yielded only baseline precision. However, adding a 64 token overlap, which is about 10% at 500 token targets, increased precision by 14.5%, that is a massive jump for a small change. Overlap prevents boundary loss because important context near a chunk edge might be missed if it falls just outside the window. With overlap, that context appears in two chunks, so if the query matches the boundary, you retrieve both and keep the context continuous. The cost is minimal since it only increases the chunk count slightly, and the precision gain more than justifies the extra tokens. One of the most crucial findings is that embedding quality matters more than chunking strategy when you're using models like GPT-4. You might assume that finding the perfect chunking strategy is the bottleneck, but in reality, it isn't. If your embedding model is weak, even perfect chunks will produce mediocre vectors that the system can't use effectively. If your embedding model is strong, the differences between chunking methods start to compress. A strong model can extract relevant signals from imperfect chunks, whereas a weaker chunk boundary is only a major problem when your retriever is also weak. This inverts how we usually think about precision. Don't waste time over-optimizing chunk boundaries if you're using powerful embedding models, and instead, invest that energy into the embedder itself. Cost comparison across these strategies shows the pragmatic trade-off you have to make. Semantic chunking adds 1.5 to 3 times more pre-processing overhead compared to fixed recursive chunking. That multiplier compounds quickly across 3.5 million pages, and you aren't just paying that fee once. You're paying that multiple across the entire ingestion pipeline every time data changes. For environments where you ingest data continuously, semantic pre-processing becomes a permanent bottleneck that slows everything down. For one time ingestion, the cost is concentrated, but it's still a significant hit to the budget. The practical recommendation from these benchmarks is straightforward. Start with fixed recursive chunking at 512 tokens with 10 to 15% overlap. Test that on your actual content and measure the retrieval precision and answer quality before changing anything. Only if you see clear failure modes, like consistent retrieval failures on a specific question type, should you layer on semantic refinement? Don't assume semantic is better just because it sounds more advanced. Measure the results and then decide. Latency and time to first token, TTFT. Speed matters and at a scale of 3.5 million pages, latency compounds until it breaks the user experience. You have to architect for speed from day one. Let's be concrete about what latency actually means in a system like this. Time to first token or TTFT is the gap between a user-hitting enter and the first word of the answer appearing on the screen. It is the only thing the user cares about when it comes to responsiveness. For a search interface, a sub-second TTFT feels instant, but two seconds feels slow, and five seconds feels completely broken. At enterprise scale, TTFT is the difference between a tool people actually use and a tool they learn to avoid. Your retrieval pipeline has many stages, and every single one of them consumes precious time. It starts with keyword search. BM25 against a well-indexed corpus usually runs in 10 to 50 milliseconds, depending on how complex the query is. A simple exact match query might hit the low end while a complex Boolean query might hit the high end, but this stage is rarely the bottleneck. Vector search also completes in tens of milliseconds for optimized indices when running in parallel. When you run both simultaneously through hybrid retrieval, neither blocks the other, and the first one to return simply feeds the results forward. Rewanking is where the latency really starts to accumulate. The crossing-coder model is a transformer that has to read the query and the candidate together to evaluate them. Rewanking 50 documents at typical lengths takes roughly 100 to 150 milliseconds. That is a meaningful delay, because it's three to five times slower than the initial retrieval. Your trading speed for precision here, and the decision to re-rank is purely architectural. Some systems skip it for applications where speed is everything, but others always include it because the precision gain prevents the LLM from hallucinating. For enterprise knowledge assistance, the 150 millisecond cost is usually acceptable because sending noisy candidates to the LLM creates much worse problems later on. Permission filtering adds even more latency to the re-ranking stage. Each candidate requires an access check to make sure the user is allowed to see it. You can mitigate this with batch operations by collecting 50 candidates and checking them all in a single authorization call. It's still overhead though, and 30 to 50 milliseconds for permission evaluation is normal for a full candidate set, then you have context shaping. Once the top candidates come back from retrieval, those raw chunks need to be assembled into a coherent context for the LLM. You might deduplicate the results if two chunks say the same thing, or you might expand the context to include surrounding sentences for better continuity. You might even order the results by date or relevance to make them more readable. This assembly process is fast and usually stays under 50 milliseconds, but it's still a cost you have to account for. The LLM call is the dominant stage of the entire process. Generating an answer from the context takes anywhere from 200 to 800 milliseconds depending on the model size and how long the answer is. A small model generating 100 tokens might take 200 milliseconds, but a large model generating 500 tokens will likely take 800. This is where most of your TTFT budget is spent. If you reduce the context you send to the LLM, you reduce the generation time, which is another reason why re-ranking is so important. Fueur, higher quality candidates, mean shorter context and shorter context means faster generation. Your total TTFT budget is typically between 500 and 1000 milliseconds for enterprise assistance. Anything under a second feels responsive to a human, but once you go beyond two seconds, users start getting impatient and lose focus. Caching is the best way to eliminate most of this latency for repeat queries. If a user asks the same question twice, you shouldn't be recomputing embeddings or re-running retrieval. You should cache the answer from the first query and return it instantly. For knowledge bases with common questions, caching absorbs a huge fraction of your traffic. A financial assistant might answer a question about a specific policy 50 times a month, so you should cache that answer after the first request. Every subsequent request will get that response in milliseconds instead of seconds. Parallel processing is how you hide latency under concurrent execution. While the initial retrieval is running, you can begin the embedding processing for the next step. While re-ranking is happening, you can start constructing the LLM prompt. Modern systems don't wait for one stage to finish before starting the next, and instead, they pipeline the operations. The elapsed time is the critical path through the pipeline rather than the sum of every single stage. A system where retrieval, permission checking and re-ranking run in a sequence might take 300 milliseconds, but that same system with parallel execution might only take 150 milliseconds because the operations overlap. The architectural lesson here is very straightforward. TTFT is a design constraint that drives every other decision you make. It drives chunking decisions because smaller chunks retrieve faster, and it drives re-ranking choices because lighter models trade precision for speed. It even drives your caching strategy by forcing you to decide which queries are worth pre-computing. When you're dealing with 3.5 million pages, you're making these choices constantly. If you get them right, users get a responsive system, but if you get them wrong, even the most perfect answers will feel too slow to be useful. Metadata enrichment and its impact. Research into retrieval systems shows us something that feels completely wrong at first, and that is the fact that metadata often matters more than your actual chunking strategy. You might have spent a fortune on your retrieval architecture by setting up recursive chunking hybrid search and complex entity graphs. But the data tells a different story because when you add rich metadata to your chunks, your accuracy goes up much faster than it does when you just try to refine your chunking boundaries. A chunk without metadata is just a fragment floating in space whereas a chunk with proper metadata becomes a searchable object with context, a location, and specific hooks for the system to grab onto. We can actually quantify this accuracy lift in a real way. If you don't use metadata enrichment, your question answer system is probably going to hit about 50 or 60% accuracy on difficult tasks. Users ask a question, the system finds some candidates, and the LLM tries to build an answer. But the problem is that the LLM has no anchors to hold onto. It has no way of knowing if a document is brand new or 10 years old, and it can't tell the difference between a casual mention and a primary source. Once you add metadata, that same system jumps to 75% accuracy, and that 15% gap is the difference between a tool people hate and one they actually trust. The way this works is actually very simple because the metadata just travels right alongside the content. When you pull a document into the system, you extract structured details like the document type, the creation date, and the specific jurisdiction it belongs to. If you were looking at something like the Epstein files, your metadata would include the name of the person testifying, the case references, and specific topic tags. Every single chunk carries these details with it, which means they become part of the index and stay available every time a user runs a search. When a query happens, metadata serves as both a filter and a way to rank the results. If a user asks about something that happened in 2005, the metadata filter immediately shrinks the search space, so the system only looks at chunks tagged with that specific year. This makes the search much faster because you aren't running expensive operations on files that don't matter, but metadata does more than just filter it also helps with ranking, because it can tell the difference between a primary source from 2005 and a summary written in 2015 that just happens to mention that year. The quality of your metadata depends entirely on how you extract it. Some of these fields are easy to get because things like creation dates and author names are usually embedded right in the file properties. You can use simple rules to pull that data with high confidence, but other fields require a bit more intelligence to get right. You might need a machine learning classifier to decide if a document is actually about a specific topic or if it just mentions it once. The system then flags which pieces of data are rule-based and which ones are predicted, so the user knows exactly how much they should trust the result. Keeping the system running is a real challenge because metadata is never static. Documents get updated, sensitivity levels change, and old policies expire, which means a file marked as internal today might be public by tomorrow morning. Your enrichment pipeline cannot just stop after the initial ingestion, so you need a scheduled process to review your documents every month or quarter to refresh those tags. If a new version of a file comes out, the old metadata needs to be marked as deprecated immediately. This ongoing maintenance might seem expensive, but it is much better than the alternative, which is having your system return outdated information because the metadata drifted over time. Setting this up does add some overhead to your infrastructure. You have to build the extraction logic, find a place to store the fields in your database, and write the ranking logic for the retrieval step, but this investment pays off on every single query the system handles. Unlike chunking tweaks that only help specific types of questions, metadata enrichment makes the entire system smarter and more reliable for every user. Handling multimodal queries. Users are going to ask questions that point to both documents and videos at the same time. Your system has to be able to handle that without splitting the search into separate buckets that don't talk to each other. The real difficulty here is detecting what the user actually wants. When someone asks a question, the system has to figure out if they need a video, a document, or both. A phrase like "Show me what happened in the meeting" is tricky because it could mean the user wants the transcript, the handwritten notes, or the actual video clip of the event. The system cannot guess, so it has to use query understanding to figure out the multimodal intent behind the words. This intent detection happens very early in the process. A lightweight classifier looks at the question and tags it based on what it finds, such as looking for temporal words like "During the meeting" to trigger a transcript search. If the user says "How does it look?" The system flags the query for image or video retrieval instead. These tags aren't exclusive, so a single query can have multiple signals that tell the system to route the search to several different places at once. The embedding challenge goes even deeper than that. Most models only work with text, but your library now includes video transcripts and image descriptions that all have different semantic properties. An image description might say "Two people in a room" while a transcript says "Person A and Person B met" and a document might just call it a formal meeting. A standard text model would see these as three different things even though they are all describing the exact same moment in time. Multimodal embeddings fix this by putting different types of media into one shared space. These models take text, images, and video data and turn them into vectors that you can actually compare against each other. A multimodal model understands that a picture of a meeting and a document about that meeting belong in the same semantic neighborhood. When you embed a user's query, it lands in that same neighborhood, allowing the system to pull candidates from every modality and rank them together in one list. Cross-modal retrieval is what allows the system to find a video that visualizes a concept found in a document. If a user asks about a specific person's involvement in a business deal, the system pulls the contracts, the video of their testimony, and even photos of the checks involved. The user gets a complete picture of the event because they have the textual evidence, the first-hand video, and the visual proof all in one place. To keep things precise, you have to make sure the transcript alignment is perfect. When the system finds a video transcript, it shouldn't just hand over a giant block of text but should instead preserve the timestamps and speaker names. When a user sees a result, they should see exactly when the person started talking, along with a direct link to that moment in the video. This bridges the gap between searching through text and watching the media, giving the user the speed of a search engine with the context of a film. Visual grounding is the process of returning different types of evidence together. If a user is looking for details on a transaction, the system should show them the financial spreadsheet and a photo of the sign check at the same time. The document gives them the data they need while the image provides the verification they want. Neither of these is as strong on its own as they are when you present them together. The final step is a fusion strategy that merges all these parallel parts before the final ranking happens. Text, video and image searches all run at the same time and then the fusion logic combines the results into one set. You can use reciprocal rank fusion to make sure the modalities are competing fairly against each other. If a result shows up as a strong signal in both the text and the video, it gets boosted to the top of the list while weaker signals naturally fall toward the bottom. In the end, the system gives the user one unified list of results. They don't have to click through different tabs for videos and documents because everything is interspersed based on how relevant it is. This allows the LLM to take all that mixed evidence and turn it into a single coherent answer that is grounded in every piece of data you have. The orchestration layer, putting it together. All these pieces, chunking, routing, retrieval and re-ranking, have to work together and that is where the orchestration layer comes in. Think of your architecture not as a bunch of isolated parts but as an integrated system where every single piece triggers the next one. At the very center of everything sits the orchestration layer and its only job is to coordinate the flow of information. Data flows in one direction during ingestion while queries flow in another direction from retrieval to generation. The orchestration layer makes sure that data lands in the right place at the right time and it ensures that user queries get routed efficiently through the systems you've prepared. The ingestion pipeline works as a sequence, starting when raw documents arrive at the front door. You might have 3.5 million pages, plus videos and images and they all enter the system as raw bytes. The first stage is extraction where PDFs are passed, images go through OCR and videos are transcribed into text. This extraction happens in parallel so you don't have to wait for document one to finish before you start processing document two. Extraction runs on a cluster that can process thousands of documents at the same time and as that work completes, the documents flow into the chunking stage. The chunking process follows the recursive structure-aware hierarchy we talked about which produces chunks that actually align with the document architecture. From there, chunks flow into enrichment where named entity recognition runs and temporal metadata is pulled out. Sensitivity labels are applied to keep things secure and then the chunks flow into the embedding stage. Your embedding model processes them in batches to produce vectors and then those vectors and metadata flow into storage. The vector database receives the vectors, the keyword index takes the text and the knowledge graph receives the entity relationships. Chunks land in specific tiers based on how you've classified their value and while all of this is happening, audit logs record every single step. Document fingerprints are computed to stop duplicates from getting in, progress is tracked and any failures are logged so they can be retried automatically. This entire pipeline is asynchronous, which means the different stages don't block each other from moving forward. While extraction is busy processing batch 10, the chunking stage is already working on batch 8 and enrichment is handling batch 6. The pipeline works like a conveyor belt where every stage feeds the next one and this makes bottlenecks immediately visible to the team. If embedding becomes a constraint, you scale the embedding service and if storage is slow, you focus on optimizing your rights. The orchestration layer monitors the throughput at every single stage and shows you exactly where the system needs to be improved. The query pipeline is just as sequential but it also involves a lot of branching logic. A user submits a question and the system immediately roots it through intent classification to see what they're actually asking. Intent tags trigger different retrieval strategies and the orchestration layer receives these tags to decide which retrieval systems it needs to call. A query about dates and specific people might trigger the knowledge graph, document search and metadata filtering all at once. On the other hand, a simple structural query might only need to invoke BM25. These decisions are deterministic based on the intent so they don't require any human intervention to move forward. The parallel retrieval paths fire off at the same time and as results come back from each path, the orchestration layer collects them. Results are merged using RRF or another fusion strategy and then the RRF looks at the entire merged candidate set. As soon as RRF is done, permission filtering kicks in and the orchestration layer trims the results based on who the user is and what they're allowed to see. The trimmed results feed into context shaping where the system assembles the top K candidates into a coherent block of text for the LLM. Redundancy is stripped out, temporal ordering is applied and narrative continuity is preserved so the model doesn't get confused. The shaped context flows to the LLM, the model generates an answer and the orchestration layer logs that answer alongside the specific chunks used to ground it for auditing. Feedback loops are what finally close the system and make it smarter over time. Users interact with the answers they get providing thumbs up or thumbs down ratings to let you know if the system is working. They might correct a hallucinational ask a follow-up question and all of those signals flow back to the orchestration layer. High confidence correct answers are cached to save money later while failed retrievals are flagged so an engineer can investigate what went wrong. Systematic biases like certain question types always returning bad results are surfaced to the engineering teams for a fix. The orchestration layer aggregates all of this feedback and uses it to trigger retraining for the models. Thresholds are adjusted, rooting heuristics get better and query classifiers are retrained on the actual logs of what users asked and what happened. User behavior is essentially teaching the system how to evolve without you having to hard code every change. Monitoring is the eyes and ears of the entire operation. The orchestration layer instruments every stage with metrics like retrieval precision, answer accuracy, latency and the cost of every single query. These metrics flow into a dedicated store and dashboards visualize the health of the system in real time. Alerts fire, the moment performance starts to degrade which allows you to react before users notice a problem. If latency suddenly spikes, you can drill down to see exactly which stage is causing the delay. If precision drops, you can identify which categories of questions are failing and why. If the cost per query starts rising, you can detect which operations became expensive and optimize them. This level of observability allows for rapid diagnosis and prevents the kind of silent failures that kill trust in a system. Scaling happens in two different dimensions to keep up with demand. Horizontal scaling adds raw capacity by spinning up more instances of the retrieval service, more embedding workers or more re-ranca replicas. Load is distributed across these resources and the total throughput of the system increases. Vertical scaling is more about optimizing performance like switching to a smaller, faster embedding model or a more efficient vector database. This makes the system leaner and latency decreases as a result. The orchestration layer manages all of this scaling by rooting traffic only to healthy instances. It can gradually drain capacity from all deployments and move it to new ones without dropping a single request. It monitors how resources are being used and triggers auto-scaling the moment-specific thresholds are reached. The system adapts to the demand on its own and that's the power of orchestration. It isn't just a collection of parts, it's a cohesive system where every part serves the whole. Data flows, queries get answered and feedback makes the whole thing better over time. The orchestration layer is what makes 3.5 million pages actually useful because without it you just have a pile of disconnected pieces. Cost analysis, semantic versus fixed chunking at scale, budget matters more than people realize and when you're dealing with 3.5 million pages, the cost difference between chunking strategies is massive. You aren't just paying a one-time fee to set this up because you're actually paying every single day the system is running, every page you ingest costs money and every query a user sends triggers more costs. These expenses compound across millions of operations and a tiny overhead multiplier that looks small on paper becomes a huge line item in your budget. It all starts with the preprocessing stage. When you chunk 3.5 million pages using fixed recursive chunking, the work is linear and predictable. You tokenize the text, you split it at the boundaries you've set and you move on to the next page. The computational cost for each page is basically a constant which means you can parallelize the work across a cluster very easily. A thousand machines can process a thousand pages at the same time and the whole preprocessing job can be finished in a few hours. When you switch to semantic chunking, the work becomes fundamentally different and much more expensive. You can't just split at boundaries anymore because now you have to compute embeddings for every single sentence. You have to calculate similarity scores between those sentences and detect exactly where those scores drop below a certain threshold. You merge small segments, recompute the embeddings at the segment level and the whole process requires multiple passes through neural models. A single page might require embedding every sentence individually just to make a decision about where a chunk should end. A cost multiplier of 1.5 to 3 times is actually a conservative estimate for this. Some setups pay even more than that depending on how sophisticated the logic is. Embedding costs scaled directly with the number of chunks you create. Your vector database is going to bill you based on how many vectors you're storing in the index. If semantic chunking produces a lot of small chunks, which it usually does because it breaks things down by topic, you end up with more total chunks from the same content. More chunks means more embeddings to generate and that leads to higher costs during ingestion and higher storage fees every month. At a scale of 3.5 million pages, even a small increase in the average chunk count has real financial consequences. If semantic chunking increases your chunk count by 20%, you're paying 20% more for your embeddings and your vector storage. That is a material difference that adds up fast. When you multiply that by your model costs and your database subscription tier, the difference becomes very visible to whoever is paying the bills. Storage costs only make this effect worse over time. Vector databases usually charge based on the number of vectors you have stored and some of them charge per million vectors every month. Regardless of how they price it, more vectors always mean a higher bill at the end of the month. A recursive fixed chunking strategy at 512 tokens might produce 1 million chunks from your data. Semantic chunking might produce 1.2 or even 1.5 million chunks from that same data. Every single one of those extra vectors has to be stored, indexed and searched. The storage multiplier is direct, so more vectors lead to a higher bill every single time. Query time costs are another factor that people often overlook. When you search a larger index with more candidates, the retrieval process takes longer and uses more compute. Your vector database has to evaluate more distances and your keyword index has more postings to look through. Your re-rancker also has to evaluate more candidates before it can produce the final rankings for the user. Rewanking 50 candidates is much cheaper than re-ranking 150 candidates. If semantic chunking creates smaller chunks, you'll have to retrieve more of them just to reconstruct the context for the answer. You end up re-ranking more and passing more data through the system, which makes every query incrementally more expensive. The cost of the LLM context is less obvious, but it's very consequential for your bottom line. Better chunking is supposed to reduce token waste, but it can actually do the opposite if you aren't careful. If your chunks are larger and more coherent, you might only need to pass three of them to the LLM to get a good answer. If your chunks are fragmented into tiny pieces, you might have to pass eight chunks to capture the same amount of information. LLM costs scale with how many tokens you consume in the prompt. Eight chunks of 200 tokens each adds up to 1600 tokens, while three chunks of 500 tokens is only 1500. In this case, the semantic chunking scenario uses more tokens to provide the exact same information. These API costs compound across millions of queries, and while the difference isn't a disaster for one query, it's significant for the whole operation. A break-even analysis will show you when semantic chunking actually justifies the extra money. For general document retrieval, the break-even point almost never arrives because the overhead costs are higher than the quality improvements. However, for high-value domains like complex legal documents or regulatory filings, the extra cost can be worth it. When the precision of the answer is worth more than the pre-processing expense, then semantic approaches become a lot easier to defend. For 3.5 million pages of mixed content, you might apply semantic chunking to the top 10% of your most important files. This hybrid approach gives you the precision where it matters most while keeping costs down across the rest of the corpus. The lesson here is to be pragmatic with your budget. Start with fixed recursive chunking and measure exactly what you're spending. You should only invest in semantic refinement when you can prove that the gain in precision is worth the extra expense. Governance and compliance at scale. When you are managing 3.5 million pages, governance isn't just a nice feature to have. It is the absolute foundation of the system. The retrieval tools might work and the AI might generate great answers, but without governance, you are leaving yourself wide open to massive exposure. At this kind of volume, a single policy mistake can leak thousands of sensitive documents and a mist legal hold can literally destroy evidence needed for court. Governance is the only thing that stops these small errors from turning into total system failures. Data classification has to start long before the retrieval process even begins. Every single document that enters your system gets sorted into a specific tier, ranging from public and internal to confidential or restricted. Public files can be shared with anyone and kept forever, while internal documents are strictly for the organization and can never be shared outside the company. Confidential files require a specific need to know clearance. And restricted documents are tied to legal holds or regulations that require very specific handling rules. This classification isn't just a label for show because it actually dictates how the entire system behaves. If a document is marked restricted, the system won't let you delete it without legal sign-off and confidential files won't even show up in a search unless the user has been granted explicit access. Retention policies are what define the life cycle of your data. You have to decide how long a document stays in your active index, which might be years for a legal case or just three years for a standard business memo. Certain industries have strict mandates like keeping financial records for seven years or employment files for five, but retention isn't just about hitting a delete button. It is about transitions where active documents stay in fast access tiers, while older files move to cheaper, slower storage with reduced search ability. Your policies encode this entire journey. So when a document hits its expiration date, the system moves it automatically. Users can't find these archived files through a normal copilot search and getting to them requires special permission and a full-ordered trail. Legal holds will always override your standard retention rules. During a lawsuit or an investigation, certain documents become immutable, meaning they cannot be moved, changed or deleted, no matter what the original policy said. Once the legal department issues a hold, the system tags those files, and they stay frozen even if they were scheduled for deletion that very day. Normal retention only starts back up once the hold is officially lifted. If you don't have this logic built in, you run the risk of destroying evidence during a routine cleanup, but a good system lets litigation needs and daily compliance live together without any conflict. Every time a user asks a question, the system logs, who made the request which documents were pulled, and whether the user actually opened them or just saw them in a summary. Every AI response is tracked to show which documents were used as context and if the answer cited them correctly. We also log every change in classification and every time access is granted or denied, but these logs aren't there to help with speed or performance. They exist for accountability, giving you a clear timeline if a breach happens, or if a regulator asks how you handle specific data. These logs are permanent and cannot be deleted by users, and we actually keep them longer than the source documents to prove we stayed compliant. Your retrieval system has to respect these lines, so if a US user tries to process EU data, the system won't pull from a US index. Instead, the orchestration layer transparently routes that request to a compliant region in Europe. Because every document has residency tags built into its classification, the routing happens automatically without the user ever needing to think about it. Incident response is your way of catching a breach before it spreads. If a user suddenly tries to pull thousands of documents they've never looked at before, the system triggers an immediate alert. We also have detection tools that watch for sensitive data, appearing in answers where it shouldn't be, or for users logging in from unexpected regions to access restricted files. These detection systems feed directly into response workflows, so your security team can investigate and fix the problem. Once the issue is resolved, we go back and adjust the preventive policies to make sure it doesn't happen again. When governance is working the way it should, it stays completely invisible to the end user. The people using the system just see the answers they need, while the back end quietly makes sure those answers follow the law, respect retention dates, and keep a perfect audit trail. The goal is a system that doesn't just fail silently when a policy is broken, but instead escalates the issue, so it can be handled properly. Real-world implementation, the workflow, theory is great for planning, but you need to know how this actually looks when you put it into practice. When you are standing up a system to manage 3.5 million pages, you have to follow a very specific execution plan. The timeline is vital because every phase is designed to prove your architecture works before you try to scale it up to the full corpus. Day one is all about ingestion and extraction. You start with a massive data dump of 3.5 million pages, including everything from PDFs and emails to old scanned images and video files. The ingestion pipeline starts moving immediately, and we run the extraction process at the same time to break those files down. PDF passes pull out the text. OCR technology turns scanned images into readable data, and we catalog all the metadata for every video and image file. We aren't looking for perfection on the first day, so it is fine if the OCR misses a few characters or a parser mislabel the section. The goal is to get coverage across the entire dataset, while a background process identifies names, dates, and organisations. By the time the sun goes down on day one, you have a raw version of your content registered in the system and ready for the next step. Days 2 through 7 are dedicated to indexing. All that extracted content goes through a chunking process that respects the natural hierarchy of the documents, and we tag those chunks with the entities and sensitivity levels we found earlier. The system starts filling up the tier one and tier two indexes, giving high value files, like legal filings, full semantic indexing, with vector embeddings. At the same time, the knowledge graph starts building connections between people and dates, while less important content goes into keyword-based storage. You don't have to wait for the whole thing to finish to start testing, so you can run sample queries to see if you can find documents by name or topic. These early tests tell you if your chunking is working, and if the system is respecting the structure of your data. Week 2 is when you finally deploy your agents. The agentech router is the brain of the system that understands what a user wants and breaks their query down into smaller parts. This is the moment where your design meets the real world, and you start feeding the system complex questions, like asking for all emails between two specific people from the year 2005. The router has to classify that request, generate subqueries and pull data from both the knowledge graph and the document index. Once the results come back, the router merges them into a single answer, and you have to manually check if that answer is actually right. This phase usually reveals where your assumptions were wrong, like a query going to the wrong index, which allows you to iterate and fix the biggest problems before moving on. Week 3 is when you turn on re-ranking. We bring the crossing-coder model online to sharpen the precision of the results, but you have to keep a close eye on how much this slows things down. If your basic search took 30 milliseconds and re-ranking bumps it up to 150, you have to decide if that extra time is worth it. In most cases, a 40% jump-in precision is a fair trade for an extra 120 milliseconds of wait time. If the delay is too long for your users, you can always shrink the number of documents you are re-ranking or switch to a smaller, faster model. Week 4 is the pilot rollout. You don't give the system to everyone at once, but instead you hand it to a small group like the legal team who really knows the data. They start running real-world queries and giving you feedback, hitting a thumbs-up for good answers and correcting the system when it misses the mark. This feedback goes straight into your monitoring tools so you can see exactly where the system is struggling. You might find that time-based questions work perfectly while relationship queries are missing some connections, but you don't rush to change things yet. You take the time to observe and understand these failure patterns before you start tweaking the settings. Ongoing operations are all about constant monitoring and small adjustments. You have to track your latency metrics every day because if the system starts slowing down, you need to know if the index is getting too big or if the cache isn't working. We also keep a close eye on the cost per query to make sure a new feature hasn't made the system inefficient. Quality is measured against a set of gold standard questions, so if the precision starts to slip, you know exactly which part of the retrieval or ranking process needs work. The system evolves slowly, getting better with every interaction and every correction until it becomes a truly reliable tool. Common failure modes and how to avoid them, even with a solid architecture, things can break and usually they break in predictable ways. Overfragmentation is a silent killer for usability. When you create chunks that are too small, say under 200 tokens, you force the retrieval system to pull dozens of fragments just to reconstruct one coherent thought. Imagine a legal deposition that spans 300 tokens but gets split into four separate chunks. If a user asks a question that matches only the second chunk, the system retrieves it, but the context is gone. The witness statement leading up to that testimony is sitting in a different chunk and the follow-up question is in another one entirely. What the LLM receives is a fragmented mosaic instead of a continuous narrative which leads it to hallucinate connections or miss the nuance of the conversation. Users will notice that the answers feel scattered and incomplete. On the other hand, chunks that are too large create massive amounts of retrieval noise. A 5,000 token chunk covering an entire legal section might contain a few core facts mixed with pages of tangential discussion. When this gets retrieved, it swamps the context window with irrelevant details and the LLM struggles to find the needle in the haystack. Both of these extremes will break your downstream performance. The only way to prevent this is through constant measurement. You need to monitor your chunk size distribution and calculate exactly what percentage of your data falls below 200 tokens or exceeds 2000. When you see the distribution skewing towards these extremes, you have to adjust the minimum and maximum bounds. The goal isn't to make every chunk identical, but you need enough consistency to prevent these pathological cases from ruining the user experience. Permission misalignment is a much more dangerous problem because it's a silent security breach. You might build a robust retrieval system, but if the permission metadata attached to your chunks becomes stale, you have a major liability. Consider a situation where a user changes teams and their access profile is updated in your identity system. If the retrieval system hasn't revalidated those permissions against the updated identity, the system is flying blind. The user runs a query and the retrieval engine returns a chunk they no longer have the right to see. Because the permission check ran against cashed, outdated credentials, the restricted information passes through without being filtered. This is actually worse than a total system failure because it isn't visible to the admins. To prevent this, you need a strict synchronization discipline where permission metadata is refreshed on a regular schedule. Depending on how often people move roles in your company, this should happen daily or even weekly. When access policies change in your primary identity system, those changes must flow to the retrieval cash immediately. You should never rely on cashed permissions for more than a few hours at a time. It's also vital to test these boundaries continuously by running synthetic queries as a restricted user. You need to verify that those restricted chunks are actually being filtered out in real time. Don't just assume the permission system is working. You have to validate it. Stale metadata causes your system to drift away from reality over time. During the initial ingestion, your enrichment pipelines extract dates, assign classifications, and map out relationships between entities. But documents are living things that evolve. A file might change from confidential to internal or a document might be superseded by a newer version without the old one being deleted. Sometimes a legal hold expires, but nobody remembers to update the metadata tags. These small errors accumulate until your retrieval system is operating on a mountain of outdated information. Users start seeing results ordered by the wrong dates and classifications no longer match current company policy. Because this degradation happens gradually, it's easy to miss until the system is significantly compromised. The fix is a scheduled refreshment of your data. Major fields like classification, retention status, and legal hold states need to be revalidated periodically. At least once a quarter, you should scan the entire corpus and compare the metadata against your current policies. When you find a mismatch, you update it. For high-velocity fields that change frequently, you'll need to increase that frequency while quarterly checks are usually enough for stable fields like document types. You should also set up automated alerts for any document whose metadata hasn't been touched in longer than expected. Rooting errors are another common pitfall where queries get sent to the wrong specialized systems. This happens when the intent classifier misses the signals in a user's question. For example, a query about complex financial relationships might get routed to a standard document search instead of a graph database. As a result, the answer misses all the structured relationship data it needs, or perhaps a temporal query gets sent to a keyword search instead of a time indexed system leading to results from the wrong years. These failures often go unnoticed because the system still returns results. They just aren't the right ones. To stop this, you need a continuous validation loop. You should maintain a dedicated test set of queries where the correct routing is already known and you should run these tests every single day. If the routing classification starts to shift, you need to investigate why. If certain types of questions are consistently failing, it's time to retrain your classifier. You should also monitor routing decisions alongside the actual retrieval outcomes. When you see that queries matching a specific pattern are consistently producing poor results, it's a clear sign that your routing logic is broken. Rewanking collapse occurs when your model becomes miscalibrated. If you have an aggressive reranca, it might assign incredibly low scores to perfectly legitimate candidates, leaving your top results empty. Conversely, a conservative reranca might barely change the order of the initial results at all. In that case, the reranking process is just adding latency to the system without actually improving the precision of the answers. You prevent this through empirical tuning. You have to test the reranca against a sample of real queries and measure exactly how much it's changing the order of the results. You need to validate that these changes are actually making the answers more accurate. If the reranca is moving things around for no reason or having almost no impact, you need to adjust your thresholds or change the size of your candidate set. Finally, there is the issue of latency creep, which comes from a dozen small optimizations piling up. Each new feature might only add 10 milliseconds but then the reranca adds 150 and the permission checks add another 30. Before you know it, the total latency has climbed to 300 milliseconds. Then someone adds query expansion or extra caching overhead and suddenly the system feels sluggish. You have to be ruthless about monitoring these numbers. Every single component in your pipeline should report its own latency and you need dashboards that show the cumulative total. When that total hits your budget ceiling, you have to make a choice. If you want to add a new feature, you have to find a way to remove or optimize an old one to keep the system responsive. Monitoring, evaluation, and continuous improvement. The reality is that you cannot optimize what you aren't measuring and this is exactly where most rag implementations fall apart. The system might be running and users might be getting answers but without proper instrumentation, you are flying blind. You won't know if the precision is slowly drifting downward or if a recent update made the latency unbearable for your users. You won't even know if your cost per query is starting to spike. Measurement is the only thing that stands between a system that degrades in the dark and one that actually gets better over time. Retrieval metrics are your first line of defense because they tell you if the pipeline is finding the right candidates. You should be looking at precision at K which measures how many of your top retrieved results are actually relevant. If you pull the top 50 candidates but only 35 of them matter, your precision is sitting at 70%. You also need to track recoloured K to see if you're capturing all the relevant documents available in the corpus. If there are 100 matching documents but you only find 40 of them, your recall is failing. These metrics help you diagnose the specific flavor of your quality issues. You might have high recall but low precision, meaning you're finding the right info but drowning it in noise. Or you might have the opposite problem where what you find is accurate but you're missing the bigger picture. You should also use NDCG to measure the quality of your ranking. A relevant document that shows up in the first slot is much more valuable than one buried at number 50, and NDCG captures that difference. Mean reciprocal rank is another useful tool that tells you how far down the list a user has to go to find their first real answer. These aren't just academic numbers. They correlate directly with how happy your users are. When precision is high, users don't have to deal with false leads. When recall is high, they don't miss critical evidence. The quality of your ranking is what determines if the system feels like a shortcut or a chore. Answer quality metrics are what your users actually care about at the end of the day. You need to measure faithfulness, which asks if the answer stays true to the source documents. An unfaithful answer is one that contradicts the evidence or starts guessing. Then there is relevance, which checks if the system actually answered the specific question the user asked. You also need to look at grounding. Can every single factual claim in the response be traced back to a specific source chunk Citation accuracy is just as important because you need to know if the links actually support the claims they're attached to. Since these are qualitative metrics, you can't just calculate them with a simple algorithm. They require human judgment or expert review. The best approach is to build a gold standard evaluation set of about 500 representative questions with verified answers and citations. You run your system against this set and have experts score the results. By tracking these scores over time, you can see exactly when a change in the pipeline helps or hurts the final output. Latency metrics need to be broken down by every single component in the chain. You should track the time to the first byte so you know when the user first sees text on their screen. You need to measure the time from the initial query to the moment retrieval is finished and then the time from retrieval to the first LLM token. It's important to remember that percentile latency matters much more than the average. Your average response time might look greater at 200 milliseconds, but if your P99 is five seconds, one percent of your users are having a terrible experience. You should track PFT, P95 and P99 latencies religiously. When the P99 starts to lag, you need to find out why. Is a specific type of query slow? Is one of your subsystems hitting a resource limit? Percentiles will show you the failures that average is high. Cost metrics are how you track the actual business impact of the system. You should calculate the cost per query by dividing your total infrastructure spend by the number of queries you serve. If you're spending $50,000 a month to serve a million queries, you're looking at five cents per query. But an even better metric is the cost per successful answer. Not every query produces a good result, and only the successful ones actually provide value. This metric tells you if your system is becoming more efficient over time. You should also break down your infrastructure costs by component, looking at retrieval, re-ranking, LLM tokens and storage. If the total cost starts to climb, you can drill down to see if the LLM is consuming too many tokens, or if your storage costs are scaling poorly. User feedback is the final piece of the puzzle that closes the loop. Simple thumbs up or down ratings can tell you if the answers were useful in the real world. You can also look at escalation rates to see how often users have to give up and ask a human for help. When those rates go up, it's a clear signal that the system is hitting a wall. You should make it as easy as possible for users to provide this feedback directly in the interface. When users rate thousands of answers, that data becomes a gold mine for future improvements. Finally, you need to move toward automated offline evaluation. Instead of checking things manually, your system should run a benchmark set of questions against your live indexes every single night. It should compute precision, recall, and NDCG automatically and compare them to your baseline. This allows you to detect regressions before a single user ever sees them. This kind of discipline is what separates a project that slowly falls apart from a professional system that catches problems and fixes them before they become disasters. Future directions. Beyond 3.5 million pages. The Epstein files case shows us what happens when you build with intention at a massive scale. But here's the problem. The architecture you build today is just the foundation for tomorrow's headaches. Those constraints that drove our design, the 3.5 million pages, the messy multimodal files, the strict permissions, those aren't going to stay the same. Data grows, requirements shift, new tech shows up. It is not a question of if your system needs to change, but how you plan for that change right now. Agente workflows are the next step in this journey. Up until now, retrieval has been a simple operation. You ask a question, the system finds some text. The model reads it and gives you an answer, but sophisticated users don't actually want answers. They want reasoning. They want to compare a witness's testimony across three different depositions to find where they lied. That isn't just finding text and repeating it. That is a process of finding, synthesizing, comparing, and judging. An agentic system treats every one of those steps as its own job. The agent pulls the first deposition. Then it pulls the second and third. It runs them through a specific tool designed to find contradictions. It thinks about what that means for the witness's credibility. This requires agents that can use multiple tools in a row, check their own work, and decide what to do next. Current systems do this a little bit, but in the future, this kind of reasoning will be the default. You need to build your architecture today so that retrieval is a tool an agent can call, rather than a closed box that nobody can touch. We are also seeing a shift toward graph-based drag. This moves us away from simple, math-based similarity and toward actual relationships. Your knowledge graph today maps people and how they are connected. Tomorrow, that graph becomes the main way you find information. Instead of looking for similar words, the system treats your question like a map. If you ask what transactions connected person X to organization Y between 2005 and 2007, that isn't a text search, it is a graph traversal. The system starts at the person, follows the money trails, filters by the dates, and shows you the path. This means you have to start investing in graph infrastructure like property graphs and relationship engines right now. This doesn't mean we stop using vector search. It means we use both. If your data is all about relationships, you use the graph. If it is all about heavy text, you use vectors. The system just learns how to route the question to the right place. Streaming retrieval is going to change how users actually feel the system working. Right now, you ask a question and you wait. The system finds the data, ranks it, builds the context, and finally shows you an answer. You see the whole thing at once after a long pause. Streaming flips that logic. Results start appearing as they are found. The system shows you the best guess immediately, while it keeps looking for better evidence in the background. The model starts talking to you based on the first piece of evidence, while the retrieval engine is still working, to find something even better. This means we have to stop thinking of this as a straight line, where one thing happens after another. We have to decouple finding data from talking about it. Even if the total time is the same, the user feels like the system is faster because they aren't staring at a loading spinner. Then there is personalization. Today, everyone gets the same answer to the same question. Tomorrow, the system will look at who you are and what you do. If an executive asks about transactions from 2005, they get a high-level summary. If a forensic analyst asks that same question, they get the raw evidence and the full citations. This isn't about changing the facts. It is about changing the priority. If you care about dates, the system shows you a timeline. If you care about networks, it shows you a map. The results stay grounded in reality, but the way they are served to you fits your job. We are also moving towards cross-silo orchestration. As these systems grow, you'll need to look outside your own walls. You might need to check partner data, government records, or public files. One layer will coordinate all of that even though every source has its own security and its own rules. The system will negotiate those permissions for you. It will respect the rules of each silo and then merge the results into one coherent thought. Finally, we have sovereign AI. Regulations are getting tighter every day. You have to be able to prove that your data stays where it belongs, whether that is the EU or a specific industry. Sovereign systems will root your questions only to approved hardware and models. This prevents data from leaking across borders. This isn't a "maybe" for the future. If you are building a global system today, you need to build sovereignty into the core. Key takeaways for enterprise leaders. If you are the one in charge of rolling out copilot, the choices you make right now will decide if it works or fails. These aren't just IT tickets. These are business decisions that carry real risks and real costs. The first thing to realize is that scale changes everything. If you have 10,000 pages, the standard approach works fine. You just index it all and call it a day, but 3.5 million pages will break that model every single time. The money doesn't make sense. The speed drops, the noise gets too loud. At this scale, you stop asking if you can index something. You start asking if that data is actually worth the cost of making it smart. That is a massive shift in how you think. It means you have to prioritize. A leader who gets this will spend their budget wisely. A leader who doesn't will go broke trying to index junk. Governance is not a nice to have feature. It is the foundation of the whole building. Sometimes people try to bolt security on at the end of a project. At this scale, that is impossible. Your permission model is what makes the system work. It is what keeps your data safe. Your audit trails are the only thing standing between you and a massive fine. You have to build governance in from the very first day. Yes, it adds some complexity and a little bit of lag, but paying that price now is much cheaper than trying to fix a data breach later. Selective activation is a strategy, not a failure. Not all of your data is equally important. Your legal filings and key depositions deserve the best indexing and the most expensive processing. Your old background files and supporting documents can live in a cheaper, lighter index. Your archives can stay in cold storage until they are needed. This kind of tiering is how you survive at scale without an unlimited budget. It is the difference between hoping your system can grow and knowing it will. You also have to measure everything. You cannot fix what you aren't tracking. This means you need metrics from the moment you turn the system on. You need to know the cost per query and the accuracy of the answers. You need to hear what the users actually think. Set your baselines before you go live so you can see when things start to drift. The teams that measure their progress are the ones that succeed. The ones that don't just wander into bad performance without even knowing why. There is always a trade-off between speed and cost. If you want better accuracy, you might add a re-ranking step, but that adds 150 milliseconds to the weight. If you want better security, you add permission filters, but that adds overhead. Every single improvement has a price tag attached to it. The question is whether that improvement is worth it for your specific case. A basic chatbot needs to be fast so you might skip the extra steps. A legal tool needs to be perfect so you pay the price for precision. You have to make these choices on purpose. Lastly, a face draw-out is just good risk management. It isn't a delay. Start with the low-risk areas and the users who understand that the tech isn't perfect yet. Listen to their feedback. That feedback will tell you what is actually happening in the real world before you try to scale to the whole company. It is much cheaper to fix a mistake for 100 people than it is for a million. If you rush the deployment, you will hit a wall. If you move deliberately, you can fix the problems while they are still small and then scale with total confidence.
