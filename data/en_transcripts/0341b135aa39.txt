Everyone thinks hallucinations are a model problem, but they aren't. Your engineers point at the LLM and say, "GBT4 is making stuff up while your security team nods and your executives frown." You all start trying to fix the AI with better prompts, newer models and fancier safety guardrails. But here is what is actually happening. The difference between a helpful draft tool and a compliance nightmare is whether you have engineered for truth or just for fluency. Right now you have probably engineered for fluency. Why LLM's hallucinate by design? Let's start where the hallucination actually begins, which is deep inside the training process. Large language models are not knowledge retrieval engines, but instead they are next token predictors. You feed them a sequence of words, and their only job is to calculate which word most likely comes next. Then they add that word and repeat the process over and over. That is how they work. This matters because the training signal they receive is entirely about fluency and completion rather than truth. When you find tuna model with reinforcement learning from human feedback, which is the standard RLHF process, you are telling the model to maximize what feels helpful, natural, and authoritative. You are not telling it to maximize accuracy. In fact, you are doing the opposite. Think about what happens when a model encounters a question it cannot answer from its training data. It has two choices. It can say it doesn't know, or it can generate something that sounds plausible, coherent, and confident. What do you think gets higher marks from human raiders? A simple "I don't know" rarely wins against a smooth, detailed, and well-structured answer that sounds like it came from an expert. The model learns fast, and it realises that confident guessing beats honest uncertainty every single time. This creates a fundamental incentive structure. The entire RLHF loop teaches models that sounding right matters more than being right, so a hallucination that is fluent and structured gets rewarded while an honest admission of uncertainty gets penalized. Benchmark only makes this problem worse. Almost every major AI benchmark like MMLU or GPQA uses binary scoring, which means you either get it right or you don't. There is no credit for saying you are not sure or that a topic falls outside your reliable knowledge. In fact, saying that costs you points, so the model optimises for getting points by guessing confidently when it is uncertain. Consider the math for a moment. If you are being graded on a multiple-choice exam and you don't know the answer, you can leave it blank and get zero points, or you can guess and have a 25% chance of being right. The guess is what maximises your score. And so the model learns that when it doesn't know something, it should guess with total confidence to win. This is the fundamental mismatch at the heart of LLM training because the models optimise for pattern matching. They learn correlations in massive amounts of internet text, so when they see Paris is the capital of, they have learnt that France follows with high probability. And it is just pattern matching, but truth is not just pattern matching and it requires grounding by connecting symbols to reality. Models trained on next token prediction have no mechanism to do that, so they cannot distinguish between a fact that appeared a million times and a falsehood that appeared once. They cannot know which sources are reliable and which are fabricated and they cannot understand what actually happened in the world versus what just gets written about frequently, yet they sound like they can and that is the danger. The model has learned to sound authoritative precisely because authority is rewarded and confidence and completion are rewarded too. Truth is merely one signal among many and often it is not even the strongest one. This is where the machine learns to lie. It isn't trying to be deceptive, but it happens because you have trained it to optimise for everything except what actually matters. The data problem, training on internet noise. But here's the thing, even if you solve the training incentive problem and even if you somehow convince the model that honesty beats confidence, you would still have a bigger problem. Your model was trained on the entire internet or at least a massive slice of it and the internet is not a reliable source of truth. The training corpus contains contradictions, it contains conspiracy theories, sitting next to peer-reviewed science and it contains outdated information that was true five years ago but is not anymore. It contains deliberate falsehoods, misinformation and opinion presented as fact. All of it is mixed together. The model has no way to distinguish between them. When you train on billions of tokens scraped from webpages, forums and social media posts, you are feeding the model a corpus that reflects human disagreement and human error at scale. The model learns the patterns in all of it but it does not have a mechanism to say that one source is reliable and another is not. It learns correlations. It does not learn truth. Consider a specific example. Somewhere in the training data, a fact appears about when a particular CEO took office. That fact appears once or twice in an obscure blog post or a rarely visited Wikipedia edit history. The model sees it once and it ingests the pattern. Meanwhile, the same fact appears in a different, incorrect version, thousands of times. Maybe it is a common misconception or maybe it is part of a meme that got repeated across the web. The model sees this wrong version hundreds or thousands of times. Now you ask the model about that CEO, which version does it draw from? Probability favors the common version because of distribution imbalance. The model has learnt that frequently repeated patterns are statistically more likely to be right, but in this case they are not. The falsehood wins. This is distribution imbalance acting as a hallucination engine. Rare facts do not get encoded reliably, but common myths do. When the model encounters a query, it cannot ground in high confidence patterns. It fills the gap with whatever pattern is statistically most plausible, regardless of accuracy. In Enterprise Rack, this problem gets worse because you have added a new layer of noise. You are not just inheriting the internet's contradictions. You are inheriting your organization's own data chaos. Your SharePoint has old policies sitting next to new ones and your documentation is a graveyard of deprecated procedures that nobody bothered to delete. You have got draft documents mixed with final versions and you have got personal notes that look official. When you index all of it and let co-pilot retrieve from it equally, you have created a data distribution problem inside your own walls. Old guidance ranks just as high as new guidance and a stale policy retrieved from an archive team site competes with the current policy from the official library. The model has no way to know which is current. It just knows both are retrievable, so it pulls from both, or it pulls from whichever ranks slightly higher by semantic similarity. If it is not confident, it synthesizes. It combines elements and it invents context to make the contradiction disappear. You have built a hallucination engine, not because the model is flawed, but because your data is fundamentally untrustworthy. It is noise masquerading as context. Now let's talk about how you actually build this into your system. Retrieval as a hallucination engine. Now imagine you have indexed all that chaos. You have got a vector database full of everything including current policies, old versions, and drafts that your users can technically access. Co-pilot is live. A user asks the question, "What happens?" The retrieval system springs to life. It encodes the question as a vector, and then it searches for semantically similar chunks in the index before returning the top results. This is where the system breaks. Retrieval systems do not return all relevant results. They return the most relevant results, often called the top-car results. Usually, this is the top five or top ten depending on your context window budget. That is a hard constraint because you cannot fit everything into the prompt. You choose the best matches according to your similarity metric. But being the best match by cosine similarity is not the same as actually answering the question correctly. Consider what happens in practice. Your co-pilot receives a query about expense policy. The retrieval system finds 50 documents mentioning expense policy, but it only returns the top five. Those five include three documents from the current HR site, one old draft from an archive team, and one external blog post about general expense management. The model receives all five. It has no way to know which ones are current, and it has no way to know that one is deprecated. It just sees five documents about expense policy that all rank roughly equally in terms of vector similarity. So it synthesizes, it pulls context from all of them, and it blends the old guidance with the new guidance. It adds confidence and structure to the contradictions. The result is a hallucination. This did not happen because the model invented facts, but because retrieval gave it conflicting signals and no way to resolve them. This is the inexhaustive retrieval trap. You cannot retrieve everything, so you retrieve what you think is most relevant. But relevance metrics are not the same as correctness. A document can be semantically relevant to a query and still be operationally wrong. Context windows make this worse. Modern models have large context windows, but even with a large window you cannot fit your entire knowledge base. So you still choose. You still truncate. You still send the model a subset of what exists, and in that subset important context gets buried. There is a phenomenon in retrieval research called Lost in the Middle. If you give a model 10 documents, the critical information in documents six through nine often gets ignored. The model attends to the beginning and end, while the middle gets lost in the noise. So co-pilot retrieves a ranked list. The most relevant document is number one, and your least relevant is number ten. The model processes them. But when you ask it a follow-up question that depends on information from document number seven, it either hallucinates an answer or contradicts what it said earlier. It lost track of the middle. Permissions sprawl amplifies this disaster. Your share point has overshared libraries and your team's channels have guest access. Co-pilot retrieves based on what the user is technically allowed to see, not on what they should see. A user with broad permissions gets broad retrieval, and they see sensitive documents mixed with public ones. Stale content competes with fresh content and drafts rank alongside finals. The retrieval system does not distinguish between them, it just returns the most similar vectors. Lifecycle management failures weaponize this completely. If you index everything without dates or deprecation flags, then old guidance never stops being retrievable. A policy from 2019 remains in the index? It is still in the vector space, and it still matches queries about that topic. It still gets retrieved and ranked. So you ask about current benefits eligibility. The system returns the 2019 benefits policy. Nobody noticed it was deprecated, and nobody marked it obsolete. It is just sitting there, semantically relevant but completely wrong. This is retrieval as a hallucination engine. It is not because the model is broken, it is because the retrieval system hands the model conflicting, stale and noise heavy context, and the model does what it was trained to do. It synthesizes a confident answer from whatever it is given. But retrieval is only half the problem. The grounding failure, when context becomes liability. Everything is supposed to be your safety mechanism. The theory is simple. You retrieve context from your knowledge base. You put that context in front of the model. The model answers based on what is there. It is bounded, it is verifiable, it is safe. But in reality, grounding becomes your liability. The problem starts with how you actually assemble the prompt. You retrieve 10 documents and chunk them into pieces. You strip away the metadata and concatenate the text into one giant messy blob. Now you have a prompt that looks like a wall of text. It has system instructions at the top, and the user's question at the bottom. When you add a final command, answer based only on the provided context. You think you've built a cage for the AI, but you haven't. What you've actually done is hand the model a pile of weak evidence and told it to build a house. The documents you retrieved aren't all the same. Some are official policies, while others are just edge cases or all discussions where people disagreed. The model receives all of it at once. Everything is flattened into the same format. There are no signals telling the AI which document actually matters more. This is the too much context problem. The assumption is that more evidence equals better answers, but it doesn't. At a certain point adding more documents doesn't clarify the truth. It obscures it. The model has to synthesize across conflicting statements and navigate contradictions. And when it tries to make sense of the mess, it hallucinates. It doesn't just invent facts from thin air. It blends facts from your context to produce something entirely new. It creates an answer that wasn't in any single document, but feels like it should be true. If document A says the policy requires X and document B says it requires Y, the model might synthesize a third interpretation. That interpretation satisfies both documents, but it isn't real. It sounds grounded because it cites sources, but it's a total fabrication. This is the citation illusion. You ask the model to cite its sources and it points to document five. It pulls a quote that actually exists in that document. But here's the problem. That quote doesn't actually support the claim the model just made. The AI used the quote as cover for an inference it made up. The citation is real, but the logic is a hallucination. You end up with confident, wrong answers backed by plausible sources. It chunks often mislead the model because they are fragments without a soul. A policy might explain a rule in paragraph seven, but paragraph three contains a constraint that changes the entire meaning. If you only retrieve paragraphs three and seven, they will look like they conflict. The model doesn't have the rest of the document to understand why that conflict exists. Because it lacks the full picture, it invents a way to reconcile the two points. Data quality corruption now runs through your entire pipeline. Your retrieval gave you mixed quality sources. Your prompt didn't wait them correctly. Your instruction said answer based on context, but didn't say what to do with contradictions. The model filled the gaps because it had to. You gave it incomplete information and demanded a complete answer. The deeper issue is that you confused more context with better grounding. You thought that if you just shoved enough documents into the prompt, the model would eventually find the right answer, but retrieval isn't the bottleneck anymore. Synthesis is. The model is incredibly good at synthesis. It is literally trained to combine information in ways that feel coherent. When you give it ten documents full of gaps, it fills them. It produces something that sounds authoritative and complete. What you don't see is whether that synthesis matches the real world. Grounding was supposed to prevent this by making everything traceable. In theory, hallucination becomes impossible because the model can only use what it was given, except it doesn't work that way. The model doesn't just rearrange the pieces you provide. It infers and produces novel combinations that feel grounded even though they were never stated anywhere. And this is where the orchestration layer becomes critical. It is a bit more complicated to see the model. This is where the system becomes genuinely insidious. If a user has been added to 50 sites over five years of reorganizations, Copilot sees all 50. Some of those sites are dormant, some are from projects that ended in 2019. Every permission you were too busy to revoke is now part of the AI's brain. Every site you added someone to, but forgot to clean up, is now part of their search results. Identity failure is compound when you start doing multi-step workflows. A user asks a complex question that requires three different sources. Now, let's talk about how you actually orchestrate this disaster. The orchestration anti-pattern. You have now built a system with deep structural problems. The training incentives are flawed, the retrieval is noisy. The grounding is weak, the permissions are a mess. You might think it couldn't get any worse, but you would be wrong. The orchestration layer is where you actively choose to make it worse. Think of the orchestration layer as the control plane. It is the system that sits right between the user's question and the model's answer. It decides what happens before the prompt reaches the model, what happens while the model is reasoning and what happens after it generates a response. This is every checkpoint where you could enforce a policy, validate an assumption, or verify a permission. That is orchestration. But here is the problem. Most organizations skip these checkpoints entirely. Instead of building deterministic controls, they let the model decide everything. A user asks co-pilot to perform an action and rather than having a system that asks if the user is allowed to take that action on their data, they let the model itself decide whether to try it. They do not have validation to see if an output complies with company policies. They just let the model generate whatever it wants and hope for the best. This is architectural negligence masquerading as trust in the model. Think about what happens when you build this way. A user asks co-pilot to summarize confidential board meeting notes and send that summary to an external partner. The model receives the request, but there is no validation layer to stop it. No one asks if the user is authorized to see those notes, or if that partner is on an approved list or if sharing that category of document is even allowed, those checks do not exist. The model just tries to do what it was told. It retrieves the board notes. It generates a summary. It prepares to send it and nobody stops it because nothing was ever designed to stop it. The orchestration layer failed to validate the request before processing it failed to check permissions mid workflow and it failed to apply policy before execution. This is what happens when you treat prompts as free form instructions rather than constrained contracts. Telling the model to do what the user asks instead of telling it to do what the user asks within specific boundaries. One approach is flexible, but the other is just reckless. Tool execution becomes particularly dangerous here. Co-pilot can connect to power automate, call SharePoint APIs or trigger complex workflows. When you allow tool execution without verifying authorization, you have handed the model access to systems that require very careful permission checks. But those checks never happen. The model decides which tools to call based on its own interpretation of what would be helpful. It executes them under whatever identity it is running as. If that identity is over-privileged like a service principle with broad access, then co-pilot's tool calls carry that same privilege. A wrong action executed with broad permissions is a recipe for disaster. Letting agents roam free across connectors and data sources is where this philosophy reaches its logical conclusion. You build co-pilot agents where each one can connect to multiple data sources like SharePoint, Teams or external APIs. Rather than constraining each agent to the specific connectors it actually needs, you give it broad access. Rather than limiting which data sources it can touch, you let it discover and access whatever is available. The agent decides what to retrieve, what to query, and which connectors matter. The result is predictable. You get confident, plausible, and completely wrong answers generated from data. The agent had no business accessing in the first place. An agent built to answer questions about sales processes might connect to the HR connector to find personal information for context. It should not have that access, but because nobody set a constraint, it accesses and synthesizes that data anyway. The answer sounds authoritative because the agent found supporting data, but the data is wrong for the purpose, so the answer is wrong. Nobody stopped it at any checkpoint because no checkpoints exist. This is the orchestration anti-pattern. You have built a system where every decision point that could catch a problem has been removed. Every validation that could enforce a policy has been eliminated, and every authorization check that could prevent misuse has been skipped. The model has maximum freedom and zero guardrails. The system works until it doesn't, and when it fails it fails with total confidence, but there is a specific way to make this even worse. Prompt engineering for maximum confidence. Now we get to the engineering work. You have built an orchestration layer with no guardrails, set up retrieval to pull from a chaotic corpus and allowed synthesis without any constraints. Now you are going to embed that entire philosophy directly into the system prompt. The system prompt is where you establish how the model behaves. It is the very first thing the model reads before it ever sees a question from a user. The words you choose in this space matter enormously. Most organizations write prompts that sound perfectly reasonable on the surface. They tell the model to be a helpful assistant to provide accurate answers and to stay professional and clear. These sound like they should work, but they really don't. The problem is that being helpful and being accurate often point in completely different directions. When the model is uncertain about the accuracy of a fact, it still wants to be helpful to the user. When you tell it to prioritize helpfulness, it will sacrifice accuracy to get there. A user asks a question, and the model could say it doesn't have enough information to answer. You are honest, but it is also unhelpful because the user walked away with nothing, or the model could generate a plausible answer even though it is uncertain. That is helpful because the user got something and the model learns which behavior you rewarded. So you rewrite your system prompts to make this trade off explicit. You tell it to prioritize being helpful and providing a complete answer instead of prioritizing accuracy. You tell the model that a user would rather have a detailed response than an honest admission of ignorance. Because that is what you have structurally incentivized anyway, you might as well make it clear in the prompt. Use vague instructions and let the model fill in the gaps. Instead of telling it to answer based only on the provided context, you write something like "use the provided context" to inform your answer. There is a massive difference there. The first is a hard constraint that says "do not invent things" but the second is just a suggestion. It says the context is one source but not necessarily the only one. The model can use it but it can also synthesize beyond it. You have left the door wide open for exactly the kind of inference that produces a hallucination. Better yet. Do not mention constraints at all. Do not say to answer only from the provided context. Just ask the question and let the model decide what to use. It will use everything available to it, including retrieval results, internal training knowledge and patterns it has learned. It will blend them and synthesize them. It will do this confidently because you never told it that synthesis was wrong. You can even encourage the model to be creative. Tell it to fill in any gaps in the information with reasonable inferences. That is an explicit instruction to hallucinate. You have told the model that gaps exist, that its job is to fill them and that using inference is a reasonable thing to do. The model does exactly what you asked. It infers its synthesizers and it produces confident wrong answers because you gave it permission. Make it clear that uncertainty is a failure. Never tell the model it is acceptable to say, "I don't know." Instead frame that as a last resort. Tell it to provide an answer. And only if it is genuinely unable to answer should it explain its limitations. But you structure it so that explaining limitations is the rare exception. The default is to provide something anything as long as it sounds coherent. This is prompt engineering for maximum confidence. You are not lying to the model and you are not telling it to make things up. You are just using language that systematically steers it towards synthesis over honesty. You are making confidence the default and uncertainty the exception. You are framing gaps as something to fill rather than something to report to the user. The magic is that you can do all of this while sounding completely reasonable. You are not saying to lie, you are saying to be helpful. You are not saying to make things up, you are saying to fill gaps with inference. The language is professional but the intent is reckless. And here is the most insidious part of the whole thing. Focus on the material that actually drives decisions classify it as a source or a reference and make sure that label can be read by the search engine. Finally, you should build retrieval quality metrics into your deployment gates. Before you roll out any change to copilot whether it is a new data source or a different ranking method you must test how it affects what is being found. Run your audit queries again to verify that the top results are still the right ones. You need to measure precision and recall on a set of gold standard data. If these metrics don't improve or at the very least stay the same, you do not deploy the update. The shift here is moving from letting the system see everything to controlling what it is allowed to find. You aren't deleting your documents or removing information from the company. You are simply constraining how aggressively copilot searches and prioritizing quality sources over the surrounding noise. You are building business logic directly into the retrieval layer. Instead of pretending you can fix the mistakes after they happen. This is the foundation of the whole system. You have to fix retrieval first because everything else depends on whether the model is given the right evidence to work with. But even with perfect retrieval, control alone is not enough. Grounding as a constraint, not a feature. Retrieval control ensures you have the right evidence but grounding is what you do with that evidence once the model has it. This is where most organizations make a massive mistake. They treat grounding like a feature that improves performance when it's available but you need to treat it as a constraint that defines which answers are allowed. This is an architectural shift you have to make. Grounding is not an optional setting that you turn on for hard questions and off for easy ones. It is the boundary that separates a real answer from a hallucination. If an answer doesn't have grounding, it isn't a helpful suggestion or a rough draft. It is a total violation of how the system is supposed to work. You should start with explicit grounding requirements. Your system prompt needs to have a rule that cannot be negotiated, telling the model to answer only using the context it was given. Don't tell it to prefer the context or try to use the documents. Make it unconditional. When the model gets a query and a set of documents, the answer must come from those pages. If the answer cannot be built from what is right there in front of it, the model should not build an answer at all. This sounds like a simple rule but it takes a lot of discipline to pull off. These models are trained to be helpful and being helpful usually means giving a full answer even if the evidence is missing a few pieces. The model has learned how to fill in the gaps and use its own training to fix weak grounding. You are asking it to kill that instinct. You wanted to refuse to guess and admit when the evidence isn't good enough to give a full response. The way you make this happen is through citation requirements. Every single fact the model claims must be traceable back to a specific piece of the retrieved context. This isn't just a style choice. It is a structural requirement for the system. The model should identify the claim it wants to make, find the source that supports it and verify that the source actually says what the model thinks it says. If it can't find a direct link between a claim and a source, it shouldn't say it. This creates a necessary feedback loop. The model learns that every sentence it writes must have proof to back it up. It can't just make assertions or combine sources to create brand new claims that aren't there. It can only restate and put together what is explicitly written in the documents you gave it. This makes the models vocabulary much smaller but that is exactly what you want. You want the words it uses to match the evidence that is actually available. You should also add confidence thresholds as a gate before any response is shown. Don't just ask for citations. Make the model decide if it is actually sure enough to give an answer. You can build this right into the generation process. Before the system shows a response, it should ask itself how confident it is that the answer is correct based on the context. If that confidence score falls below a certain level, like 70%, the system should stop. Instead of a guess, it returns a message saying it doesn't have enough information to answer. This is the specific tool that stops the model from talking when the retrieval step has failed. If the search only brought back weak or unrelated documents, the model needs to recognize that the evidence is bad. Confidence thresholds turn that recognition into a rule. They force the system to be honest about poor evidence. Instead of pretending that a bad document is good enough to use, refusal patterns are another way to teach the model that staying silent is often the right choice. Instead of trying to generate something every time, you train it to see which requests it should stay away from. If the evidence is thin, it shouldn't answer. If the question asks for an interpretation that goes beyond the text, it shouldn't answer. If the sources contradict each other and there is no clear way to resolve them, it shouldn't answer. The model needs to learn a vocabulary for saying no, using phrases like "the provided documents don't address this" or "I would need more information to answer this accurately". You also need multi-layer validation to check every answer before it leaves the system. Once the model writes a response, it should pass through several checks. You need to ask if every claim has a citation and if that citation actually supports what was said. You have to check if the response contradicts the documents or if the model is making inferences that aren't there. If any of these checks fail, the response is rejected or flagged for a human to look at. Moving from being helpful to being accurate is a fundamental change in how you think. Being helpful without being accurate is a huge liability for a company. Being accurate without being helpful might feel incomplete, but it is much easier to defend. You are choosing the safer path. The system will sometimes tell a user it can't help and that might be disappointing, but when it does give an answer, that answer will be backed by evidence you can verify. This approach based on constraints is what actually kills hallucinations. A model cannot lie with confidence if it is forced to show its work with citations. It cannot make up stories if it isn't allowed to make claims without proof. It won't sound like an expert on topics it knows nothing about if the confidence thresholds force it to admit its lost. Now that we have the rules in place, we need to look at the orchestration layer that actually enforces them. Orchestration as the control plane. The orchestration layer is where intention meets execution. It sits between the user's request and the model's response acting as the buffer between what the model suggests and what the system actually does. This is your control plane. Everything else you've built, the retrieval governance, the grounding requirements, the confidence thresholds. It all exists only on paper until this layer makes it real. Think of orchestration as a series of gates. Each gate inspects the process and decides whether to let it move forward. You have gates before the model receives a request and gates after it generates an output. There are gates before tools execute and gates at every single junction where something could go wrong. None of these gates trust the model. They don't assume the process will work correctly, so they verify every single step instead. Input validation is your first gate. When a user types a question, that text doesn't go directly to the model. It goes through sanitization first to determine the intent and check for manipulation. The system looks for prompt injection patterns or sensitive information that shouldn't be processed. The orchestrator extracts the legitimate question, redacts the sensitive parts, and strips away adversarial instructions before the model ever sees a word. This sounds obvious, but in reality most systems skip it entirely. The user's text flows directly to the model and that's your biggest vulnerability. The orchestration layer stops that by acting as a hard boundary between untrusted input and the model. Output validation then checks what the model generated against your specific requirements. The model produces an answer, the orchestrator receives it and validation runs before that answer goes anywhere. It checks if the answer has citations for every claim and ensures those citations actually lead back to real source material. The system looks for contradictions in the context or claims that seem made up rather than grounded. It checks compliance rules and verifies if the confidence level is high enough to even show the response to a human. If validation fails on any of these points, the answer doesn't get delivered. It gets flagged, maybe it goes back for regeneration or maybe it gets escalated to a person for review. In some cases it's rejected with a simple message saying the system can't confidently answer, but it never circulates as if it's verified truth. Policy enforcement ensures these answers follow your business rules. Your organization has specific standards like ensuring benefit questions only reference current policies or financial guidance comes from approved sources. The orchestration layer encodes these rules and checks every answer against them. A response that violates policy stays hidden, regardless of how confident the model feels about it. Tool authorization is what prevents the model from triggering actions it shouldn't. If your co-pilot can send emails or update records, the orchestration layer verifies the action before it happens. It checks if the user has permission and if the action matches what was actually asked for. It verifies the target resource and ensures the model didn't just hallucinate an instruction. Only after this verification does the tool actually execute. This is where orchestration differs from retrieval governance. Retrieval governance controls what the model can see but orchestration controls what the model can do. It's the difference between limiting what goes in and limiting what comes out both are mandatory. The shift from trust the model to verify everything is a change in your operational mindset. Previously you likely assumed the model would work and treated errors as exceptions. Now you assume errors are the default state. Verification is the baseline and trust is something that must be earned after inspection. This orchestration layer will add latency because verification takes time. Sanitization, validation and policy checks aren't free so your response times will increase. That's acceptable. Latency is the price you pay for a system you can actually rely on but the real control happens one level deeper. At the data layer, data governance is the foundation. The orchestration layer is the mechanism but the data layer is the foundation it sits on and here's the uncomfortable truth. You can build perfect orchestration around terrible data and you'll still produce hallucinations but you can't build perfect data and survive without orchestration. The hierarchy is clear data governance comes first. This means you need classification before indexing not after the fact and not as a someday project when you have more budget. Before a single document enters your co-pilot index it needs a classification tag. Is it current or archived? Is it a draft or an approved version? This classification lives as metadata that travels with the document through the entire pipeline. During retrieval that metadata decides what gets surfaced during ranking it sets the priority and during validation it defines which answers are allowed. This requires real discipline at the point of creation. When someone starts a document in SharePoint they should have to classify it immediately. Your templates, libraries and workflows must enforce this. The document simply can't be published until it has a tag.
