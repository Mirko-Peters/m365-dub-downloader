If the manager opens the catalog and sees a collection called call x7f2qa or an asset called sqlprod_rohr_e2, they have already lost trust. They leave, they do not come back. Name every collection for the human who reads it. Claims. Finance. Customer. Use simple business names. For the domains themselves, you need a structure that lines data up with experts and survives the next organizational reog. Use a blend of subject area domains like claims and customer, functional domains like finance and risk, regulatory domains for compliance and privacy, and project domains like fraud analytics. You need multiple perspectives because data serves multiple purposes. Three habits keep this relatable. Its friendly names instead of system defaults because the friendly name is what people actually see. Spend real effort on naming. Let the governance domain name echo the collection name when they align and keep your hierarchies shallow. A non-technical user should be able to navigate your structure intuitively without a guide. This is how you avoid a fragmented experience where users have to search both the collection tree and the domain tree to find what they need. Your name things deliberately, so the two structures feel like they are describing the same landscape from different angles. Now we understand how to structure the catalog, but structure means nothing without clear ownership. That's where data products become the bridge that connects everything. The data product, where structure becomes value. A data product is a business concept with a name, a description, owners and a list of data assets. It is the layer where all the architectural work you have done actually converts into something people can use. Everything up to this point is enabling infrastructure. The four layers, the role groups, the collections organized by business domain and the governance domains are all necessary, but they are not where value happens. Value happens when a consumer opens the catalog, finds one thing and gets an answer. A data product groups assets under one specific use case. The consumer finds it, requests access once and receives everything they need behind it. Policies attach to the product cascade down to the assets and access approval happens at the product level rather than the asset level. The consumer never touches the complexity underneath. This is the moment the rollout pays off, take the fraud detection question, which claims are most likely to be fraudulent this quarter. That is one product. All it certified claims fraud signals and put it inside the fraud analytics governance domain. Now list the assets that power it. You have the raw claims table from the policy admin system in Azure SQL, the payout history from Snowflake and enriched features table in the fabric gold lake house and a scored output report in Power BI. That is four sources across multiple platforms with different ownership models underneath. But from the perspective of the fraud analyst, it is just one thing. Certified claims fraud signals has one owner, one access policy and one set of terms of use. It is a single contract that tells you what you can do with the data, how fresh it is and who to contact if something breaks. That owner is accountable for all four assets, even though they live in different places and are managed by different technical teams. The owner does not manage the SQL database or the Snowflake connection and they do not run the fabric pipelines. They own the product that emerges from all of it. They define what fraud signal means and attach a glossary term like suspicious claim that is defined once in the fraud analytics domain. Any asset tagged with that term inherits the product's access policy. The definition is the single source of truth and the enforcement is automatic. This is how governance scales. You do not make every asset its own policy document. Instead you group assets under products and apply policies at the product level. When an approved data scientist requests access to certified claims fraud signals, they do not request access to four different assets on four different platforms. They make one request and get one approval. In that moment, the data product owner or an approver checks if this person needs the data and if the purpose makes sense. Once access is approved, the system provisions all four assets at once. This stops the scientist from having to chase four different platform teams. Because you build use case first, you can prove value before you scale. You do not build 10 data products at the same time. You build one, test it with the fraud team and measure whether the model actually works. You find out if it predicts fraud, if the analyst trusts the score and if they can act on it. You gather that feedback and iterate before you hand it over. Keep the domain in draft and give the fraud team read access. They use it and tell you what works and what does not. You learn what is actually valuable before you spend time maintaining a catalog of things nobody asked for. Then you move to the next use case. One question is proven and the next is already lining up. This is the difference between a catalog and a swamp. A swamp is a thousand assets no one asked for. Organized in a way that only makes sense to the IT team and discovered by no one. A catalog is a curated set of answers to questions people actually need. It is built one question at a time. It is built by someone accountable, governed by policy and discoverable through business language. The product is the bridge. It pulls assets across collections and layers into one unit and it hides the plumbing from the person who just wants an answer. But there is one more critical piece before a product can actually work as data transforms through the medallion layers ownership transforms with it and that handoff has to be explicit. The medallion accountability chain bronzes to gold. Most catalogs ignore one specific question. It isn't because the answer is hard to find. It is because the answer is uncomfortable who actually owns the data after you transform it. You already know who owns the raw claims table. The policy admin team pulls it from the source system. They make sure the ingestion worked. They certify that the data was captured faithfully. Their job is fidelity not correctness. They vouch for what came out of the system that is their role. But then something changes. The fraud analytics team takes that raw data and starts working. They clean the claims. They join them to historical payout records from a completely different system. They engineer features in a fabric leg house. They score the results. They build a model to predict which claims are likely fraudulent. By the time that score hits the gold layer, the business ready zone, it isn't the policy admin team's data anymore. They didn't write those joins. They didn't engineer the features. They don't even understand the model. They can't explain why a specific claim got a high score. They can't stand behind a number they didn't create. But on paper, they still own the raw claim. So who owns the fraud score? Is it the policy admin team who brought in the source? Is it the engineering team who handled the transformation? Is it both? This is where most catalogs fail. They don't answer. They register the raw table. They scan the gold table. They build a data product that includes both. And then they leave the ownership ambiguous. The assumption is that we will figure out who is accountable when something breaks. That's theatre. It isn't governance. In reality, here is what happens. Something breaks. A regulator asks who is responsible for a fraud score that turned out to be wrong. Three different teams point at each other. The policy admin team says they provided accurate source data. The engineering team says they followed the spec. The product owner says everyone did their jobs. No one is actually accountable because accountability was never assigned. The solution is an explicit handoff, as data flows from bronze to silver to gold, accountability must flow with it. It moves from the source owner to the data product owner who's team did the work. The structure of ownership changes. The responsibility shifts, usually. That shift happens in the silver layer. Silver is where the magic happens. Raw data gets cleaned. Multiple sources get joined. Business rules get applied. By the time data reaches silver, it is no longer source aligned. It isn't a faithful copy of the system anymore. It has been interpreted. It has been shaped by business logic. Someone had to decide how to handle nulls or how to join ambiguous keys. Those decisions belong to the team that made them. So the ownership shifts. The policy admin team owns the raw claims in bronze. The fraud analytics product owner owns the gold features table. Someone in the middle, usually the central data engineering team, owns the silver layer. They own the transformation logic. They own the joints. They can explain why the data looks the way it does at that specific step. Each zone has an owner, each owner answers for what they actually built. You have to write this into the catalog. Don't just leave a comment. Use explicit assignments. Set the policy admin team as the owner of the bronze claims table. Set the fraud analytics product owner as the owner of the gold table. Set the data engineering team as the owner of the silver table. Draw the lineage to show exactly where the raw source ends and the derived product begins. This is the difference between governance and theatre. In theatre, you have policies and roles. In governance, you have clear accountability at every transformation point. Everyone is responsible. Someone can be asked. Someone can answer when the model breaks or the score doesn't match reality. The moment you write ownership explicitly at each layer, the medallion architecture stops being just a storage pattern. It becomes an accountability chain. Each layer has owners. Each owner is responsible for the quality and correctness of their layer. That is what separates a real rollout from a performative one. It isn't the tool. It's the clarity of who answers when something goes wrong. PIM. Caging the purview administrator. There is one role in purview that requires a different class of control. In purview administrator role, this role can change almost anything in your account. It can create domains. It can delete them. It can modify scan rules or reassign roles to other people. And here is the part that should concern you. It can raise its own permissions. A purview administrator can make themselves a governance domain owner. They can grant themselves access to sensitive data products. They can change the policies for the entire organisation. If that role is compromised, the damage is effectively unlimited. If credentials leak or a terminated account is reactivated, the scope of exposure covers the entire platform. The window to detect the problem is incredibly narrow. This is why the purview administrator group cannot work under traditional access models. You cannot make people permanent members of this group. You cannot let them use it every day. That is structural risk. The solution is Microsoft Enter Privileged Identity Management. PIM. PIM isn't a tool you layer on top later. It is structural governance for privileged roles. It enforces just-in-time access instead of standing access. When an admin needs to work, they don't log in with that role active. They request activation. Here is how it works. An admin realises they need to create a new governance domain. They don't use their admin account directly. They go into the PIM portal and request the purview administrator role. The system asks why they need it. They provide a justification like creating a new domain for a specific use case. The system requires multi-factor authentication. They complete it. The request goes to an independent approver. That person reviews the request to see if it makes sense. If it does, they approve it. Now the admin has the role active, but only for a set amount of time. Usually this is between one and four hours. They do the work. They create the domain. They assign the owners. Then they step away. The role expires automatically. The system revokes it. No one has to remember to remove the access. The system enforces the expiration itself. Every single activation is logged. You see who activated it and when. You see the justification they provided. You see how long they held the power and what they did with it. This creates an unbreakable audit trail. The configuration of PIM around purview is intentional. You set the maximum duration to four hours. You always require MFA on activation. This means even if someone steals a password, they can't activate the role without a phone or a hardware key. You require justification to force intentionality. You require approval for high-risk roles, so someone else has to sign off. You set up notifications to alert domain owners when these activations happen. These settings implement controlled access with real accountability. The blast radius of a compromised admin is now measured in hours, not months. The attack surface shrinks. It moves from someone with standing access to someone with credentials, a second factor, and an approval from a peer. The purview administrator group sits empty by default. No one is an active member. Users are made eligible. Eligibility means they can request activation, but they don't have the power until they actually activated. That distinction is critical. Eligible is not the same as active. Eligible is potential. Active is power. When an admin needs to work, they activate. When they are done, the role expires. When something breaks, you look at the log to see who activated what and why. This is the difference between trusting people and verifying their actions. This is structural control over the most powerful role in your account. Now, let's talk about how to actually roll this out. The rollout sequence. Start narrow, scale slow. Most teams fail here. Not because they picked the wrong structure, but because they tried to do everything at once, they spend weeks designing a perfect permission model and documenting role groups. Per view spreads access across four distinct layers. Most teams discover them one access denied ticket at a time. Here is how it works. You have the tenant layer.
