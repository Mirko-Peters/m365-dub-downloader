Your data map is supposed to show everything, but in most organizations it shows the data someone already registered. It doesn't show the rogue S3 bucket a marketing team created 14 months ago. It doesn't show the one drive copies of your customer database. And it doesn't show why your AI training pipeline keeps pulling from unverified sources. That's not a failure of policy. It's a failure of architecture. The invisible problem. Shadow data isn't hidden because someone is malicious. It's hidden because your governance model was never designed for visibility first. Most enterprises only see 60 to 70% of their sensitive assets. The other 30 to 40% lives outside formal governance frameworks and it grows every day. The definition is simple. Shadow data is any unknown, hidden or overlooked copy of sensitive information that exists outside your IT security and governance frameworks. It's not the same as shadow IT. Shadow IT is the unauthorized applications employees use. Shadow data is the information itself, the actual files and records that slip through the cracks. You can block every unauthorized app on your network and still have massive shadow data exposure because the data lives in authorized tools that nobody is monitoring. Data sprawl is the broader phenomenon. It describes the uncontrolled proliferation of data across multiple locations, systems and devices. Shadow data is the subset of that sprawl which remains invisible to your governance teams. You can have data sprawl that you know about. Shadow data is the part you don't. The distinction matters because many organizations celebrate reducing data sprawl while their shadow data footprint continues expanding unnoticed. Data silos are another related problem. Different teams collect, manage and store their information separately, often with limited cross-departmental access. Silo's create visibility gaps that frequently have a shadow data. But silos are usually the result of organizational structure and historical system implementations. Shadow data often emerges when employees deliberately circumvent official systems to get their work done faster. A silo is the finance team keeping customer data in a separate database that IT knows about. Shadow data is the finance analyst exporting that data to a spreadsheet and emailing it to a vendor. Understanding where shadow data hides is essential because you can't scan for what you don't know to look for. Personal devices are an obvious vector. Employees store corporate files on laptops, tablets and smartphones that never connect to your mobile device management platform. They use consumer cloud storage accounts like Dropbox or Google Drive because the official corporate file share is too slow or too restrictive. They copy databases to local drives for offline analysis. Then forget the copies exist when they move to another project. Departmental servers represent another common hiding place. Business units maintain their own infrastructure for specific applications and these servers often escape central backup, patching and monitoring regimes. A marketing department might run a web server for campaign landing pages that stores prospect data. A research team might maintain a file server for experimental results that contains proprietary formulations. These servers aren't malicious, they're business critical. But if they aren't in your governance framework, they're shadow data repositories. Road cloud storage accounts proliferate faster than most organizations realize. Departmental procurement of SaaS solutions creates numerous data stores that central IT never sees. A sales team adopts a new CRM add-on that syncs customer data to an external database. A support team starts using a cloud-based ticket system that stores attachments containing screenshots of sensitive systems. Each new service creates a new potential shadow data location and the cumulative effect across dozens of business units is staggering. Forgotten repositories are perhaps the most insidious category. Develop a spin-up test databases for a project, use production data for realistic testing and leave the databases running after the project ships. Marketing teams create temporary storage buckets for campaign assets and forget to delete them when the campaign ends. Contractors upload project files to collaboration platforms that remain accessible long after the contract expires. These repositories aren't actively used, which makes them invisible to user activity monitoring, but they remain accessible and they remain full of sensitive data. AI training pipelines have created an entirely new category of shadow data. Data science teams routinely extract production data sets for model training, store them in personal workspaces or blob containers, and iterate on them without documenting the lineage or access controls. These training data sets often contain the most sensitive information in the organization because models require realistic data to produce useful results. The copies multiply as different teams train different models on overlapping data sets and each copy becomes another shadow repository. When a model is deployed to production, the organization often can't verify that its training data was properly governed, which creates compliance exposure that persists for the entire lifetime of the model. The historical arc matters here. In the early days of personal computing, employees started creating local copies of databases and spreadsheets to work around mainframe limitations. That habit never went away. It accelerated with cloud computing and SaaS solutions, which led business units, deploy applications and store data without involving central IT. The proliferation of mobile devices added another layer. Employees began storing and sharing sensitive corporate information on personal smartphones and tablets, often through consumer grade applications that lacked enterprise security controls. Then the global shift to remote and hybrid work created an inflection point. Organizations rapidly deployed new collaboration tools and storage solutions without implementing corresponding governance measures. And the result was an exponential increase in unmanaged data repositories across distributed environments. When employees work from home, the boundary between corporate data and personal data blurs, files moved to home directories, family shared drives and personal cloud accounts because that's the easiest way to access them from multiple devices. Cloud adoption added even more complexity. Business units often procure cloud services through departmental budgets rather than centralized IT governance, which creates a fragmented data landscape where information flows freely across boundaries without proper visibility or control. The lift and shift approach to cloud migration replicates on premises shadow data challenges in cloud environments while introducing new complexities related to multi-cloud architectures and hybrid deployments. A database that was shadow data on a physical server in a branch office becomes shadow data in an Azure subscription that the central cloud team doesn't monitor. Artificial intelligence and machine learning initiatives have introduced the most recent driver of shadow data proliferation. Data science teams frequently create local copies of data sets for model training and experimentation, often storing these copies in unsecured locations without proper governance oversight. The pressure to deliver AI capabilities quickly leads many organizations to prioritize speed over governance, resulting in numerous shadow data repositories containing sensitive information used for AI development. The irony is that these AI initiatives are often sponsored at the executive level while their data practices remain invisible to the same governance programs that protect production systems. The convergence of remote work, cloud adoption, and AI acceleration has created a perfect storm. Each factor reinforces the others to produce an increasingly complex and opaque data landscape that challenges traditional governance approaches. These aren't temporary phenomena. They represent fundamental shifts in how businesses operate and they require equally fundamental changes to data governance strategies rather than incremental adjustments to existing frameworks. The old model for data governance relies on periodic audits, spreadsheet inventories, and department self-reporting. This approach was never fast enough, but in a static on-premises environment, it was at least possible. Today, it's neither fast enough nor possible. Data creation speed exceeds manual cataloging capacity by orders of magnitude. By the time a manual audit finishes, the environment has changed. New repositories have been created, old ones have been forgotten, and the inventory is already outdated. This is the latency of enforcement problem. Your governance policy might be correct, but if your map of the environment lags reality by months, the policy is governing a fiction. The fundamental mismatch is that governance was designed for static systems and is being applied to dynamic cloud estates. Most organizations build their data governance frameworks around the assumption that data lives in known databases and approved file shares. That assumption is broken. Work doesn't start with navigation anymore. It starts with context, and context is created in tools and repositories that emerge faster than any manual process can track. Organizations that conflate shadow data with shadow IT often implement ineffective solutions. They focus on blocking unauthorized applications without addressing the data that already exists in unauthorized locations. Or they attempt to break down data silos without first identifying and securing the shadow data those silos contain. Shadow IT governance focuses on application inventory and user behavior. Data silo governance focuses on integration, architecture, and access policies. Shadow data governance focuses on discovery, classification, and lineage. All three are necessary, but they require different tools and different success metrics. The latency of enforcement isn't just a technical problem, it's an organizational problem. When your governance cycle operates on quarterly audits, your policy changes take effect quarterly. In a cloud environment where new data stores appear daily, quarterly governance is effectively no governance at all. The gap between policy intent and policy reality widens until the policy becomes irrelevant. This is why manual governance failed and it's why automated discovery is the prerequisite for any effective modern governance program. Let's look at how the engine actually runs. Automated Discovery. The scanning architecture in Perview connects to your data sources and extracts metadata without requiring you to move the data itself. This is a critical distinction. Perview doesn't copy your files into a central repository. It reads metadata about your files, databases and storage accounts, then builds a searchable index from that metadata. This approach keeps the performance impact on your production systems minimal while still providing comprehensive visibility. The metadata includes schema information, column names, file sizes, access patterns, and sample content for classification purposes. Because only metadata is indexed, your sensitive data never leaves your controlled environment which addresses a common objection from security teams who worry that a governance tool might become another attack surface. Supported sources span the entire modern data state. On the Azure side, Perview connects to Azure Data Lake storage, Azure Blob storage, Azure SQL database, Azure Synapse Analytics, Azure Cosmos DB, Azure Databricks, Azure files, and many others. For multi-cloud environments, it supports Amazon S3, Google Cloud Storage, various AWS database services including RDS and Redshift and Google BigQuery. On-premises systems are covered through SQL Server, Oracle, Teradata, MySQL, PostgreSQL, and a wide range of file systems including Windows, file shares, and Linux, NFS mounts. SAS applications like Salesforce, SAP ServiceNow and Power BI are also supported. The breadth of connectors matters because shadow data doesn't respect your cloud strategy. It lives wherever business units found it convenient to store it. The scanning process operates through configurable scan rules that determine what data to collect, how frequently to scan, and what classification rules to apply. You can tailor discovery efforts to your specific needs and risk profiles rather than running one size fits all scans across your entire state. This configurability is essential for large organizations where different business units have different data types, different compliance requirements, and different tolerance for scanning overhead. A financial services firm might configure aggressive scanning with deep content sampling for trading databases while running lightweight metadata only scans for marketing assets. The scan configuration workflow starts with registering the data source in PerView. You provide connection details and authentication credentials, then define the scan scope. Scope determines which databases, folders, or containers, the scan examines. You then select a scan rule set that specifies which classification rules to apply. Finally, you set the schedule and trigger type. The initial scan is always full, reading the complete metadata structure of the source. Subsequent scans can be incremental, reading only changes since the last scan. Automated triggers can detect newly created storage buckets and rogue databases almost instantly. When a new S3 bucket appears in your AWS account or a new Azure storage account is provisioned, PerView can be configured to scan it automatically. This prevents the accumulation of ungoverned data that characterizes traditional periodic audit approaches. In the case study we discussed earlier, the marketing teams rogue bucket remained undetected for 14 months because the organization relied on manual inventory processes. Automated triggers would have surfaced it within hours of creation. The trigger mechanism works by monitoring your cloud accounts for new resources. For Azure, PerView can integrate with Azure Event Grid to receive notifications when new storage accounts or databases are provisioned. For AWS, you can figure cloud watch events to notify PerView when new S3 buckets or RDS instances appear. This event-driven approach is far more responsive than polling-based discovery and it ensures that shadow repositories are caught at creation rather than at the next scheduled audit. Incremental scanning is one of the most important capabilities for production protection. Instead of rereading your entire data estate every time, incremental scans focus only on what has changed since the last scan. This dramatically reduces the load on your source systems while keeping your data map current. The initial scan of a large source is always full, but subsequent scans should typically be incremental unless you have made significant schema changes or updated your classification rule sets. When you modify classification rules or scan rule sets, PerView may automatically trigger a full rescan of affected sources to apply the new rules across the entire data set. Managed identities are the recommended authentication model for PerView scanning in 2026. Each PerView account exposes a system assigned managed identity that you grant appropriate reader roles on your data sources. For Azure SQL, you create a user from an external provider mapped to the PerView managed identity and grant database reader roles. For Azure storage, you assign storage block data reader to the managed identity. For on-premises SQL server, you use a service principle or SQL authentication through the self-hosted integration runtime. This pattern removes the need for stored passwords or keys which eliminates a common source of credential rotation failures and security vulnerabilities. The performance overhead of managed identity authentication itself is negligible compared to the scan workload. Once the identity has the right roles, scans authenticate and proceed with metadata reads just as they would with service principles or account keys. When you see slow scans or high load on a data source, the main drivers are almost always scan scope, data volume, schema complexity or classification rules rather than the authentication method. Managed identity does introduce a dependency on Azure Active Directory availability, but in practice, AD outages are rare and scans queue gracefully until authentication resumes. Scan scope optimization is the most effective lever for controlling performance impact. Instead of scanning entire subscriptions or storage accounts, you configure scans at the granular level. For a SQL server, you might scan only specific databases rather than every database on the instance. For a data lake, you might scan only specific folders rather than the entire container. You can also exclude file types that aren't relevant to your governance program such as image files or executable binaries in a document repository. This targeted approach ensures that scan costs and performance remain optimal while still covering your high risk assets. Custom scan rule sets let you exclude classifiers that aren't relevant to a particular asset type. If your scanning a storage container that only contains log files, you don't need to run every sensitive data classifier against it. You create a rule set that includes only the classifiers relevant to your risk profile for that source. This reduces both scan time and the noise in your results. A well designed rule set might include 10 classifiers for a human resources data source and only three for a public marketing content repository. Scan frequency should align with the change rate of your source, highly dynamic sources like streaming pipelines or frequently modified transactional databases might need daily or even sub-daily incremental scans. Static or slowly changing sources like archived financial records might only require weekly or monthly scans. The key is to match your scan cadence to your actual data change patterns rather than running everything on the same schedule. Overscanning waste capacity and increases throttling risk, under scanning leaves gaps in your visibility that shadow data can exploit. Scheduling scans during off-peak hours is a basic but essential practice. For cloud data stores that serve analytics only during business hours, you run per view scans at night or on weekends. For globally distributed systems, you identify the lowest traffic window and schedule accordingly. Staggering scan start times across your estate prevents the burst patterns that often trigger throttling or source system slowdowns. Instead of starting all scans at midnight you might start database scans at 11 pm storage scans at 1 am and SAS scans at 3 am. In hybrid environments where per view scans on premises systems through self-hosted integration run times network throughput and gateway capacity become the dominant bottlenecks rather than per view itself. You should use multiple self-hosted integration run times for scale and isolation and you should consider scanning only metadata rather than content sampling in systems where performance or regulatory constraints exist. The integration run time is your bridge to on-premises data and like any bridge it has capacity limits. A single integration run time scanning a thousand on-premises file shares will eventually saturate its network connection. Scan execution monitoring is essential for identifying when a scan has stalled or failed silently. Per view provides scan history views that show start times end times and status for each scan run. You should review these histories weekly during initial rollout and monthly during steady state operations. Failed scans are particularly dangerous because they create the illusion of coverage while leaving assets undiscovered. A scan that fails due to expired credentials on a critical database might go unnoticed for weeks if you aren't actively monitoring scan outcomes. Your operational dashboard should alert on any scan failure, not just on system level outages. Automation is the final piece of a mature scanning strategy. Instead of relying on administrators to manually trigger scans you use Azure CLI, PowerShell or Logic apps to orchestrate scan creation and execution. This ensures that scans run consistently and that new data sources are brought under governance as part of your standard provisioning workflow rather than as an afterthought. You can integrate scan provisioning into your infrastructure as code pipelines so that every new database or storage account automatically gets registered and scanned within hours of deployment. This closes the window between creation and governance that shadow data exploits. A terraform template that provisions a new Azure SQL database should include a purview registration block that creates the scan rule set, assigns the managed identity role and triggers the initial scan as part of the same deployment pipeline. Throttling and production protection scanning at scale introduces a problem most teams ignore until production slows down. The Microsoft purview data map is a cloud metadata service that stores information about data sources assets, schemas, classifications and lineage. It's not an infinite resource. It has throughput limits and when you hit them the consequences cascade through your entire governance program. Throttling occurs when requests to the data map exceed the provision throughput capacity units capacity units represent how many operations the service can reliably process per unit time. When limits are hit additional requests are delayed, rejected or retried with back off. Scan and ingestion jobs slow down or stall interactive operations like search, browse and lineage views become sluggish during spikes. The user experience degrades precisely when your governance teams need the catalog most. Each purview account has a base allocation of capacity units included with the account. You can request increases through Azure support quotas but once you upgrade data map throughput you can't downgraded under the classic model. This makes capacity planning important for both cost and long term architecture. Many organizations adopt a stepwise increase strategy raising capacity incrementally based on observable load rather than jumping straight to the highest tiers. A common pattern is to start with the base capacity monitor for throttling events during the first month of production scanning then requests a single increment that covers observed peak load plus 50% headroom. The default limit for concurrent scans per purview account is five. You can request an increase up to 10 through Azure support but this requires justification based on your workload needs. Self-hosted integration runtime scenarios may be governed by separate limitations that are explicitly outside the standard concurrency table. If you attempt to start more scans than the allowed concurrent limit you encounter throttling errors and queued or failed scan runs. The error messages typically indicate that the scan is waiting for capacity rather than failing permanently which means your governance data arrives late rather than never arriving. Late data is still a problem for time sensitive compliance requirements. Throttling most often affects large initial estate scans, high frequency incremental scans of big data platforms, massive lineage event ingestion from synapse and fabric bulk updates of classifications or glossary terms and parallel automation scripts calling data map APIs for custom metadata management. Burst the ingestion from massive initial scans tends to hit throttling first continuous steady workloads fair better even at moderate capacity when properly scheduled. The difference is analogous to water pressure in a building a single faucet running steadily works fine but every tenant opening every faucet at once overwhelms the system. When data map throttling is triggered the direct technical impacts include scan job slowdowns and failures backlog of metadata ingestion where assets scanned in the source aren't yet reflected in the catalog intermittent errors for API clients and degraded interactive experiences in the catalog user interface. From a business and operational perspective this translates to delayed compliance visibility slower AI and analytics onboarding reduced trust in lineage and catalog data and incident response friction when security teams can't quickly locate and assess impacted assets. Lineage tracking classification tells you what the data is, but lineage tells you where it came from and where it went and that's where shadow data becomes visible. Data lineage is the life cycle that spans a data sets origin and its movement over time across your data state. It enables you to trace information from source to destination, including all transformations it undergoes during processing. This comprehensive map of data flows often reveals previously unknown shadow data repositories that you would never find through static scanning alone. The lineage graph shows not just the endpoints, but the paths between them, which is how you discover that a govern data set has spawned unauthorized copies in unexpected places. Microsoft purview implements lineage through a metadata collection process that connects with various data processing, storage and analytics systems. It extracts movement and transformation information, then combines that information to represent a generic, scenario specific lineage experience that accurately reflects data flows regardless of the underlying technology. The visual representation shows data moving from source to destination with transformation steps clearly marked, which provides an intuitive way for both technical and business stakeholders to understand complex flows and identify potential shadow data touch points. The relationship graph lineage alone is just a map. You need to know who owns the territory. The relationship graph in purview connects disparate data points to identify hops between secure environments and unmanage shadow repositories. It lets you pinpoint the specific owners and applications responsible for creating unauthorized data copies. The pattern repeats in nearly every organization that has shadow data. Data originates in a governed production system moves to an unmanage development or testing environment then leaks to personal storage or external collaboration tools. Production to dev, dev to personal one drive, personal one drive to a consumer cloud storage account. Each hop takes the data further from governance and each hop creates a new shadow copy that your static inventory won't detect. The pattern is so predictable that you can almost diagram it from memory once you have seen it a few times. Understanding the hop pattern is essential because each hop introduces new risks. The production system is governed, backed up and monitored. The development environment might have relaxed access controls to make debugging easier. The personal one drive has no enterprise backup and no access logging. The consumer cloud account is outside your identity perimeter entirely. By the time data reaches the fourth hop, you have lost virtually all visibility and control yet the data might still contain the same sensitive information it had in production. The 3 to 5 hop rule is a practical guideline for graph visualization. Beyond 3 to 5 hops, lineage graphs become difficult to interpret and maintain at scale. This keeps the graph usable for business stakeholders while still preserving the technical detail for engineering teams.
