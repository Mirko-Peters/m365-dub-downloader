When a machine stops, a planar needs more than a red line on a dashboard. They need to know what stopped, why it stopped, which order sits on that line and whether the production plan can still hold. Here's the real problem. Most dashboards start at the wrong end. They show a temperature, a fault code, or an OEE number, but nobody can actually explain where that message came from when it was measured or what changed just before it arrived. So let's follow one telemetry message from a factory sensor to the people trying to make a decision. We'll look at MQTT as the transport, Azure IoT Hub as the managed connection layer, message rooting, and the paths into streaming analytics and history, but you have to start where the signal begins not in Azure. A sensor signal is not yet manufacturing information. Picture a packaging line during a normal production run with products moving down a conveyor toward a filler, then a capper, then inspection and packing. The conveyor motor has a temperature sensor and the PLC sees the temperature rise beyond its normal operating range. At that moment, the PLC holds a raw tag value, motor temperature at 82 degrees Celsius, with a timestamp and a quality state. And that's useful, but it's still just a signal. A sensor reading tells you what one instrument measured, but it doesn't explain the factory. Let's slow down here because this is exactly where a lot of IoT projects lose their way. The raw signal might include a tag name, a numeric value, a unit. The time the PLC read it, a status flag, good, uncertain, or invalid, plus which controller or gateway read the tag. That gives us evidence, but it doesn't give us an operational fact. An event sits one step above a reading. It might say the conveyor motor temperature crossed a warning limit while the line was running, and that already adds interpretation because someone had to define that limit, choose a rule, and decide crossing it deserves attention. Now, a production fact needs more context. It might say the conveyor feeding line two showed a sustained high motor temperature during work order 481C, while the line ran a specific product on the late shift. With that context, a maintenance lead can judge urgency, a planner can ask if the order might slip, and a process engineer can compare this run to others of the same product. Same temperature, very different meaning, consider the questions that follow a temperature rise. Is this motor part of the main conveyor or an auxiliary conveyor that can stop without halting the line? Is the line running, waiting for material blocked by downstream equipment, or in a planned changeover? Which product runs right now? Has the motor been at high load for hours or did the temperature jump in a few minutes? None of those answers live inside the raw number by default, but you don't put every piece of business context inside the PLC. A PLC controls equipment under tight timing and safety rules. It needs to read inputs, execute control logic, and drive outputs predictably. If the motor exceeds a hard limit, local logic may need to slow the conveyor or shut it down without waiting for a cloud service, a dashboard refresh, or a very confident chatbot. The control loop stays close to the machine. Cloud telemetry has a different job, supporting observation across shifts and sites, helping maintenance teams spot patterns, combining machine signals with MIS and ERP, and providing evidence for decisions that go beyond a single PLC cabinet. Sending data to the cloud doesn't turn cloud software into a control system. It gives other parts of the organization a way to understand what the equipment reported, within the limits of the data and the delay in the path. Now picture this, the PLC records three things at nearly the same time. Motor temperature rises, conveyor current rises, and the line state changes from running to fault it. If those messages reach the cloud without reliable timestamps, source identities, units, and quality flags, an analyst later has to guess which came first and whether any of them are trustworthy. And guessing which came first is a terrible foundation for maintenance work. You also need stable asset identity. The tag might come from PLC07 address DB12 offset 34. That means something to the engineer who configured the controller, but not to a planner six months later. The pipeline needs a way to relate that technical source to a physical motor, then to the conveyor, line, and process, step it supports. That relationship can grow over time. At the start you might only know the signal comes from conveyor motor A, but later you can link it to product family, work order, maintenance history, and energy use. So the early design should leave room for that growth instead of locking meaning into a tag name nobody can safely change. The first discipline in this pipeline is simple. Capture the signal faithfully, preserve its source and time, and be honest about what it means. A temperature reading doesn't equal downtime, and a fault code doesn't tell you the root cause. A sensor can't tell you the business impact unless you connect it to the rest of the factory. The PLC also rarely speaks cloud MQTT directly. Between the controller and Azure, there's usually another layer doing the hard translation work. The edge layer bridges PLC reality and cloud messaging. Here's the problem most manufacturers don't talk about. You already have PLC's talking their own protocols while Azure IoT Hub expects a direct cloud connection. That gap is wider than most people realize, and the edge layer is what actually bridges it. Picture a typical older plant with three generations of gear on the same line. One PLC exposes OPC UA, another needs a proprietary driver, and a third only talks through a SCADA system, while the newest controller already has a supported interface. That mix is completely normal. Factories don't retire equipment because its network stack doesn't look modern in a cloud architecture diagram. They keep running it as long as it's safe and productive, so you drop an industrial gateway between that equipment and the cloud path. It reads the signals, maps them into a common structure, and sends selected data upstream. You'll find that gateway running close to the line, maybe in a cabinet, or an industrial PC on the OT network, with strict rules on what crosses into the enterprise network. This isn't bureaucracy for the sake of it, it limits exposure and gives the plant a clear boundary between machine networks and cloud connected services. Here's where the gateway earns its real value. It gives you a fixed point to normalize data. One controller reports temperature as an integer scaled by 10, another gives you a floating point value. One source uses a cryptic engineering tag, another has a readable name. The gateway turns all these different forms into a common message shape before anyone downstream tries to analyze them. Skip that normalization, and every downstream system inherits the same translation headache. Your data platform becomes a museum of controller-specific quirks, and eventually someone builds a spreadsheet to decode them all. Excel always finds a way in. Collection frequency takes the same kind of thought. A PLC might scan an input at hundreds of hertz for local control, but the cloud doesn't need every single scan. If you're monitoring gradual motor heating over a production run, a sample every few seconds, or a one-minute aggregate with min, max, and average, answers the question just fine. On the other hand, a state change demands immediate treatment, a line shifting from running to faulted, a safety interlock signal, or a high temperature threshold crossing, means something different than another normal range reading. The edge layer can publish when something meaningful changes, rather than clogging network and storage with values that say, "Once again, nothing happened. I'm not saying you should blind the discard data." During troubleshooting raw detail is critical. The real point is to decide what each signal is for before you even start collecting it. Keep high frequency values locally for a short window, send aggregates to the cloud for long-term trending, and send exceptions immediately when operations need attention. That design starts with the operational need, not the gateways maximum throughput. Now, the gateway also has to handle the conditions that make factory networks different from office ones. Links drop firewall rules change, a maintenance shutdown restarts equipment in an order nobody planned. During a cloud or network outage, the gateway might need to buffer selected data locally and send it later, while production keeps going under local control. That buffering comes with its own responsibility. The gateway needs enough disk space, a cap on buffer size, and a clear policy for what to discard when that limit hits. You do not want to lose a fault event because a high-rate vibration tag filled the disk overnight. Time needs careful handling before any message leaves the plant. The gateway should preserve the source timestamp whenever possible, rather than replacing it with the moment it happened to read the value. If a message gets queued during a brief outage, it arrives much later than when the PLC measured it. If you only keep cloud arrival time, you create a misleading history of the line. Clock drift creates quieter but real problems. The PLC gateway and cloud service may each have a different idea of what time it is. You don't need perfect time synchronization for every use case, but you do need to know which clock produced each timestamp and how that clock is maintained. Use a common time scheme, store time in UTC where practical and make the source clear. So the edge layer does a lot more than forward tags. It selects, translates, timestamps, buffers, and protects the route out of the plant. And once that gateway publishes upstream, MQTT enters the story. MQTT in plain terms, publishers, topics, and brokers. Once the gateway has selected and shaped a message, it needs a way to send that message without knowing every system that might consume it later. That's the core idea behind MQTT, a lightweight messaging protocol built on publish and subscribe. Take that conveyor motor temperature we talked about. The gateway publishes a message, it doesn't need a direct line to the maintenance app, the historian, the analytics team, or any future alert service someone builds next year. It just sends the message to a known MQTT endpoint under a topic and the broker handles the delivery. A publisher sends a subscriber receives and the broker sits in the middle coordinating. That separation matters because the gateway should focus on its real job, collecting trustworthy signals from the plant and publishing them. It shouldn't have to care whether five downstream systems or none care about that motor temperature right now. In a typical MQTT design, the topic acts like an address for a category of messages. A gateway might publish under a topic that identifies a site, a line, an asset, or a signal type. Subscribers declare interest in one or more topics and the broker forwards messages that match. This creates real loose coupling. You can add a new analytics service without touching the gateway firmware just because another team wants the data. You can also remove a consumer without ever changing the machine site connection. The publisher and subscriber only need to agree on the topic structure and what the payload means. But here's a key point. Don't overload topics with more responsibility than they can handle. A topic can tell you where a message belongs in the transport path. It can support access control and message filtering. It can help a consumer distinguish telemetry from a command or an alarm. But what it cannot do well is service a full description of your factory. I've seen topic paths trying to encode everything. Plant, building, line, machine, product, shift, work order, tag name. And even the engineer who originally created the tag, it looks beautifully organized for about three weeks. Then the line changes and asset moves. The product mix shifts. And that naming scheme becomes a museum of outdated assumptions. A topic is an address. It is not your asset model. The payload still needs its own contract. Any system receiving that motor temperature needs to know if the value is in Celsius, whether the source recorded it at the PLC or the gateway. And if the reading has good quality, MQTT just transports bytes. It doesn't judge whether those bytes represent a motor, a work order, or a faulty sensor. That's also why MQTT works well on constrained devices and unreliable links. The protocol has a tiny footprint and its core behavior stays simple. A client connects, authenticates, publishes messages, subscribes to approved topics and sends regular, keep-a-live traffic to maintain connection state. For a sensor or small gateway, that's a smart model. You don't need the overhead of opening a new HTTP request for every reading. And the publisher doesn't have to call each consumer individually. The connection stays open and the broker becomes the central hub that accepts messages and distributes them. There are delivery options too, but let's stick with the basics for now. MQTT supports quality of service levels that govern how sender and broker handle acknowledgement and retry. It supports persistent sessions and brokers that retain session state. And it supports a last-will message. The client defines a message the broker should publish if it disconnects unexpectedly. These are all transport-level behaviors. They help systems handle network failures and connection state, but they don't magically turn MQTT into a manufacturing system. Making that distinction upfront saves a lot of confusion. MQTT does not calculate OE. It does not decide whether a conveyor fault threatens a customer order. It does not know if a line is in a planned stop unless another system provides that context. What MQTT does is give you a reliable way to move messages between clients through a broker. In practical terms, MQTT is part of the data plumbing. Good plumbing matters, especially when you're connecting OT and IT, but a pipe can't interpret what flows through it. There's also a difference between MQTT as a protocol and a specific service that supports MQTT. A full-broker supports broad-published subscribe patterns, custom topic trees, and many kinds of client-to-client communication. A managed cloud service might only support a subset of that behavior because it's designed for a more specific job. That distinction matters a lot when you're looking at Azure IoT Hub. At small scale, you can publish a JSON message, subscribe to a topic, and call it done by lunch. At fleet scale, the questions change fast. Who owns each connection? Which device can claim which identity? What happens when thousands of devices reconnect after a site outage? And how do you make sure a message path built for one test line doesn't become the unofficial architecture for your entire enterprise? Publishing a message is easy. Running that message path reliably for years, that is the real work by yet. Why millions of devices change the problem? A single gateway on a single line? That's easy. Register a client, send a few messages, confirm the data gets there, simple. But then the pilot grows. More lines connect, more plants join. A device vendor switches, certificates start expiring, or a site network drop causes hundreds of gateways to all reconnect at once. The MQTT packet itself hasn't gotten more complicated, but managing the whole fleet has. Here's the real picture. A large manufacturer has gateways spread across multiple sites. Each one representing dozens or hundreds of physical assets behind it. You need to answer basic questions fast. Which gateway sent this message? Who owns it? Which plant approved it? What software version is it running? And should it still be allowed to connect? An IP address won't help you there. IPs change, networks get rebuilt, devices move between segments. You need an identity that stays tied to the managed device itself, not its network location. And that identity has to have a life cycle. Somebody creates it, a device proves it owns that identity, then credentials expire or rotate. The device might get replaced after a hardware fault, move to another line, or taken out of service completely. If nobody cleans up old identities and old credentials, the system slowly collects access parts that nobody can explain. That's not theoretical. That's normal operational debt over time. Shared credentials seem convenient early on. The team puts the same secret into every gateway because it gets the first connection working fast. Later, one gateway needs to be retired but revoking that shared secret would disconnect every other gateway that still depends on it. So the team leaves it active. Now a lost machine, a copied config file, or a forgotten test gateway can still connect as part of the fleet. The system can't tell one device from another with confidence because they're all using the same identity. You traded a quick setup for a long security and support headache. Per device credentials take more planning, but they give you control. You can revoke one identity without taking down an entire plant. You can trace a message back to the gateway that authenticated it. You can rotate credentials in stages instead of holding a tense maintenance window where everything changes at once. Connection behavior also becomes a fleet problem. Imagine a site loses its outbound connection for an hour. The line keeps running under local control. Gateways buffer selected telemetry and then the network comes back. Every gateway notices the link at roughly the same time and tries to reconnect. If they already try with the same short delay, you get a connection storm. The cloud endpoint gets a burst exactly when the site needs to recover. A good client design uses back-off and randomness. After a failed connection, the gateway waits before trying again. If the connection keeps failing, the wait increases. Small random differences stop every device from knocking at exactly the same second. Sounds boring because it is. It is also what separates a calm recovery from a preventable incident. You also need to watch fleet health without confusing silence with failure. A gateway that hasn't sent telemetry for two hours could be offline. It could also be connected and idle because the line stopped for a plant change over. Or the gateway might still connect while its southbound driver stopped reading the PLC. Connection state and data freshness answer different questions. Track both. Ask when the service lasts or the device connected and separately ask when each expected signal lasts arrived. A connected gateway with stale motor readings needs attention. So does a gateway that sends messages continuously, but with a timestamp that stopped advancing. Duplicate connections cause another quiet failure. Two gateway instances might start with the same device config after a restore, a cloned disk image, or an operator plugging in a spare unit. Both think they own the same identity. The result can look like intermittent data loss, strange reconnects, or telemetry that seems to come from one source, but actually comes from two. That kind of issue can burn days. The dashboard only shows the last message, not the two devices competing behind the same identity. Then there's ritris and buffered messages. A gateway sends a message loses the connection before knowing if delivery succeeded and sends again after reconnecting. Downstream systems may get two copies. That's normal for at least once delivery. It only becomes a business problem when the consumer treats each copy as a new event. For high rate telemetry, noisy data creates a different cost. A tag that publishes every small fluctuation can flood storage, eat throughput, and bury the one event in engineer needs. More messages don't automatically mean more insight. Often they just mean a bigger cloud bill and slower troubleshooting. This is where Azure IoT Hub enters the design. It adds managed identity, authenticated device connections, device facing controls, and routes from incoming telemetry into cloud services. MQTT still carries the message, but the surrounding system starts managing the harder parts of running it at scale. An Azure IoT Hub is not a general MQTT broker. Azure IoT Hub supports MQTT, but that doesn't mean it acts like every MQTT broker you'd run inside a plant or use for general pubs sub. Its job is more specific. IoT Hub manages the connection between a registered device and cloud services. It gives that connection an identity, authenticates it, accepts device telemetry, and exposes defined parts for device management and cloud communication. Then it routes incoming messages into Azure services for processing, storage, or analysis. That focus changes how it handles MQTT. With a general MQTT broker, a publisher can use a custom topic tree, and any approved subscriber can listen for matching topics. You can build local pubs, sub-patterns, where one machine publishes a state, another application subscribes, and neither side needs a direct relationship. IoT Hub doesn't target that freeform broker design. It supports MQTT version 3.1, one for device connectivity, using documented topic patterns for telemetry from the device, messages back to the device, device twin operations, and direct method calls. Those parts give you a structured device to cloud model, not an open topic space where clients invent their own routing rules. That's intentional. A design choice, not a limitation. For a fleet of gateways that needs secure cloud connectivity, it gives you a more managed model. The cloud can identify the device behind a connection. A back end service can receive telemetry through service endpoints. Device management patterns have known routes instead of becoming a collection of custom conventions that every team interprets differently. But it does mean you should check the interaction you need before picking IoT Hub as your MQTT endpoint. Take device to device communication. If conveyor gateway A, publishes a local state, and filler gateway B needs to react with low delay. A cloud round trip through IoT Hub isn't the natural answer. The line should generally coordinate that locally through plant control or through a local broker if pub service part of the design. A production line shouldn't wait for the internet before deciding a downstream machine stopped accepting product. That's a sure way to create scrap. IoT Hub also limits connections in ways that matter during deployment. A device identity supports one active MQTT connection at a time. If a second client connects using the same identity, IoT Hub drops the existing one. That behavior enforces the idea that one identity means one active device connection. It also exposes weak device management quickly. If a spare gateway, a test client, or a cloned image connects with the production identity, the working gateway loses its session. From the plant side, it looks like an unstable network. From the cloud side, it looks like normal reconnect activity. The actual problem is in the identity design. Subscriptions are limited to. A device can subscribe to up to five MQTT topics through IoT Hub and those subscriptions use the documented device facing topic filters. This fits the normal pattern where the cloud sends messages to a name device. Informs it of twin changes or invokes a defined request response operation. It doesn't fit a design where a device wants to subscribe broadly across a custom hierarchy of plant topics with wildcards and many peer publishers. Keep that distinction clear. MQTT as a protocol supports a broad, publish, and subscribe model. IoT Hub uses MQTT as one transport for a managed device connection. The same applies to retained messages. In many MQTT broker designs, a retained message gives a new subscriber the last known value on a topic. IoT Hub doesn't persist MQTT retained messages in that broker sense. If a device sends one with the retained flag, IoT Hub passes it onward and adds an application property recording the flag. So don't build a state distribution model assuming every new subscriber will get current machine state from IoT Hub. For device state synchronization, use the patterns IoT Hub provides and keep device, cloud, and local state responsibilities explicit. There's a practical architecture choice here. If your main requirement is collecting telemetry from managed devices, securing those connections and sending data into Azure services, IoT Hub fits well. If your main requirement is flexible MQTT pubs sub inside the factory, peer-to-peer topic exchange, custom wildcards, or broad fan out between local applications, use a dedicated MQTT broker where that behavior belongs. You can use both. A local broker supports fast plant site exchange while a gateway or bridge forwards selected messages to IoT Hub for cloud processing. That separates local production behavior from cloud ingestion and stops a cloud device service from carrying duties that belong in the OT network. Before sending the first telemetry message, define the connection model. Decide whether this client represents a device, a gateway, a local broker bridge, or a cloud facing service. The rest of the pipeline depends on that answer. Device identity comes before the first message. Before a gateway can send a single telemetry value to Azure IoT Hub, the hub needs to know exactly which device is knocking. That identity starts in the device registry. The cloud side record that says this device exists and here's how it authenticates. Take our packaging line example. The cloud facing device is the industrial gateway for line two and its registry record holds a device ID plus the authentication method. But don't confuse that record with a catalog of every motor or sensor hiding behind the gateway. It's just the identity of the client that opens the connection to IoT Hub. Nothing more. Choose that device ID carefully. It should stay stable for the whole life of the device, traceable to a real asset and fit your naming rules. But here's the thing. Never tie it to an IP address because networks change and don't attach it to a temporary project name and install a laptop convention or a cabinet label that might get swapped during a line rebuild. You'll regret that when the next annual retooling happens. A name like plant aligns O2 gateway, O1 works fine for an early deployment if you've got a control scheme. But in a larger estate, you might prefer a neutral identifier and keep the plant line and asset details in a separate register. Either approach can work. The key is that the device ID needs a clear owner and a record of what it represents. That's the foundation. Now, there's a design choice here that affects everything downstream. One approach is to create a single IoT Hub identity for the gateway and let it publish telemetry for multiple PLC's, machines and sensors. That's common in manufacturing because older devices can't connect to the cloud on their own. The gateway becomes the managed cloud client and the payload itself identifies which physical source each signal came from. This keeps the device registry manageable and matches the physical architecture. A single industrial PC can collect data from several controllers through local drivers and send selected telemetry over one secure cloud connection. But you lose some detail at the connection level. IoT Hub knows that gateway is or one authenticated and sent the message but it can't tell you which PLC behind that gateway actually generated the signal. Your payload contract and gateway logs need to preserve that source chain because if someone later asks whether the motor reading came from PLC 007 or PLC 009, the answer can't be a guess. That's the cost of abstraction. On the flip side, you can assign identities closer to individual devices that gives you direct traceability and lets you revoke or manage one endpoint without affecting others. It works well for native IoT devices, smart sensors or equipment with its own secure client and network path. The trade-off is operational load. Each identity needs enrollment, credentials, renewal, monitoring and retirement. Creating thousands of device records isn't the hard part. Keeping them tied to the right physical equipment through replacements, firmware changes and plant modifications that takes real discipline. So model identity at the point where you can actually manage it. For most factory environments, that means a gateway identity for cloud transport plus separate asset identities inside the manufacturing data model. Don't pretend every old PLC has become cloud native just because a gateway can read it. That usually creates false traceability, which is worse than admitting the boundary exists. IoT Hub supports two common authentication methods. The first is shared access signatures or SIS tokens. The device proves access using a cryptographic token derived from a secret tied to its identity. That works for control testing and some managed device cases but the secret needs proper storage and a planned renewal process. The second method uses X509 certificates. The device proves its identity through a certificate and private key. For a long-lived industrial fleet, certificates often give a stronger path because you can build issuance, trust and renewal into the device lifecycle instead of distributing shared secrets across field devices. Neither method removes the need for operations. A certificate that expires during a holiday shutdown doesn't care that the architecture review approved it six months ago. Someone needs to know which certificates expire, how the gateway renews them, what happens if renewal fails and whether the plant can fit the work into its normal change process. That's where Azure Device, provisioning service or DPS comes in as the fleet grows. Instead of hand configuring every gateway with a fixed IoT Hub connection, a device can enroll through a repeatable provisioning process. DPS authenticates the device and assigns it to an IoT Hub based on the enrollment rules you define. That helps when you build or replace devices at scale. A new gateway arrives with its approved identity material, contacts the provisioning service, receives its assigned hub details and starts its normal connection process. You still need governance around the enrollment group, certificates and ownership but you've removed a lot of manual setup from the field. Consider a failed gateway online too. If the replacement process depends on an engineer copying a production connection string from the old machine, you've got both a security problem and a support problem. A controlled provisioning path gives the replacement its own identity, its own credentials and a record that it entered the fleet. Identity comes first because everything after it depends on trust. The device ID tells IoT Hub who claims to connect, authentication tests that claim and the next layer protects the conversation as it travels across networks the factory doesn't fully control. TLS and network paths, security without pretending the plant is simple. Identity tells Azure which gateway claims to connect. Transport layer security or TLS protects the connection itself so the message can travel through networks without exposing the telemetry or the credentials. For MQTT connections to Azure IoT Hub, TLS isn't optional. IoT Hub won't accept unsecured traffic on port 1883, the port most people know from basic MQTT testing. The normal MQTT connection uses port 8 and 8 III with TLS and if that port can't get through the network rules, you can use MQTT over web sockets on port 443. That second path often sounds like a simple workaround and sometimes it is. But it also tells you something about the organization. Port 443 typically passes through corporate firewalls because it carries normal encrypted web traffic while port 8883 may trigger a security review or get blocked by default. Neither choice removes the need to involve the people who own the network. In a plant, the gateway rarely has an unrestricted route to the internet. It might sit in an OT network zone behind a firewall, maybe even behind another firewall between OT and Enterprise IT. That separation exists for good reason. A production network has different risks, different maintenance windows and different failure consequences than a standard office network. So map the route before you deploy anything. Ask where the gateway resolves the IoT Hub hostname, which firewall permits the outbound connection, whether an outbound proxy sits in the path and who changes a rule when a new gateway model needs a different connection method. Those questions sound basic until a commissioning team arrives on site and discovers the device can reach a local DNS server but can't resolve the Azure endpoint. Suddenly, the MQTT client looks broken even though the problem lives somewhere else. Certificate trust adds another layer. The gateway needs to verify it's really talking to the Azure endpoint, not something pretending to be it. That means a trusted route certificate chain and a current trust store on a modern OS that happens through normal updates. But on an older industrial gateway with a locked down image, it may need explicit maintenance. You need a plan for that before the first certificate chain changes at an inconvenient time. If you use X.5.09 client certificates, protect the private key like you would a physical key to a control cabinet. Don't leave it in a plain config file that any local admin contractor or backup process can copy. Use the hardware and OS controls available on the gateway and define who can recover or replace that credential when hardware fails. SAS credentials need similar care. A SAS token has an expiry time so the client needs a way to renew before it runs out. If the gateway stores a long-lived secret to create tokens, that secret becomes a high-risk item. If it receives short-lived tokens from another service, then that service becomes part of the recovery path. There isn't one answer for every plant. There's only the need to make the dependency visible. Least access matters here too. A gateway should authenticate as its own device identity and receive only the rights it needs. Don't use an owner-level cloud credential just because it was easier during proof of concept. A production gateway doesn't need permission to create devices, read every identity or alter-rooting rules. Give the gateway permission to do its job and nothing beyond it. Security controls also need to respect how factories change. You can't casually update certificate stores, proxy settings, or gateway software during an active production run just because a cloud team wants to close a ticket. Plant teams need tested changes, rollback plans, approved windows, and a clear view of what happens if the gateway doesn't reconnect. This isn't resistance to security as its how security survives contact with production. Here's a useful test. If a certificate expires, a DNS record changes or a firewall policy blocks the route, can the line keep operating safely? It should. Cloud telemetry may pause and the gateway may buffer data, but the machine control path must stay local and independent. Once the network path and TLS trust are in place, the client still needs to speak to IoT Hub in exactly the form IoT Hub expects. The MQTT connection to IoT Hub. So you've got the network path sorted. Now the gateway has to establish an MQTT connection that Azure IoT Hub will actually accept, which is where generic MQTT knowledge runs into a service with very specific rules. The client ID must match the IoT Hub device ID exactly, so if you registered your gateway as PlantAlignedO2Gateway01, that's the value the MQTT client uses to identify itself. That link matters because IoT Hub uses the device identity for access control, connection tracking, and message context all at once. You can't treat the client ID like a friendly label you pick for convenience. In a local MQTT broker, you can choose almost any client ID you want, but IoT Hub ties it directly to a registered identity. That means if a gateway starts with the wrong ID or a copied configuration points to gateway machines at the same ID, the connection behavior gets confusing fast, and it's hard to troubleshoot from the shop floor. The gateway also needs the IoT Hub hostname, which in the standard endpoint model ends with Azure devices. Net, the MQTT username follows a specific IoT Hub format that includes the hostname, the device ID, and an API version. If you're connecting without an Azure IoT device SDK, those details aren't optional formatting trivia. They're part of the request IoT Hub users to understand who's connecting. Now authentication completes the connection. With shared access signature authentication, the client puts a valid SAS token in the MQTT password field. With X-Miver 9 authentication, the client proves possession of its certificate and private key during the TLS handshake. So it doesn't use a SAS token password the same way. Most teams should use the Azure IoT device SDK where it fits their gateway environment. The SDK handles protocol details, renews tokens where configured, and gives you established patterns for reconnecting and for IoT Hub features beyond plain telemetry. But some industrial gateways run a small client runtime, a vendor platform, or an existing MQTT library that makes a direct MQTT connection more practical, and that can work. It just shifts more responsibility onto your engineering team. A direct client needs to build the username correctly, manage the token lifetime if using SAS authentication, validate the server certificate, choose the supported MQTT version, and handle connection failures in a controlled way. Miss one small protocol detail and you get a connection that fails with little more than a dropped socket to explain it. IoT Hub device MQTT support uses MQTT version 3.1. One, so if your gateway defaults to MQTT version 5, you need an explicit configuration change. That matters because MQTT 5 adds behaviors a client developer might expect, while IoT Hub follows a defined 3.1, one device connection model. The connection also includes a KeeperLive setting, which lets the client and IoT Hub notice when an otherwise quiet connection has dropped. The gateway picks a KeeperLive interval, then sends MQTT ping messages when normal traffic hasn't crossed the connection, and IoT Hub applies its own timeout based on that client setting. Choose the interval with your plant network in mind. A very short interval adds more traffic and can cause needless disconnects on a slow or busy link, while a very long interval means it takes longer to detect that the path disappeared. There's no universal number, tested against the gateway runtime, the network path, and how quickly you need to know whether cloud telemetry is still flowing. Session settings deserve the same attention. MQTT 3.1.1 has a clean session flag, and when the client connects with CleanSession set to zero, it asks the service to keep session state across reconnects. Azure IoT Device SDK is normally used that persistent session approach, and if the gateway subscribes to support a device facing topics, a persistent session can help it resume after a temporary break. A clean session starts fresh, which may work fine for a simple telemetry only gateway, but it changes what the device receives after it reconnects, so don't set session flags just because they happen to be the default in a generic MQTT client. Decide based on what messages that gateway needs to receive and how it should recover. Reconnect behavior needs deliberate design as well. When a connection fails, the client shouldn't hammer the endpoint in a tight loop. It should wait, use increasing delays, add a random element, and keep doing its local job while the cloud connection recovers. The gateway also needs to renew its SAS token or certificate path without treating an expired credential as a temporary network fault forever. One active MQTT connection exists for each IoT Hub device identity, so when a second client connects under the same device ID, IoT Hub closes the earlier connection. That can happen when someone tests from a laptop using production credentials when a virtual gateway restarts before the old process has ended, or when a cloned gateway image reaches the network. The replacement connection may succeed, which misleads the person testing it, while the real gateway loses its session. Treat a forced disconnect as a clue, check for device identity reuse before blaming the network because the issue may be two clients claiming the same place in the fleet. Once the gateway has authenticated, established its session, and settled into normal, keep-alive behavior, it can publish telemetry. The next question is much more concrete, which MQTT topic does IoT Hub expect that message to use? The device to cloud topics, the first address in the pipeline. When the gateway sends telemetry to Azure IoT Hub, it can't publish to just any topic it makes up. IoT Hub expects a fixed device to cloud topic pattern that includes the identity of the connected device. For a device identity called PlantAlign 02 Gateway 01, the gateway publishes telemetry to a path like this, devices, then the device ID, then messages, then events. The exact syntax matters to the MQTT client, but the design point matters more. IoT Hub knows which registered device connection sent the message and the topic confirms it's going device to cloud. That topic describes the cloud facing sender, not every physical asset behind the gateway. Our line 02 Gateway may collect data from a conveyor motor, a filler, a caper, and an inspection station, but it can still publish all of that telemetry through its one gateway identity. In that case, the device ID in the MQTT topic identifies the gateway, while the message itself needs to name the source asset. So keep those two identities separate. The connection identity answers which managed gateway authenticated to IoT Hub, the source asset identity answers, which machine, sensor, or PLC produced this reading. When those get mixed together, people later assume IoT Hub authenticated the motor itself when it only authenticated the gateway that carried the message. That distinction becomes very practical during fault finding, say the gateway sends a high temperature reading for conveyor motor A. A cloud operator can verify that the line 02 gateway sent it and a maintenance engineer then needs the asset reference in the message and the gateway's source mapping to trace it back to the correct PLC tag and physical motor. The MQTT topic alone can't carry that chain of evidence. IoT Hub also lets a device attach message properties through the topic after the events path, which arrive as URL encoded name and value pairs. A gateway might attach a property, like message type equals telemetry or plant equals plant A, where that property helps an IoT Hub routing rule distinguish one message type from another without opening and passing every message body. Think of these properties as labels on the message envelope. They work well for small pieces of information that the transport and routing layer need to inspect. For example, an application property could mark a message as an alarm candidate, a raw reading, or an equipment state event. Later, IoT Hub can apply rules based on that property and route each class of message to a different destination. But be disciplined about what goes there. A property that drives routing belongs close to the envelope while detailed machine events, readings or context that analytics needs belongs in the payload. If teams put the same business field in both places, they eventually disagree. One path changes a value, the other doesn't and people waste time asking which copy is correct. Use a clear rule. Put routing metadata and message properties, put the actual telemetry and its source details in the body and keep a field in one place unless you have a specific reason to duplicate it. The MQTT topic path can also carry system related values like content type and content encoding using the documented property syntax. That may sound like a small technical detail, but it affects how downstream services interpret the data. A message that contains JSON needs to declare that it contains JSON rather than leaving the destination to infer it from a few curly brackets. We'll get deeper into the payload contract shortly, but for now, remember that the topic helps IoT Hub accept the message and properties help classify it. Neither one replaces the actual message structure. Topic names need a naming discipline even within these fixed IoT Hub patterns. The device ID appears in the path, so device ID should stay stable, readable and controlled. Don't build them from temporary line names, current work orders, or a product that will disappear next quarter. The path must keep working while the factory changes around it. A gateway may move from line two to line four during a rebuild and you can update its asset relationship in the factory model, but you shouldn't need to rename a cloud device identity and rewrite every downstream dependency just because someone moved the cabinet. This is where teams sometimes try to put business meaning into the transport path by adding plant, line, product order, shift and asset hierarchy into names because it seems convenient for filtering. Then an order changes, a shift crosses midnight or a machine serves another line and the identity path becomes wrong. But changing it creates a whole new set of problems. MQTT topics move messages, they don't model the factory. Use the topic IoT Hub expects, let the authenticated gateway identity tell you which connection sent the data, put a stable source asset reference in the message body and keep changing production context in the systems that manage it, where it can change without rewriting the transport path. Once that message reaches IoT Hub, the payload decides whether anyone downstream can understand it. Telemetry payloads need a contract. Here's a problem I see all the time. A message reaches IoT Hub just fine but ends up completely useless to the people who need it. The gateway connects, MQTT accepts the publish and the route might even deliver it to storage or a stream, but if nobody agrees on what the payload means or how it's shaped every downstream team decodes the same message differently and that gets expensive fast, take a conveyor motor. A useful telemetry payload needs a stable source asset ID, the measured value, the unit, the source timestamp, and a quality state along with a correlation value so you can connect readings from the same collection cycle. Now say the message contains a source asset called conveyor motor A, a temperature of 82.1, a unit of degrees Celsius, and a quality state of good. A receiver can interpret that number without hunting through gateway documentation or guessing whether 82 means Celsius or Fahrenheit. The message needs to say what it means. Jason is a practical starting point for telemetry payloads because it's readable, widely supported, and flexible enough for an early industrial data contract. But that flexibility needs boundaries. Jason lets you send almost any shape of data, which means teams can quietly change a field name or data type and break analytics without noticing for days. So treat the payload like an interface between systems. A simple structure includes an asset identifier, an event type, a timestamp, and a list of measurements, each with a name, a numeric value, a unit, and a quality code. You don't need every possible field in every message, but you do need rules for when a field appears, what type it uses, and what an absent value means. An absent temperature can mean several things. The sensor may not support it, the sensor may have failed, the gateway may have lost communication, or a developer simply removed the field in a firmware update. Those are not the same condition, so don't turn them all into a blank value and hope an analyst can work it out later. Quality needs the same care. A sensor can report a value inside a sensible range and still be untrustworthy. The PLC may flag the input as bad, the gateway may have read an old cashed value after losing contact, or a calculation may derive from incomplete inputs. If the pipeline strips out that quality information, the dashboard gains a neat number, and loses the warning that should have traveled with it. That's how false confidence enters a production report. Units are another quiet failure point. One gateway report sells you as well, another reports Fahrenheit, and a current signal might appear in amps, milliamps, or a raw controller count. You can normalize at the edge if that's part of your design, but record the rule and apply it consistently. A field called temperature, with no stated unit, isn't a data contract. It's an invitation to make assumptions. Naming rules matter just as much. Decide whether the payload uses a stable technical tag ID, a stable asset ID, a readable measurement name, or a combination. Don't depend only on a human readable label because labels change, and don't depend only on a PLC address because that moves when a controls engineer revises a program. Keep both forms where they support traceability, but give each one a clear job. Version the contract from the beginning. That doesn't require a large governance committee for one pilot line. It just means the message declares which schema version it follows and changes happen in a controlled way. If version 2 replaces a number with an object, renames a field or changes a unit, consumers need time to accept both versions or migrate safely because firmware and analytics rarely change on the same day. IoT Hub also has a device to cloud message size limit of 256 kilobytes, and that applies to both batches and individual sends. Plenty for normal telemetry, but if a gateway regularly approaches it, pause and ask what it's trying to send. Large diagnostic files, images and raw waveform captures usually need a different transfer pattern with the telemetry message carrying a reference rather than forcing the whole file through a small event channel. Set the content type and encoding clearly too, when the body contains JSON, identify it as JSON and use UTF-8 encoding so IoT Hub and downstream endpoints handle it as structured text, instead of treating it as unknown binary data that ends up as base 64 in storage. A payload contract sounds less exciting than a dashboard, but it decides whether the dashboard can be trusted, and even a well-formed payload can tell the wrong story if its timestamp doesn't mean what everyone thinks it means. Time is a data model problem. A timestamp looks simple until someone tries to explain why a line stopped. That's when you discover a message can carry several different times, each answering a different question. Take our conveyor motor again. The PLC detects a high temperature at 10 minutes past 2, the gateway reads it a fraction later, packages it with other readings, and sends it to Azure, where IoT Hub receives it at another time. And if the connection dropped, the gateway might not send it until 20 minutes later. Which time belongs on the dashboard? For an engineer understanding machine behavior, source event time matters most. That's when the PLC or sensor observed the reading. For someone supporting the Cloud Pipeline, IoT Hub in Qtime matters too because it tells you when Azure received the message and the gap between those two times can expose buffering, network delay, or a gateway that stopped forwarding. Don't override one with the other. Keep the source event time in the payload or a defined message property and let IoT Hub add its own receive time as trusted pipeline metadata. Then downstream users can ask two separate questions. When did this happen on the line? And when did the Cloud learn about it? Those questions often lead to different actions. Imagine the line lost its outbound network connection during a shift. The gateway buffers motor readings and fault events locally, then reconnects later and sends the backlog. If your report sorts only by IoT Hub received time, it can show a burst of temperature alarms long after the line returned to normal operation. And a maintenance lead may think the fault just started. With source event time preserved, the report can place those readings in their actual production sequence and mark them as later rivals, so nobody mistakes delayed evidence for a current alarm. Real time visibility needs that honesty. A number arriving now doesn't always describe the factory now. Use coordinated universal time, UTC as the shared storage time where you can. It avoids the awkward hour when local clocks shift for daylight saving time and it lets data from several plants sit on one timeline without each system guessing what local time another segment. But local time still matters to operations. A shift starts at a local time, a plant change over belongs to a local production calendar and a supervisor asks what happened in the night shift. Not what happened between two UTC values. The answer isn't to store ambiguous local time stamps as truth, store UTC, retain the site context, and apply the plant calendar when you analyze the event. That sounds like a small modeling decision, but it prevents some very strange reports twice a year. Clock quality needs attention at every layer. The PLC may use a controller clock, the gateway has its own system clock, and Azure records the receive time. If the PLC clock drifts by several minutes, its readings can still look perfectly ordered while sitting in the wrong place against MES events, maintenance records and shift boundaries. You need to know which clock you trust for which purpose. In practical terms, maintain time synchronization for gateways and controllers where equipment and plant rules allow it, monitor drift rather than assuming it never happens, and record the origin of important time stamps. Especially when a gateway derives an event from multiple source signals. A sequence number can help too. If a gateway sends readings from one source in known order, a sequence value gives downstream processing another clue when time stamps collide, or events arrive late. It won't solve every distributed systems problem, but it can show that message 418 arrived after message 420 because the network recovered out of order. Time order also has limits. You can preserve a sensible sequence from one gateway and one asset, but you can't assume two independent publishers will reach the cloud in the same order their machines generated events because network paths differ, buffers behave differently, and clocks drift independently. So avoid building a production story on arrival order alone. A mature pipeline stores the event time, the cloud receive time, source context, and where needed a sequence clue. Then the report can separate current conditions from late data and separate a machine event from the moment an analytics platform first noticed it. Once time is explicit, the next decision becomes unavoidable. What delivery behavior can downstream systems actually trust when messages fail, retry, or arrive more than once? QoS, retries, and duplicates. Choose the failure mode. Here's the thing about MQTT. Once that message leaves your gateway, you have to decide what kind of failure you can live with. That's what quality of service Kyrgyz Kyrgyz comes down to. You're basically choosing between a message that might vanish or one that might show up twice. QoS zero is at most once delivery. Your client fires off the message without waiting for IoT Hub to confirm it got there and if the network drops at the wrong moment, that message disappears. The tradeoff is less overhead on the client and less traffic on the wire. That can work for signals like frequent temperature samples from a motor. The next reading arrives in seconds. So losing one point probably won't change your trend or maintenance decision. Sending every sample with full delivery guarantees might cost more than its worth. So don't pick QoS zero just because it sounds efficient. Only use it when a missing message is an acceptable gap. State transitions usually need a different approach. When a gateway detects a line fault or records the start of a batch that needs tracing later, you probably want QoS one. That means at least one's delivery. The sender waits for an act and if the act never comes, it sends the message again. It reduces the chance of a silent loss, but it doesn't guarantee the receiver sees exactly one copy. Picture a gateway publishing a fault event, IoT Hub receiving it, but the acknowledgement never returns because the connection drops. The gateway can't know if the event arrived, so it reconnects and sends it again. Now Downstream gets two messages describing one fault. Neither the gateway nor IoT Hub actually failed. The delivery contract simply allowed that outcome. That's why consumers need idempotent behavior, processing the same logical event multiple times without creating a different business result. If a maintenance workflow opens a ticket for a high temperature alarm, a duplicate should update the existing event, not open a second ticket just because the message traveled twice. Give the message a way to support that decision. A message id can identify a logical event, along with a source event time, source asset id and sequence value. The exact method depends on the event type, but the consumer needs enough evidence to tell a retry, apart from a new occurrence. Avoid weak duplicate rules like ignore anything with the same temperature. A motor can report the same value several times and each sample may be legitimate. Did you application should identify a repeated message or source event, not erase normal data just because two readings match? MQTT also defines QOS2, exactly once delivery, through a more involved exchange between client and broker. Azure IoT Hub doesn't support QOS2 for device telemetry. If a device publishes with QOS2, IoT Hub closes the connection, so configure the client intentionally. QOS0 and QOS1 belong in the IoT Hub MQTT design, but QOS2 does not. Now here's a broader point, delivery guarantees don't travel cleanly through every part of a pipeline. A gateway may publish with QOS1, but a downstream service can still fail while processing the event. A storage process might write the raw message while an alert process retries later. Each stage needs its own handling for retries duplicates and failures. So don't claim exactly once business processing, just because an MQTT setting sounds reassuring. Classify each signal before picking the behavior. A high-rate sample may tolerate gaps, a fault event needs retry and duplicate safe handling, and a configuration update needs confirmation, the device applied it. That's a different pattern. The delivery choice should match the cost of absence, the cost of repetition, and the action someone takes from that signal. With that choice clear, the message enters IoT Hub, receiving it is only part of the job. The service also adds its own trusted connection context, keeps telemetry available for a limited period, and prepares it for the downstream paths that need it. What IoT Hub receives and stamps onto the message, when IoT Hub accepts a device to cloud message, it doesn't just take a JSON body from a gateway, it receives that body through an authenticated device connection, then adds system information that downstream consumers can use without trusting what the gateway wrote about itself. That gives the pipeline a chain of custody. The normal intake point is IoT Hub's built-in device to cloud endpoint called messages events. Multiple readers can process the telemetry for their own purposes within the service's limits and your design. Our line-to-gateway sends a motor temperature event. IoT Hub receives it through the authenticated connection and stamps the message with the device identity from that connection. Including the connection device ID, authentication generation ID and authentication method info. Those fields matter because they come from IoT Hub not from the payload. A gateway could write something like device id, plant a line 02 gateway 01 into its JSON. That's fine as a business field, but a downstream service shouldn't treat it as proof of who connected. A payload can claim anything, but the platform connection context records what IoT Hub actually accepted. There are two types of message metadata to keep separate, system properties and application properties. System properties have defined meanings in IoT Hub. Like the time IoT Hub received the message, connection identity, content type, content encoding and optionally a message ID or correlation ID. Application properties are labels your application ads. String-based fields that IoT Hub doesn't rewrite, a gateway might add an event type property with value telemetry or alarm candidate, so downstream code can classify messages without passing the body, use each for its own job, system properties record transport and service facts. Application properties carry small controlled labels for filtering or handling. The JSON body carries the equipment data and source context that gives the event meaning. Mixing those jobs creates confusion. If a developer puts a cloud receive time in the JSON body and another service trusted as if it came from IoT Hub, the data lineage gets muddy. Keep cloud generated facts separate from device generated facts. IoT Hub retains device to cloud messages at the built-in endpoint for up to seven days depending on your configuration and tier. That gives consumer services a window to read telemetry after a failure or restart, but it doesn't turn IoT Hub into your long term historian. If you need months or years of raw evidence, you need a separate storage path. Keep that in mind. The next architecture decision is about where each kind of telemetry should go. One more behavior shapes downstream processing. IoT Hub Partitions, device to cloud messages by originating device ID. It doesn't create one universal factory timeline. Our line to gateway may preserve its own sequence more predictably than events from unrelated gateways but separate connections can still arrive and recover at different times. Source event time and identity context still matter when combining data across machines. Partitioning also affects how you build consumers. A processor needs to read across partitions, handle its own checkpoints and not assume one worker sees the entire plant in order. If you need a condition for one gateway or asset, partition-aware processing helps. For a cross-line view, the logic must account for independent streams and late data. So IoT Hub receives a device message, adds trusted connection facts and keeps it briefly in a partitioned event path. That's your controlled intake point. One intake stream rarely has one consumer. Maintenance alerts, raw evidence, live analytics and downstream business processes all need different handling. That's where message routing starts to shape the pipeline. Message routing turns telemetry into separate data paths. Once telemetry reaches IoT Hub, the real question shifts from where to send it to who needs it, how fast and what happens if it doesn't arrive. Those answers define the routes. Take the same motor temperature event from line two. One copy lands in a raw history store so engineers can pull the original evidence months later. Another message, but only when the temperature crosses a defined threshold, needs to reach a near real-time process that decides whether maintenance should act. A third-class feeds operational analysis. Those aren't the same workloads, so they shouldn't share one vague destination and expect a dashboard to sort it out. IoT Hub message routing lets you define rules that inspect a message and send matching ones to custom endpoints. A route check system properties like the authenticated device ID or content type and it can also use application properties the gateway attached for a known purpose. The routing should depend on an explicit contract. For example, the gateway might tag a message with an application property like event type. Routine telemetry carries event type equals telemetry. A state transition carries event type equals machine state. A message that needs faster review carries event type equals alarm candidate. IoT Hub evaluates those labels and sends each message class down the path build for it, but don't use routing as a shortcut for understanding the data. A route can filter on properties and under the right conditions query adjacent body, but that doesn't mean every rule should bury business logic in a cloud expression. If the definition of an alarm depends on machine mode, product limits, maintenance status and a rolling temperature pattern that belongs in a named processing component with tests and ownership. Routing should separate traffic, not become an invisible production rules engine. Start route design with the consumer and its retention need. Roe evidence needs durable storage. A stream processor needs an event endpoint that supports its read pattern and a business workflow may need a queue or topic with its own delivery behavior. The destination follows the work, not the other way around. That sounds obvious, but many early deployments root everything to one endpoint because it's quick to configure. Then the analytics team, maintenance team and application team all consume the same undifferentiated stream. Each writes its own filters, handles mail form messages differently and eventually has a different count for the same event. The pipeline becomes a debate club. A route also needs a clear rule for messages that don't match anything. IoT Hub provides a fallback route which can send unmatched messages to the built-in endpoint. That protects telemetry from disappearing during a routing change, but it can also hide a mistake. If a new message type misses its intended route and quietly lands in the fallback stream, nobody may notice until a consumer asks why data vanished from its normal destination. Treat fallback as a safety net, not the main architecture. Monitor it, know what arrives there and why. If the fallback starts carrying a growing share of production telemetry, the root rules or message contract need attention. An unmatched message isn't just an operational detail. It might mean a gateway published, an unexpected property, a firmware update, changed a value or a root rule no longer matches the data it was meant to handle. Destination health matters just as much. An endpoint can accept messages normally, slow down, become unavailable, or fail after retries. IoT Hub exposes endpoint health states and its routing metrics help you spot delays and delivery problems. Somebody needs to own that monitoring, not just cloud operations. Because an unhealthy route can affect a maintenance process or an operations report without always stopping the gateway from publishing successfully. From the plan team's view, the data left the gateway, from the cloud team's view, IoT Hub accepted it, yet the intended consumer may still receive nothing. That gap needs an agreed response path. Test routes, like you test any other production dependency, send known message types and confirm each reaches the correct endpoint, send a message with a missing property, change an endpoint permission in a non-production environment, and observe the failure behavior. Then check whether alerts identify the route and destination clearly enough for someone to act. A streaming consumer needs data while it still has operational relevance, whether it detects a pattern, updates a live operational view, triggers an investigation workflow, or feeds a custom service that needs a steady event flow. Azure Event Hubs fits that kind of work. IoT Hub can route selected device to cloud messages to an Event Hubs endpoint where one or more downstream processes read the stream at their own pace. Think about line two again. The gateway sends routine motor readings, state transitions and alarm candidates, and a stream processor reads the alarm candidates as they arrive, checks the source event time and quality, then applies the rules that decide whether the maintenance team needs a notification. The processor doesn't need every other application to stop while it does that. Event Hubs uses consumer groups to separate readers. A consumer group gives each downstream application its own view of the event stream with its own read position and check pointing. One group may run alert logic, another may feed an operational application, and a third may support diagnostics during an incident. Those consumers read the same incoming events without IoT Hub needing to send separate copies. That separation helps, but it doesn't remove responsibility. Every consumer needs to track where it has processed up to, recover after a restart, and handle events it sees again after a failure. Check pointing records progress usually at a partition level, so the processor resumes from a known point rather than guessing. If the service fails halfway through a batch, it may read some events again after starting. That's normal event processing behavior, but the application still needs duplicate safe handling where the output has business consequences. This is where people often confuse a fast event stream with a real time decision system. Event Hubs can move a high volume of events through a cloud stream, but it doesn't know whether a motor runs normally, whether the line is in a planned stop, or whether an operator already responded to the alarm. It carries the event and your processing logic needs the context. A custom service might enrich the event with asset details from another source. It might check the current operating mode or hold an alarm candidate for a short period and look for repeated high readings instead of reacting to one spike. That work belongs in visible code or a defined stream processing rule set where someone can test and change it. Don't bury it in a dashboard refresh. Event Hubs also supports replay within its configured retention period. If you change the processing logic, discover a defect, or need to investigate an incident, a consumer can read earlier events again as long as they remain in the stream. That helps teams recover from a processing error without asking a gateway to recreate history it no longer holds. Replay isn't the same as permanent history. It gives operational consumers a time window to catch up or rerun logic, but it doesn't replace a deliberate long term data design, partitions shape the read model here, just as they do at the IoT hub intake. Events distribute across partitions and a consumer reads each partition through its assigned processing work. You can preserve sensible order within a partition, but only where the publishing and partitioning model puts related events together. So don't assume an event hub stream creates a perfect sequence across the factory. If the conveyor gateway and the filler gateway published independently, their events may sit in different partitions. A processor can compare their source timestamps and relationship context, but it shouldn't use a rival order as proof that one physical event happened before another. That matters when you build alerts. A downstream staff condition may appear in the stream before the upstream conveyor fault that caused it. The event stream didn't invent the problem. The system simply received independent messages through an asynchronous path. Designed the logic to cope with that, use event hubs when a consumer needs to process events continuously, with enough throughput for your traffic and enough retention to recover from normal service failures. It works well for stream processing, custom operational services, and a alert evaluation that needs more than a static report, but a streaming endpoint isn't where you keep the factory's raw memory forever. Some telemetry needs a cheaper, durable place where it can remain available for later-ordered, troubleshooting, and reprocessing. Storage for raw history and reprocessing. Here's the reality. A stream processor can react to a message while it still matters, but sooner or later, someone needs to go back. Think about the scenarios that come up every day. A maintenance engineer needs original telemetry from the week before a motor fault, a data engineer needs to rerun a calculation after fixing a bug, and auditor wants proof of what the gateway actually sent, not some later interpretation from an application. Later, you can build clean tables, normalized measurements, or business events from that data, but don't let those later versions become the only record. A cleaned data set answers a current reporting need, but raw history lets you check whether that data set got the event right. Storage routing supports Apache Avro and JSON output. A useful storage path often includes time-based folders because time is the common starting point for investigation. Those layers should link back to each other. Analytics can organize data, but it doesn't invent machine meaning. So when routing IoT Hub telemetry into fabric, preserve the fields that let you trace back. From telemetry to OE without fiction, let's start with something practical. A motor temperature trend can show rising friction, poor cooling, a loading problem, or a sensor issue, but it can't tell you the OE of the line. I see that leap happen all the time. A few tags hit a dashboard, a gauge pops up, and suddenly the screen claims it's showing OE. The calculation might look clean, but the factory meaning behind it is usually missing. OE combines three things, availability, performance, and quality. Availability asks if the equipment could run when we expected it to. Performance asks if it ran at the expected rate. Quality asks if the output met the standard. Those words sound simple on paper, but each one needs a definition that production, maintenance, quality, and finance can all live with. Start with availability. A conveyor motor temperature reading won't tell you whether the line actually stopped and even a motor fault signal might not reveal the real cause. Was it another machine holding the line or a planned cleaning period? You need a state model. For a packaging line, that model might distinguish six states, running, blocked, starved, faulted, plan stop, and unknown. Running means the asset is doing its intended work. When it can't pass material downstream, that's blocked. Starved is the opposite, waiting for upstream material. A defined fault condition that stops normal operation, that's faulted, plan stop covers approved breaks and changeovers. Unknown simply means the system doesn't have enough trust where the evidence to classify the state. Unknown is not a failure of reporting. It's an honest state. Without that honest state, many systems label every idle minute as downtime, which inflates losses, creates arguments with operators, and sends maintenance teams chasing problems that may not exist. A line can sit idle for many reasons. No order, no packaging material, no operator release, an upstream shortage or a planned product change, and none of those automatically belongs in equipment downtime. Performance needs the same discipline. A counter might show finished units, but that doesn't tell you if the line ran slowly, unless you know the expected rate for the current product, pack format, operating mode, and agreed speed basis. A filler running at one speed on a small bottle might be normal while the same speed on a different product signals a loss. The expected rate belongs to the production definition. It's not just a number copied into a report. Quality brings in another source of truth. The machine may count rejects. A vision system may identify inspection failures, and the MES may record quality decisions, rework, or batch disposition. If those sources don't use the same scope and timing, the quality part of OEE becomes a guess dressed up as a percentage. Think about our conveyor motor. Its temperature rises during a product run, and the IoT data can show the rise, its duration, and whether the signal quality remained good. To decide whether it affected OEE, you need more evidence. Whether the line entered a faulted state, output stopped, the event fell inside planned production time. Downstream counters, slowed, or any product failed inspection. That is a production question, not a sensor question. The right model combines telemetry with machine state, production counters, MES execution status, product data, and the planned schedule. But only where that schedule changes the interpretation. The incoming event needs to fit into the period when a specific order ran with the product configuration active at that time, and the asset states around it. Only then can you begin to describe a loss with some confidence. This doesn't mean every OEE calculation needs a massive cloud project. Start small, agree definitions for one line, decide what counts as planned time, which signals prove running, which events create faulted time, and who can correct or classify an unknown period. Then compare the calculated result with the people who ran that shift. If the dashboard disagrees with the operators every day, it isn't exposing the factory. It's producing its own version of it. Telemetry provides fast evidence from the equipment, but OEE also needs execution data, product context, and business rules that sit elsewhere in the factory. That means the next question isn't how to collect more sensor tags. It's how ERP, MES, and IoT each describe different parts of the same production event, and ERP, MES, and IoT each know different parts of the factory. Here's the real challenge. Once you start asking why a line lost output, one system rarely has the whole answer. ERP, MES, and IoT each record part of the factory often had different speeds and for different reasons. That isn't a defect. It's how the system's developed. Your ERP system holds the commercial and planning side of production, customer demand, sales orders, material requirements, planned production orders, inventory positions, and delivery promises. It can tell you what the business expects the plan to produce and often what materials should support that work. But ERP doesn't normally know that a conveyor motor rose above its normal temperature at 2.10 in the afternoon. It might know a production order should run online to that day, but it won't know whether the line was blocked, starved, or stopped at that exact moment. The MES sits closer to execution. Where it's been adopted deeply, it can record what operators and machines report during production. Work order progress, material consumption, genealogy, labor activity, quality checks, batch events, production status. MES often provides the bridge between a planned order and the work performed on the floor. Still, MES doesn't automatically capture every fast-changing machine signal. It may receive a machine state from a PLC, or production counts or alarm events, but a high-frequency temperature trend, current draw, vibration signal, or sub-second state change often lives outside its normal scope, or arrives only after an edge layer filters and interprets it. Then there is IoT telemetry. IoT captures what equipment and sensors reported operational speed. Motor temperature, pressure readings, machine modes, countervalues, gateway health signals. That makes it useful for real-time visibility and close to the process evidence, but IoT doesn't know the business commitment on its own. At 2.10pm UTC, a gateway can report a filo-stop, but without MES or ERP context, it can't tell you which work order the stop affected, whether the order had already completed, whether the planned plan to change over, or whether another line could take over the work. The sensor reports what it sensed. It doesn't carry the production plan in its pocket. This is where teams create unnecessary conflict between systems. Somebody asks which platform owns the truth and the answer becomes ERP, or MES, or the IoT platform, as if one database must win. That isn't the right question. Each system should own the facts it can create and maintain with authority. ERP owns planning and commercial commitments, MES owns execution records and production workflow, where the plant uses it, and IoT owns machine and sensor observations with the data lineage that explains how those observations traveled from source to cloud. A useful production view joins those facts. It doesn't force them into one system and pretend the original source no longer matters. For that to work, identifiers need discipline. The asset ID in a telemetry message needs to map to the asset known by maintenance, MES, and the factory model. The production order reference needs to mean the same order in both ERP and MES. Product codes, line codes, plant codes, they all need stable definitions, even when different systems use different internal formats. Without that mapping, integration becomes pattern matching. Picture a planner asking why order 4837 finished late. ERP knows the due date and planned quantity. MES may show that the order ran online too during a defined window, and IoT can show that the conveyor feeding the filler reported a fault condition during part of that window. Now the systems can connect the dots between IT and OT. That doesn't prove the motor fault caused the late order, maybe the line kept running, or a material shortage caused the loss. The point is that the investigation starts with linked evidence, not separate screens, and relying on someone's memory from the night shift. You also don't need to copy every ERP record into a telemetry message or push every sensor sample into MES, that creates duplicate data, more interfaces, and more chances for systems to disagree. Bring context across system boundaries only when it changes a decision, calculation, or traceability need. Keep the source facts where they belong, then connect them through stable references, so the next problem becomes more precise. A telemetry message can identify conveyor motor A, and MES can identify a production order, but the architecture still needs to explain how that motor relates to the conveyor, the line, the process step, and the work currently in progress. Digital twins and knowledge graphs add relationships. A telemetry message can name a motor, and an MES record can name an order, but the architecture still needs a way to say how they connect without relying on someone who just happens to know the line. That's where a digital twin or a knowledge graph becomes useful. A digital twin, in this context, is a digital model of an asset, plus the facts that describe its current or known state. It doesn't need to copy every temperature sample, every PLC register, or every packet that crossed the network. In fact, trying to copy all of that usually creates a noisy model nobody can maintain. The twin should describe the asset in a way that supports decisions. For example, conveyor motor A can have an asset identity, motor type, location, health state, and a relationship to the conveyor it drives, which belongs to a packaging line that includes a filler, a labeler, and an inspection station. Those relationships let a system move from one signal to the equipment around it. The temperature reading stays telemetry while the twin provides the frame. A knowledge graph takes the relationship idea further by linking the things that matter to the factory and keeping those links explicit. Assets, lines, process steps, products, work orders, maintenance plans, sensor tags, and sometimes skills or roles when they affect what happens next. Think about the motor event we followed through the pipeline, the gateway reports that conveyor motor A crossed a temperature threshold. On its own, that tells you a component may need attention. But the graph can state that this motor drives conveyor C12, which feeds filler F02, which currently supports a packaging operation for a specific product order, and now the event has an operational path. You can ask which downstream equipment may lose material if the conveyor stops, which order currently depends on the line. Whether the line runs a product with tighter temperature or speed limits, and which maintenance procedure, spare part, or qualified role links to that motor. But none of those answers should live inside an MQTT topic name. A topic can root a message and help identify a message class, but it cannot safely become the place where you encode every fact about the plant, current order, process root, and maintenance history. Once business meaning changes, topic paths turn into brittle strings that gateways and consumers need to decode the same way, and that's too much responsibility for a transport address. Keep the telemetry pipeline focused on moving verified events and put the asset and process relationships in a context service or model that can change without forcing a firmware update across hundreds of gateways. The telemetry record then carries stable references like an asset ID and source tag ID while the twin or graph resolves what those references mean in the current factory structure. This also helps when the factory changes. A conveyor can move to another line, a product family can shift to a different filler or an old motor can be replaced, but keep the same functional role. If the only relationship sits in a hard-coded topic path or analytics query, every change becomes a hunt through integrations. With an explicit model, you update the relationship where it belongs. There's a distinction here, a digital twin and a knowledge graph don't replace ERP, MES or the IoT platform, and they don't become a new owner of every operational fact either. Instead, they provide a shared context layer that points back to the systems that own those facts. ERP still owns the production order, MES still owns execution status, the IoT path still owns the observed signal and its source lineage, and the graph links the references to give applications away to reason across them. That's a more practical form of OTI-T convergence, then copying every database into one giant table and hoping the joints stay correct, it also gives you better questions when an event occurs. Instead of asking only which motor sent a high temperature, you can ask which process step this motor supports, what's running there now and who needs to assess the risk. The answer may still be to watch it and take no action and that's fine. Context doesn't force an alarm. It lets the system explain why an alarm does or doesn't deserve attention. Once that context sits beside the incoming telemetry, the dashboard can move beyond a list of red sensor values and show the part of the factory the event might actually affect. Dashboard design starts with a decision. Once the relationship model can explain what an event affects, the dashboard has a more useful job. It shouldn't try to display every signal the factory produces, but instead help a person make a decision with enough context to trust it. Start with the person and the decision, an operator may need to know whether the line is running within expected conditions right now, whether a fault needs a local check and whether the data itself is current. A maintenance lead may need to decide which asset deserves attention first along with the related condition trend and effect on production. A planner needs a different view, whether a disruption threatens an order, whether capacity changed and whether the schedule needs review. A plant manager may only need the broader pattern, which losses repeat, where they occur, and which issues need investment, rather than another shift-level workaround. One dashboard can't answer all those questions well. When a single report tries, it usually becomes a wall of gauges, colors, and filters that nobody touches after the first demonstration. Real-time visibility doesn't mean every number updates as fast as possible, but that the user can see the state needed for the decision, understand how current that state is, and reach supporting evidence when something looks wrong. Take the high-temperature event from ConveroMotor A. An operator view may show that the condition needs attention, the current machine state, and whether the signal arrived recently, but it doesn't need a six-month trend by default. The maintenance lead may open the same event and see the temperature history, quality state, asset relationship, and production context. While the planner doesn't need to diagnose the motor, they need the operational consequence. This is where the path through the architecture should stay visible. A person should be able to move from a line-level condition to the affected asset, then to the current or recent production context, and finally to the raw evidence behind the calculated state. That doesn't mean every user sees every technical field, but that the system can answer where a conclusion came from when someone needs to challenge it. Trust disappears quickly when a report can't do that. Suppose a dashboard reports that line-2 experienced a temperature-related risk during a run. A maintenance engineer should be able to check the source asset, event time, reading in-unit, quality flag, and whether data arrived late after a gateway buffer replayed it. If the condition resulted from a calculated rule, the rule should be clear enough to inspect, because a report that only shows a red status gives no one much to work with. Show uncertainty as part of the operational picture. If data is delayed, say it's delayed, and if a source stopped reporting, show the missing signal, rather than carrying forward, a last-known value as if nothing changed. If a reading arrived out of sequence or carries a bad quality state, make that available to the person who needs to decide whether the condition is real. That may feel less tidy than a green amber red display, but it's more honest. There's a difference between no alarm and we don't know, a dashboard that hides that difference can create false confidence at exactly the wrong time. An operator might think a machine is healthy when the gateway has lost contact, or a manager may compare shifts without realizing one shift includes delayed data that arrived after the report window. Data confidence belongs beside the results, not in a technical appendix nobody opens. Power BI and fabric reports fit naturally at this consumption layer. They can bring prepared operational data, production context, and governed business data into views that different roles can use. But the report isn't the system of record for machine state, root health, asset relationships, or production execution. It's where people consume the outcome of that work. That distinction keeps teams from trying to solve pipeline problems with more visuals. If timestamps are unclear, adding another chart won't repair them. And if an asset ID doesn't map to the MES context, a better slicer won't create that relationship. If the alert logic is weak, a Lifetyle simply lets people watch the weak logic update faster. Design the report around the next action who sees the condition, what they need to verify, who takes ownership if it needs escalation, and which evidence lets them decide without calling three people and opening four unrelated systems. The first serious test comes when the system tries to notify someone because an alert forces the architecture to state not only what it observed, but also what response it expects. Alerts, commands, and the boundary between observe and control. Alerts are where your telemetry pipeline stops being a data project and starts affecting someone's actual work. Start with a simple threshold. If conveyor motor A temperature exceeds a limit, you trigger an alert, but a single reading above a limit rarely gives enough evidence on its own. A sensor can spike, the machine might be in a non-production mode, or the reading arrives late after a connection glitch. A better alert rule takes more into account by checking whether the temperature stayed high for a defined period, whether it's rising faster than normal, whether the conveyor is underload or stopped for planned cleaning, whether the signal quality is good, and whether someone already opened an investigation for the same condition. That turns an alert from a noisy message into a decision aid. Now here's where it gets more interesting. Once the cloud detects a condition, can it tell the equipment to do something? As your IoT hub supports several device-facing patterns, but they solve different problems and shouldn't be mixed together. Cloud-to-device messages let a back-end application send a message to a specific device, and this works well for cute instructions. The device should receive when it connects. Like a request to upload diagnostic data or acknowledge a maintenance notice. The device processes the message based on its own logic. Device twins handle state synchronization through two property stores, desired properties written by the cloud, and reported properties written by the device. During an investigation, engineering might want the gateway to sample motor temperature every minute instead of every five. The desired property records that request, the gateway applies it when appropriate, and reports back what it actually runs. That distinction matters more than it might seem. A desired property is not proof that the equipment changes behavior. It's a requested state. The reported property gives the devices view of what actually happened, and even that needs sensible error handling if the gateway can't apply the setting. Direct methods use a request response pattern, where a cloud service calls a named action on a connected device and expects a response. For example, a maintenance application might ask a gateway to run a diagnostic check, collect a defined set of non-control data and return a status result. The device needs to be online for that interaction, and it needs a clear contract for time-outs, authorization, and how to respond when the action can't run. None of these patterns should become a shortcut around plant control systems. Don't send a cloud command to stop a conveyor, just because a dashboard noticed a temperature trend. Safety critical control loops don't belong on a path that goes through an Internet connection, a cloud message service, and a reporting layer. The latency, connection state, and failure modes have no place protecting people or equipment. Safety logic stays local. With the PLC, safety controller, interlocks, and the engineered operating procedures that govern the machine. That doesn't mean cloud-connected systems can't support action. They can support a human decision, request diagnostics, distribute an approved configuration, and create a traceable workflow when a condition crosses a defined risk level. But authority needs to remain clear. Imagine the maintenance lead gets an alert that the motor is staying hot while the conveyor runs. The system shows the evidence, current production context, and approved maintenance guidance. It creates a work request and might ask the gateway for another diagnostic reading. The decision to reduce speed, stop the line, isolate equipment, or continue running belongs to the people and local control rules authorized to make it. If remote action exists, add human approval, whether risk calls for it, record who requested the action who approved it, what the gateway received, and what the device reported afterward. Local interlocks must still reject actions that violate machine conditions even if the remote user has the right credentials. A secure command isn't automatically a safe command. This boundary also improves system design. The cloud observes broadly, combines data from different sources, and helps people reason about patterns over time. The edge and control layers respond by timing, safety, and physical process knowledge demand local behavior. Keep those roles separate and connect them with auditable messages and agreed authority. And the system needs one more discipline. The plant can't wait for cloud connectivity before it can keep operating, so the next part of the pipeline needs to handle the moment that connection disappears. Edge resilience and store and forward behavior. Cloud connectivity can fail without the production line failing. That needs to shape your edge design from the start.
