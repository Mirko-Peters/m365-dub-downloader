That learning app will be measured in months of execution speed and years of superiority over those who started late. Later adopters face a very real and dangerous choice. You can choose to redesign now while you still have the luxury of time to learn or you can wait until the pressure becomes unbearable. History suggests most companies will wait until they see what their competitors have done and then they will panic. They will try to force adoption during a crisis without proper governance which creates the kind of chaos that confirms everyone's worst fears about AI. The technology is not the bottleneck anymore because the APIs are sound and the architecture is solid. The only real constraint left is organizational readiness and whether you can actually change how you measure success. Can you grant decision authority to an agent and reorganize your teams fast enough to keep pace? Most organizations cannot do this, not because they lack talent but because true transformation threatens the existing power structure. They will continue to deploy agents in small tactical ways and eventually wonder why they aren't seeing a return on their investment but some organizations will be ready for this shift and those are the ones that will pull away from the rest of the pack. The readiness assessment If the next 18 months are going to matter, you have to answer one question first. Are you actually ready to use that time productively? I'm not asking if you're ready to buy a license or run a small pilot. I'm asking if you're ready to transform. Work IQ deployment is a mirror. It reveals exactly how prepared your organization is and in reality most companies will discover they aren't nearly as ready as they assumed. The assessment itself is straightforward. It covers four specific domains. You either have clarity in these areas or you don't. If you have it across all four, you can move fast. If you have it in some but not others, you know exactly where to focus your energy first. And if you have clarity in none of them, then congratulations. You just identified your actual starting point. The first domain is Data Hygiene. I'm not talking about your high level data strategy or your complex architecture. I'm talking about the actual condition of your SharePoint permissions right now. Can you honestly say who has access to what? You might assume people follow best practices but when you actually audit the system, you find out they don't. You need to know which sites are active and which are dormant. You need to identify which teams are business critical and which are just abandoned projects that nobody bothered to archive. If your file naming conventions are a mess, nobody can understand what they're looking at. If your metadata tags are inconsistent, the whole purpose of tagging is defeated. This is the messy part of the job. You'll likely find thousands of archive teams taking up space and shared drives where half the company has access to sensitive contracts. The second domain is governance maturity. This isn't about your aspirations or the frameworks you plan to build next year. It's about whether you have clear and enforceable policies right now. You need to be able to articulate what an agent is allowed to do and more importantly, what it isn't. When an agent produces an outcome that's different from what you expected, do you have a defined process for that? Most organizations discover they only have fragments of a plan. You might have a DLP policy over here and a retention schedule over there but nothing is integrated. You have governance theatre instead of a governance structure. If your rules don't cover agent autonomy or memory, you aren't ready. The third domain is leadership alignment. This question matters more than any other. Are your executives committed to redesigning how work gets done or are they just hoping for a tool that makes the existing process faster? If leadership just wants faster email, that's exactly what you'll get. But you won't get transformation. You won't get the organizational redesign that actually delivers a real return on investment. You'll just end up with a faster version of the same broken system. Real alignment means leaders are willing to change how they measure success. They have to be willing to restructure around workflows and grant actual decision authority to agents. If they aren't ready to measure outcomes instead of just activity, work IQ won't deliver any meaningful value. The fourth domain is outcome clarity. You have to define what winning looks like in terms of business impact. Don't talk to me about features deployed or pilots completed. Tell me if decision speed is going to drop from 30 days down to 5. Tell me if cross-team collaboration is going to produce better results in less time. When context is reused instead of reconstructed, you might save each person 2 hours every week. Projects should close 2 weeks faster and rework should drop because decisions are clearer from the start. These are measurable outcomes. If you can't articulate success in terms that matter to the business, you don't have a strategy. You just have hopes. Most organizations are going to fail this assessment. To be honest, you probably will too. You'll find gaps in all 4 domains. Your assumptions won't hold up under pressure. You'll realize your data is messier than you thought and your leadership isn't actually aligned. Success will mean different things to different stakeholders and your governance will look like a collection of scattered policies. But that isn't a failure. It's a diagnostic. It's the moment you discover exactly where you need to focus. Use this assessment to prioritize your next moves. Clean up the domain that is the most broken. Build governance in the space that matters most. Align your leadership on the outcomes that will actually unlock value. Every domain you strengthen makes the next one easier to tackle. The first 90 days. Readiness is a diagnostic tool, but it shouldn't be paralyzing. You don't have to solve every problem in all 4 domains before you can start. What you need to do is pick one single workflow and commit to learning everything you can from it. The first 90 days aren't about scaling work IQ across the entire enterprise. They are about building your capacity to learn before you try to go wide. Start with one high value workflow. It doesn't have to be the biggest one you have. Pick the one where improvement actually matters and where the friction is easy to see. This could be project execution, sales deal closure or customer onboarding. It might be HR hiring or finance approval chains. The specific department matters less than the characteristics of the work. You want a workflow that exists across multiple teams, where speed is a factor and where you can measure exactly where things are getting stuck right now. Once you have the workflow, map the current state. Don't map the ideal state or what the official org chart says. Map the reality. You need to find where decisions are actually made and where information is moving through hidden email chains instead of being centralised. Look for the spots where handoffs are manual when they should be automated. Find the people who are waiting for approvals from other departments. This is detective work. You are following information through real conversations and documents to see who actually influences the outcome. Most organizations operate on theory, but this mapping breaks that theory and shows you the truth. From that map, you can see where work IQ will actually reduce friction. Look at information retrieval. Can agents find the right context faster than a person hunting through all documents? Look at context synthesis. Can agents assemble everything needed for a decision in one place? Then look at decision routing. Can agents get that information to the right person without a human coordinator? These are your leverage points. These are the places where continuous intelligence actually changes the flow of work. You need to pilot this with a small engaged team. Don't pick your most skeptical people, but don't pick the most naive ones either. Find a group that is tired of how slow things are and is actually willing to work differently. A group of 20 people is much better than 200. You aren't trying to measure adoption yet. You are trying to learn how this technology affects this specific way of working. Before you deploy a single thing, you have to establish your baseline metrics. I'm not talking about how many people log in. I'm talking about actual workflow numbers. Measure how long it takes from the moment information enters the workflow until a decision is made. Track the rework rate to see how many decisions have to be redone. Count how many times people have to rehash the same information in separate conversations. These numbers won't be perfect, but they are real. They are the baseline you will measure against six months from now. Now, deploy work IQ to support that workflow. Don't try to automate the whole thing at once. Pick one critical stage or a single decision point. Maybe the agents handle the context assembly or the follow-up automation. It needs to be something tangible that people interact with every day. It should be something that either works or it doesn't. This generates the signals you need to understand what happens when intelligent systems touch your processes. You need to measure the results at 30, 60 and 90 days. If you wait until the end, you've waited too long. The 30 day mark shows you if the system is being used or if people are just working around it. That tells you if you have a user education problem. At 60 days, you can see if behavior is actually changing. People should be using agents instead of manual steps. By 90 days, you should see if the outcomes are changing. Is the cycle faster? Is the rework dropping? Is the team moving differently? Whatever you do, don't measure adoption. Adoption is a lagging indicator that only tells you if someone clicked a button. Outcome change is the only thing that matters. The decision cycle metric will tell you if speed increased and the rework metric will tell you if quality improved. These are the only numbers that justify moving forward. Use these results to shape your next phase. If agents helped in one stage but confused people in another, you need to double down on what worked and redesign what didn't. If certain types of decisions improved while others stayed the same, that is your signal. It tells you where the intelligence layer actually changes behavior. If some people use the system while others ignored it, you'll know if you have an adoption problem or an alignment problem. Those first 90 days are your learning cycle. You aren't trying to transform the whole company in three months. You are building the organizational capacity to understand how this changes the nature of working. You are building the evidence that intelligent agents can improve outcomes. This is how you create a foundation for everything that comes next. Work IQ isn't just another productivity tool because in reality, it's a complete structural overhaul of how your organization thinks. Moving from simple storage to active reasoning will fundamentally change your governance and your culture. Most companies will fight this shift because it exposes every hidden dysfunction in the system. But the advantage goes to the leaders who redesign their models around it right now. You need to start your data cleanup today because that 2026 inflection point is coming much faster than you think.
