From oversight to system feedback. Once authority moves into alignment the way we handle oversight has to change along with it because the old model was built entirely for visible labor. In that world you watched activity, tracked manual approvals and checked whether people stayed responsive inside the chain of command. That approach made sense when a manager's primary job was to monitor execution closely, mainly because execution was difficult to see any other way. But in an organization powered by AI that traditional model starts breaking down fast and the reason is that busyness is no longer a reliable proxy for actual contribution. Approval volume is no longer a reliable proxy for real control and managerial presence is definitely not a reliable proxy for system health. You can have leaders and meetings everywhere while the organization still performs poorly and in many cases a high density of oversight is actually a signal that the underlying design is weak. So here is the shift. Leaders have to stop treating oversight as a personal inspection and start treating it as feedback design. You are no longer asking if you personally reviewed enough work but instead you are asking what the system is telling you about how work is really flowing. You want to know where the process stalls, where trust drops and where people are constantly overriding or ignoring the AI. When you see rework, clustering or escalations piling up around the same few roles that is the system giving you feedback. A distributed human AI system does not stay healthy because leaders remain highly involved in every single moment but it stays healthy because the system produces signals early enough for leaders to adjust the design before a small failure compounds into a disaster. This requires a completely different leadership posture that is less about being a watchdog and more about building a sensor architecture. Instead of checking effort you are reading patterns which is exactly how mature technical systems already work today. We don't keep infrastructure stable by standing next to every server and hoping a person notices a problem. We build observability and track anomalies to find failure patterns. Now map that logic to leadership. If decisions are slowing down, don't just ask who is underperforming but ask where the flow is getting trapped in the pipes. If teams keep escalating things they should own. Don't just tell them to be bolder. Ask what in the system taught them that escalation is safer than taking action. This is where it becomes relevant for anyone responsible for systems because feedback is how you prevent hidden fragility. Without it you won't see the single points of failure until they finally break and you won't notice when one leader is approving everything or when a team is stuck waiting on permissions. One day the organization will demand more speed and ROI but you'll realize the system has been quietly draining all three for months. I keep coming back to structural resilience because feedback lets you correct the course before pressure becomes permanent damage. It shows you whether your operating model is actually learning or just repeating the same mistakes. It tells you if your AI rollout is changing how decisions are made or if it's just adding another layer of noise on top of an unchanged hierarchy. There is another reason this matters. Feedback reduces blame. When leaders only inspect people, every problem looks like a personal failing, leading them to believe a manager is weak or a team lacks urgency. But when you inspect patterns you see that many recurring people problems are actually design problems wearing a human face. It's a system outcome and once you see it that way your intervention quality improves significantly. You stop pushing for motivation where the real issue is ambiguity and you stop adding governance where the issue is actually a lack of ownership. Oversight in the AI era isn't disappearing. It's just getting sharper and focusing on the health of the decision environment. The real leadership questions now are whether the system can move without constant executive rescue and whether AI contributes in ways that actually change outcomes. Once you accept that your job is to manage the system rather than the people, the next step becomes very practical. You have to decide what to measure to ensure the system is healthy rather than just appearing to be under control. What leaders should measure instead? If you want to know whether your system can actually scale, you have to change what you are tracking. But with decision latency, which isn't about how long a meeting lasted or how fast someone replied on Slack but the full time from detecting an issue to taking meaningful action. When a problem becomes visible, the clock starts and how long it takes to resolve tells you more than any activity metric ever could. If latency stays high even after AI improves your analysis then the problem isn't a lack of intelligence, it's a broken pathway or a clogged queue. The second metric to watch is the dependency rate, which tracks how often workpourses because one specific person or approval path is required before anyone can move forward. Dependency is where fragility hides and a system with high dependency looks coordinated until the pressure rises and you realize too much motion is concentrated in two few places. This is a single point of failure and measuring it allows you to see concentration without turning it into a personal judgment against the manager. You aren't accusing a leader of being a control freak, you're simply measuring whether the operating model can function without them. The third metric is ownership clarity, which asks if people actually know who decides, who inputs and who owns the correction when things go wrong. This needs to be true in real workflows under pressure on an ordinary Tuesday afternoon, not just in a theoretical handbook. If ownership is fuzzy, decision quality will be inconsistent no matter how much AI support you add because people hesitate when the authority path is unclear. Then there is the matter of AI adoption in the actual workflow. We aren't talking about license activation or how many prompts were written last months but whether the AI actually changed how the work moves, did it reduce the cycle time, improve the quality of the first pass or help the decision happen closer to the front lines. If AI usage sits outside the primary workflow, you don't have a transformation, you just have assisted busyness. Leaders should also watch the override pattern to see how often AI recommendations are ignored or reversed by humans. And near zero override rate might mean trust is high or it might mean nobody is actually reviewing the work carefully. Conversely a high override rate could mean the model is weak or the team doesn't understand where the AI should be trusted. You should also measure rework specifically decision linked rework where a choice is reopened because boundaries or owners were unclear. Re-work is a clear sign that the system is wasting energy to compensate for poor design yet many organizations still mistake this for being thorough. Often it is just structural compensation for a system that didn't get it right the first time. Pay close attention to decision consistency as well. When similar situations appear in different teams do they produce similar choices within the same guardrails or does the answer depend on who is in the room. Consistency tells you if your context and decision design are strong enough to travel across the entire organization without constant hand holding. Finally you must track trust signals to see if people feel safe acting within their assigned boundaries. You need to know if they believe the system will support a good local decision or if they expect to be punished later through political correction. Low trust creates delay, delay creates escalation and escalation creates the kind of concentration that leads to systemic fragility. When you put all of this together you get a much clearer picture of leadership effectiveness in the AI era. It's not about how visible a leader is or how many approvals they signed off on but whether the organization can convert insight into action without needing executive heroics. We are moving from activity to flow, from presence to resilience and from managerial touch to true system health. Once you start measuring this way you'll find it impossible to ignore whether responsibility and access are actually aligned. Power alignment matching access with responsibility. This is where leadership design becomes very concrete. A system becomes fragile the moment we make someone responsible for an outcome without giving them the access, permissions or intervention rights required to influence it. That sounds obvious but it is one of the most common design flaws in enterprise environments today. We ask managers to improve adoption while the data sits in a different silo and we ask teams to own service quality even though every workflow change requires another department's approval. We ask business units to use AI responsibly but they often don't know which data is trusted or which automations are allowed or who has the authority to pause a process when things start drifting. The result is that responsibility moves down while power stays at the top. When that happens the organization starts performing alignment instead of actually living it. People attend the meetings and repeat the priorities while producing the reports but underneath it all the system is still waiting for power to travel from the center. That is not empowerment it is just delay with better language. And why does this matter even more in the Microsoft ecosystem tools like co-pilot, power platform and automation don't just support work. They actually amplify whatever structure already exists. If your governance is vague, automation scales that vagueness across the company. If your permissions are inconsistent, co-pilot scales that inconsistent context for every user. If your approval parts are concentrated in one office, power platform can make teams build faster right up to the point where they hit the same old bottleneck. The technology does not fix power misalignment it exposes it and sometimes it does so quite brutally. But I think leaders need to get much sharper about four specific kinds of rights. First are decision rights which define who can actually make the call. Second are data rights which determine who can access the information required to decide. Well, third are escalation rights which clarify who can trigger a higher review and underwater specific conditions. Finally there are intervention rights which dictate who can stop, correct or re-root a workflow when reality changes. If those rights are split across too many places without a clear logic the system becomes politically expensive to operate. People spend more time negotiating movement than they do creating it and then something entirely predictable happens. Shadow behavior appears as teams build around the official process. Someone exports data manually or a manager uses private chats to get a fast sign off. An analyst might keep a side spreadsheet because the formal dashboard is visible but not actionable. This is not usually an act of rebellion but rather a form of structural compensation. The official system is often too slow, too unclear or too disconnected from the actual responsibility carried by the people closest to the work so they root around it. Leaders often respond the wrong way by seeing, sprawl and tightening everything or locking the platform down when they see local tools. They see inconsistency and try to centralize more approvals but if the root problem is power misalignment more restriction does not create control. It creates pressure and that pressure will always find a workaround. That's why redundancy matters here too. If one leader, one inbox or one approval board becomes the only path through which meaningful action can happen you have created a single point of failure. It might be a very senior or intelligent point of failure but it is still a failure point. From a system perspective, concentration is risk. The answer is not decentralization for its own sake because we are not talking about creating chaos. We are talking about control distribution through clear guardrails, ownership and thresholds. You need enough local authority for the people carrying accountability to actually move the work. This means if a team owns a workflow outcome they should also have the minimum viable access to improve that workflow safely. If a manager is held accountable for adoption they need visibility into usage, permission, posture and process friction. If a platform owner is expected to govern safely they must have intervention rights that are real rather than symbolic. Power alignment is really about removing contradiction from the operating model. Don't tell people they own what they cannot influence and don't give people access without accountability. You should never deploy AI into workflows where no one can clearly say who decides, who validates and who corrects. If you do, the system will still produce outcomes but they will be fragile. The work will be slow in some places, chaotic in others, over-controlled at the top and under-supported at the edge. That's when leaders start mistaking symptoms for causes. They think the issue is adoption, discipline or resistance but often the reason is much simpler. The power model and the work model do not match. Once you see that the next step gets very practical. Now map that into the enterprise scenarios where this breaks most visibly. Enterprise scenario one, co-pilot rollout under control logic. Let's make this real with a scenario, a lot of Microsoft 365 leaders will recognize immediately. The co-pilot rollout begins with energy as licenses are purchased, use cases are collected and town halls happen. Leaders say the organization needs to move fast but then the old operating model quietly takes over. Every meaningful use case needs approval, every prompt pattern gets reviewed by central teams and managers ask for permission before teams even experiment. Business units are told to adopt a tool but nobody is fully sure where the acceptable boundary actually sits. Access becomes brought on paper while trust stays narrow in practice and that combination is usually fatal. Broad access without decision clarity does not create adoption, it creates hesitation. People open co-pilot and try a few low risk tasks like summaries, draft emails or meeting notes but they do not embed it into the workflows where real value lives. The moment the output starts touching customer communication or internal decisions, the organization falls back into control logic. People feel they must check with legal IT, compliance, the manager and the steering group all at once. Now co-pilot is not part of the operating model but rather a side car or a convenience layer. It is useful but it remains structurally disconnected from how work actually moves. This is where a lot of leaders misread the problem. They look at user reports and assume adoption is low because people need more training. While that might be true sometimes, the deeper issue is often that the organization has not made the workflow decision ready. The people inside the system do not know where co-pilot is trusted, what outputs are acceptable or when human validation is required. They don't know who owns the final judgment or what business outcome this tool is actually supposed to improve. The rollout becomes a technology deployment without an operating model redesign, which is why licenses can be high while embedded value stays low. The tool exists but the workflow does not change and once that happens reporting takes over. Leadership asks for dashboards on active users and prompt counts but those numbers can be misleading. A team can use co-pilot every day and still produce almost no structural business value if the output never changes a real decision. Activity gets measured because outcome design was skipped. A leader wants safe adoption. So they centralize guidance, which sounds reasonable until local experimentation slows down. If every department must wait for centrally approved scenarios, learning and value both slow to a crawl. The organization then concludes that co-pilot is interesting but immature. When often the tool is not the immature part, the leadership design is. Co-pilot scales where context is explicit and permissions are understood. It works where trusted data is visible and where a team knows which decisions they can support with AI versus which require escalation. You need to define target workflows first by clarifying where co-pilot supports summarization or recommendation. State the validation pattern and name the owner of the decision before the rollout begins. When you make the trusted data boundary visible and set thresholds for escalation, people finally know how to use the tool inside their actual business reality. That is when confidence rises and local initiative increases. Co-pilot stops being an expensive assistant sitting beside the workflow and starts becoming part of the workflow itself. In the end a rollout fails when leadership treats it like software distribution instead of a decision system redesign. Enterprise scenario 2, power platform sprawl and the wrong response. Now let's look at a second scenario because this one shows the same leadership failure from a different angle. We often see the power platform start spreading through an organization and usually this happens for a very good reason. The official systems are simply too slow to keep up with the business. So a team builds a quick app to solve a problem right now. Someone automates a manual approval that's been sitting in an inbox or a department creates a workflow to finally reduce the email chaos. For a while this feels like progress because it actually is progress. Local friction gets removed by the people closest to the work and the platform proves its value precisely because it lets the edge of the company respond faster than the center. But then scale arrives. You suddenly have more apps, more flows and more connectors than anyone can track. The logic starts to overlap, the owners are unknown and leadership starts to get nervous. This nervousness isn't irrational because the estate can look incredibly messy from the outside. Many questions appear alongside concerns about support and data quality, making the entire environment harder to see or manage. This is usually when the control reflex kicks in, leadership decides to lock it down, freeze new builds and restrict environments until every request can be rooted through a central review. In most cases this is the wrong response. It's not that governance is wrong, but panic centralization treats the symptom while killing the learning loop that revealed the demand in the first place. That is the part most leaders miss. Power platform sprawl is rarely just a governance problem. It is a signal. It shows you exactly where your official operating model is too slow, too rigid or too disconnected from business reality. People do not build shadow workflows because they enjoy complexity. They build them because the formal path cannot absorb the speed, nuance or volume of their local needs. When leadership responds by shutting the whole thing down, the organization does not become healthier, it just becomes quieter. The demand doesn't disappear, it just goes underground. That is structural compensation. The system is doing exactly what it was designed to do. It's just not designed for what the business actually needs. Now map that to leadership. A control-oriented leader sees sprawl and concludes the problem is too much autonomy, but a design-oriented leader sees that same sprawl and asks a better question. They want to know what unmet need this growth is pointing to and where the official platform model is failing to serve the work. They look for where people are solving real friction without a safe, visible path to do it well. That shift in perspective changes the intervention completely. The answer is not a blanket restriction, but rather a system of tiered autonomy. You need guardrails, shared patterns and visible ownership. You need a structure that says certain automations can be built locally inside clear limits, while others require review because the risk profile is higher. This is governance as enablement, not governance as fear. Why is this so important in the Microsoft ecosystem? Because the power platform is designed to amplify initiative. It lets business teams translate friction into action, but if governance only shows up as a blocking function, leadership teaches the organization a dangerous lesson. They teach people not to solve problems visibly, but to hide them until they are undeniable. That is how trust collapses between the center and the edge. Once that trust breaks, every future platform conversation gets harder. Business teams hear the word governance and think delay, while central teams hear innovation and think risk. Everyone becomes defensive, and the platform loses its strategic role. This is where power alignment matters. If local teams are expected to improve workflows, they need safe building lanes to do it. If central teams are expected to govern, they need visibility and standards that don't depend on discovering problems when it's already too late. Platform owners who want to scale value need a model that distributes capability without distributing chaos. That means moving from uncontrolled creation to designed participation. You build a community model with reusable components and clear environment strategies. The payoff for this approach is much bigger than simple control. You keep local initiative alive and surface real business demand, while reducing shadow IT by making the sanctioned path actually usable. Every app and every workaround is telling you something about the business reality your core systems are not handling well enough. Power alignment beats blanket restriction every time. If leaders miss that, they will spend their time suppressing symptoms instead of redesigning the system around the demand. Enterprise scenario 3. Decision bottlenecks in hybrid AI teams. Then there is the most common executive failure of all. The team gets faster, but the approvals don't. hybrid AI teams can now generate options, summaries and implementation plans far faster than most management structures were built to absorb. The visible output goes up and the number of possible next actions increases, but the coordination model often stays exactly where it was before, that mismatch creates a new kind of bottleneck. It's not that the team is slow, but that the organization cannot metabolize the speed of the team. Leaders often misdiagnose this by saying the team is moving too fast or that AI is flooding the office with noise. Well that might be partly true, the deeper issue is orchestration. The system has increased generation capacity without redesigning decision capacity. This matters a lot in hybrid work because the old informal correction paths are weaker. People are not always in the same room to catch nuance through hallway conversations or resolve ambiguity through quick visual cues. When AI accelerates output in distributed environments, what used to feel manageable through informal coordination starts breaking apart. You see more recommendations and proposed actions, but less shared clarity about what actually moves next. This creates the visibility paradox. The organization can see more than ever, but it feels less coordinated than before. Speed without orchestration creates fragmentation. One team might be on version three of a response, while another is still waiting for sign-off. A manager might have a smart summary from Copilot, but no clear authority to act on it. The issue is not paste. The issue is that increased decision volume is still flowing through all the approval architecture. In a hybrid AI team, if every meaningful action still needs to move upward for reassurance, then AI doesn't reduce friction. It just raises the volume of decisions waiting in line. The queue gets smarter, but it does not get shorter. Leaders start feeling overwhelmed because the structure around capability has not evolved. This is why lightweight decision frameworks matter so much more than heavy approval chains. A heavy chain assumes uncertainty should be solved by upward review, but a lightweight framework assumes uncertainty should be sorted by category. You have to define what can be decided locally and what truly needs escalation. That kind of design performs much better under AI acceleration because it keeps movement close to the work. Carmo organizations start separating themselves from stressed ones by making movement legible. People know what lane they are in, they know which threshold changes that lane, and they know who owns the call. This creates speed without chaos and calm without stagnation. The best AI-enabled organizations are not frantic, they are decisive. Frantic organizations confuse activity with adaptation, but decisive organizations reduce ambiguity at the point where action needs to happen. When I look at hybrid AI teams, I don't ask if they have enough tools. I ask if their decision pathways were redesigned for a world where option generation is abundant and coordination is distributed. If that redesign does not happen, the whole organization starts blaming the wrong thing. They blame AI for the pressure, but that pressure is a system outcome. AI increased the pace of possible action, but the leadership model failed to increase the pace of authorized action. That is the bottleneck. Organizations that solve it gain something bigger than speed. They become easier to work inside. You end up with fewer unnecessary escalations, less hidden waiting, and more confidence at the edge. There is more energy for real judgment instead of managerial choreography. That is where the future advantage sits. It's not in producing more options, but in deciding well among them without routing every important moment through a structure built for a slower world. After all these scenarios, the real question becomes obvious. What capabilities does a leader actually need if control is no longer the thing that scales at our capability one? System thinking. So what is the actual capability that sits underneath all of this? It is system thinking. I'm not talking about a nice-to-have skill or something we should leave to the architects, operations specialist or transformation teams. This is a fundamental leadership requirement for the world we live in now. Because if traditional control is failing while AI increases our speed and decision volume, leaders need a way to see far beyond individual events. They need to see the flow. They need to understand dependencies, incentives, and bottlenecks. Instead of reading recurring friction as an isolated human weakness, leaders must start seeing it as a structural pattern. That is exactly what system thinking does for an organization. It changes your entire unit of analysis. Rather than asking who dropped the ball, you start asking where the work consistently slows down. Instead of wondering why people are resisting change, you ask, "What in the environment makes caution the only rational behavior for them?" When a team isn't taking ownership, you stop blaming their motivation and ask what part of the design separates their responsibility from their actual authority. This is a very different way of seeing the world and it has never been more important than it is today. The reason is that AI makes local symptoms appear much faster than they used to. A delay that once stayed invisible for weeks now becomes obvious in days and a bad approval structure that once felt manageable will collapse under a higher volume of decisions. A week handoff that used to stay hidden inside endless emails and meetings is now exposed the moment teams generate and process options faster than ever before. If a leader cannot read systems, they will spend their entire career reacting to symptoms. They will throw more training at a problem, add more governance or schedule a new meeting and a new dashboard. While those things might help a little bit, they are often just structural compensation layered on top of structural confusion. The real question is always deeper than the surface level. You have to ask where the work is waiting, where the context is breaking and where the approvals are clustering. You need to identify where a single person, team or Q is becoming a single point of failure for the entire operation. That is the lens you need to adopt. This is why system thinking is now business critical rather than just being technically interesting. Strategy does not fail only at the strategy layer, it fails in the operating flow. A company can have a clear AI ambition, a massive budget and executive sponsorship, but they will still see disappointing results if the movement of work remains badly designed. That is not a technology problem. It is a systems problem. This really clicked for me while I was working on large technology programs where leaders kept trying to solve delays with pure pressure. They would tell people to push harder, review more often or escalate sooner. But when we actually mapped the flow, the same patterns kept showing up regardless of how hard people worked. Decisions waited in the same place. Clarification was needed in the same spots and ownership blurred in the same areas every single time. The issue was never about effort because the issue was actually the architecture of the system itself. Once you see that, your approach to leadership changes completely. You stop trying to be the person who has all the answers. Instead, you start becoming the person who can see the shape of the problem clearly enough to redesign it. That means looking across boundaries rather than just focusing on your specific function. Or your reporting line. You have to look at the interaction between tools, teams, permissions, habits and incentives. Most business friction lives in the seams between departments. You see marketing, waiting on legal operations, waiting on data or project teams, waiting on a sponsor to give them reassurance. Business units wait on IT and manages wait on leaders who are themselves waiting on another committee to make a move. That is how organizations burn through time without even noticing it. System thinking lets you trace that hidden costs so you can actually do something about it. It protects you from one of the biggest mistakes of the AI era, which is over personalizing structural outcomes. When a team looks slow or a manager looks cautious, the easy move is to make it about their mindset. But if the same behavior keeps appearing across different people in the same environment, the smarter assumption is that the environment is teaching that behavior. It is a system outcome. Once leaders internalize this truth, they become much more precise in how they work. They intervene with design rather than just applying more pressure. They change thresholds, clarify rights and remove unnecessary handoffs that don't add value. They focus on creating better feedback loops and reducing concentration risk. This is what makes system thinking so powerful for a modern business. It helps you see that performance is rarely just about talent or motivation. It is actually about the conditions under which that talent and motivation are asked to operate. In the AI era, those conditions are changing faster than we can keep up with. This is the first capability that separates old school leadership from what comes next. When you see the organization as a living decision system, can you spot the hidden dependencies before they turn into a total failure? Can you read recurring friction as valuable design data? Once you can do those things, the next capability becomes possible. You can start designing the decision layer itself. Capability 2. Decision design. Once you can see the system, the next leadership capability you need is decision design. This is where systems thinking turns into an operating reality for the business. Seeing a bottleneck is useful, but redesigning how decisions actually happen is what changes your performance. This is the part that many leaders still skip over. They understand that too much work is moving upward and that teams are waiting too long for answers. They see that AI is producing more options than the organization can possibly absorb, but then they respond with encouragement instead of architecture. They tell people to be more proactive, use better judgment or escalate less often. While that sounds reasonable on the surface, it is an incomplete solution. If you want better decisions at scale, you have to design the categories, thresholds and review parts that make good judgment repeatable. Let me make this practical for you. A useful decision model starts by defining specific decision classes. Not every decision deserves the same treatment, which means some should stay local while others are shared or escalated. Some can even be automated entirely. Some decisions should only trigger human attention when something unusual happens. That classification matters because without it everything starts to feel equally risky to the people doing the work. When everything feels risky, escalation becomes the default behavior for everyone involved. That is exactly how leadership overload gets recreated, even in organizations that claim they want more autonomy. A leader needs to ask a small set of design questions to fix this. Which decisions belong closest to the work and which ones need cross-functional input before anyone takes action? You have to decide which ones genuinely require senior escalation because the trade-offs affect the entire enterprise. You also need to know which ones are repetitive enough to automate inside a safe boundary and which ones should run normally until an exception occurs. That is what decision design looks like in practice. It is not an abstract concept. It is a purely operational one. Since those classes exist, the next layer of inputs becomes just as important. You have to define what data is required before someone acts and what level of confidence is actually enough to move forward. You need to know what counts as sufficient evidence and what specifically triggers a second review or an escalation. If these things stay unwritten, people will fill the gap with office politics. One manager will move fast while another waits for total certainty. One team might treat AI as a strong first pass while another treats it as a draft that still needs a full manual recreation from scratch. The result of this is inconsistency and it doesn't happen because people are careless. It happens because the decision environment is vague. This is why I always say that predictability matters more than raw intelligence. A highly intelligent organization with poorly defined decision pathways will still behave erratically. On the other hand, a moderately intelligent organization with strong decision design often performs better because people actually know how to move. Now map that logic to your governance. A lot of leaders hear the term "decision design" and assume it means they have to give up control. Actually it is the opposite. It is how you keep the quality of control without forcing a concentration of control at the top. You are not removing oversight. You are moving it upstream into the design of the pathway itself. You define where human review is required and where automation is allowed. You define where local action is safe and what happens when the pattern finally breaks. That creates consistency without needing executive hands in every single moment. This is where the concept of escalation has to change as well. Escalation should be the exception to the rule, not the standard operating model for the company. If every unclear moment gets pushed upward, the organization eventually trains itself to avoid using judgment at all. But if escalation is tied to explicit thresholds, people start learning where their decision lane really begins and ends. That builds the kind of confidence that turns distributed capability into real performance. Without that confidence, teams have access to tools but no real agency to use them. AI might be present in the workflow, but it won't be trusted. Leaders will stay overloaded because the system keeps routing every bit of uncertainty back to their desks. If you want one shortcut for this, here it is. Design the path before you demand better decisions from your people. Define the classes, the thresholds, the review logic, and the exception flow. Most importantly, define who owns the correction when the pattern fails. That last part matters a lot because a decision system without correction ownership becomes brittle very quickly. People will act until something goes wrong and then everyone suddenly rediscoveres the old hierarchy. That isn't resilience, it is just borrowed speed that won't last. Real decision design includes feedback on the back end. You need to know who notices drift and who is responsible for updating the rules or changing the thresholds. Someone has to be in charge of retiring the automation if it stops serving the business reality it was meant to support. That is the real leadership move. You are moving from heroic decision making to a repeatable decision architecture. Once leaders start doing that well, the organization becomes noticeably lighter for everyone involved. There is less waiting, less ambiguity, and much less executive drag on the system. You get more local judgment backed by stronger guardrails. That is what scales in the AI era. It isn't about smarter leaders making more calls. It is about better leaders designing how those calls get made. Capability 3. Power alignment. Decision design falls apart if power remains unevenly distributed. This is the next capability leaders have to build and to be honest it is exactly where most AI initiatives quietly fail. You can invest in better tools, more advanced models and optimised workflows, but you will still get weak outcomes if the people expected to act, lack the authority or support to carry their responsibility. That is what I call power misalignment and once you know what to look for you see it everywhere. A team is told to improve the customer experience yet they aren't allowed to change the underlying workflow that causes the friction. A manager is held accountable for AI adoption but they can't see the actual usage signals clearly enough to know when to intervene. We see business units expected to use AI responsibly while the rules for data and escalation are scattered across four different departments. The burden of the outcome sits in one place but the ability to influence that outcome sits somewhere else. From a system perspective that isn't just a management headache it is a structural fragility that puts the whole operation at risk. Accountability without authority creates hesitation while authority without accountability creates drift and neither leads to the performance you need. Power alignment is the leadership work of matching four specific things. Authority, Access, Accountability and Support. If any one of those pieces is missing the system will try to compensate in ways that look like bad behaviour. People will escalate every tiny detail just to stay safe or they'll start roting around formal processes to get things done. They create side channels and wait for unofficial blessings before moving which makes leadership look at the team and call them resistant or immature. Usually the reality is much simpler than a lack of maturity. The structure is asking people to perform ownership without actually equipping them for it and this gap becomes a massive problem in AI enabled environments. AI increases reach by giving more people the ability to analyse, draft and automate which sounds empowering on paper. But if the authority model stays vague you get a dangerous mix of widened capability and concentrated permission. People can see more and suggest more but they still can't decide more and that gap creates frustration faster than almost anything else. This is why so many AI outputs end up influencing absolutely nothing. The recommendation is there, the insight is accurate and the summary is good but the person receiving it doesn't own the decision. The output just hangs in the system like optional advice, interesting and visible but ultimately non-binding. When that becomes the pattern, adoption feels inconsistent because structurally it is inconsistent. I believe leaders need to become much more explicit about where power is concentrated. You have to ask who can act, who can stop action and who has the right to intervene when the risk profile changes. If too many of those rights sit in one person or one approval queue you've created a single point of failure. The issue here isn't a lack of competence, it's a problem of structural dependence. Many organisations are still compensating for fragile power models through informal behaviour like private chats and manual data exports. People build these shadow routes because they need movement and the formal system can no longer carry the speed of the work. That is structural compensation in action. The goal isn't blind decentralization or giving everyone equal power and hoping for the best. It is about control, distribution and clear decision rights that match local authority to local accountability. You need enough oversight to catch drift without suffocating the flow of the work. If you remember nothing else, remember that people cannot sustainably own outcomes they are not structurally allowed to influence. Since leaders understand this they stop asking who is responsible and start asking if responsibility and power are actually aligned in the same place. That alignment is what makes AI useful at scale. It isn't just about broader access or smarter outputs, but a model where the people closest to the work can act inside clear guardrails without waiting for hierarchy to bless every move. To keep that structure healthy leaders need one more capability. Feedback loops. Capability 4. Feedback loops. Feedback loops are the fourth capability because even the best design systems eventually drift. A decision pathway that worked six months ago might not fit the business today and guardrails that felt clear at rollout often become ambiguous under pressure. AI outputs that were useful in one context get overused in another as priorities and risks change. The environment is always moving and if you don't build a way to sense that movement, the organization becomes brittle without anyone noticing. In the AI era, scale creates distance, which means there are more teams, more automations, and more delegated decisions than ever before. This creates more places where things can quietly go wrong while looking perfectly fine on the surface. Without a functional feedback loop, leaders only discover failure after the damage has become expensive and visible. You see it in customer escalations, compliance issues, or a burnt out manager who has been struggling in silence. By the time a platform locks up or a workflow loses the trust of the team, it's already too late. A real feedback loop is designed to catch that strain much earlier by telling you where the design is no longer producing the intended outcome. To make this work, you have to keep it small and avoid the trap of building giant review rituals full of slides that nobody actually uses. The point of a feedback loop isn't to generate more reporting but to create a short learning cycle around the health of the system. I suggest anchoring your feedback around a few specific signals. Decision speed, ownership clarity, override frequency, rework, and trust. If decision speed drops or ownership clarity fades, it's a sign that people are losing confidence in where action belongs. When you see overrides rise sharply, it usually means the AI output is weakening or the review logic has become too vague for the current context. If rework increases, the system is likely compensating for ambiguity somewhere upstream and if trust drops, performance will almost always follow it down. Leaders need recurring reviews that ask design questions rather than personality questions to get to the root of these issues. Instead of hunting for someone to blame, you should be reading signals from the operating model to see where decisions are slowing down. The system creates silence and causes people to hide problems until they become political but feedback creates a culture of learning. If anomalies are treated as design data, the organization becomes much more honest and you can solve problems while they are still small. This is exactly why mature engineering environments tend to outperform traditional management cultures. In engineering, a recurring failure is a signal to inspect the architecture, whereas many managers still treat failure as evidence that someone needs to try harder. Even harder doesn't scale, it just exhausts the people inside the system until they eventually quit or check out. Leaders need to normalize a posture where they inspect the design when a problem repeats and inspect the context when trust drops. That is the loop, signal interpretation, adjustment and retest. The adjustment doesn't have to be a massive transformation. Sometimes it's just a threshold change or removing one unnecessary approval that has been distorting behavior for months. Small design changes made consistently create a far more adaptive organization than occasional transformation theatre. This is also where the ROI of AI becomes realistic because value doesn't come from a one-time tool launch. It comes from continuously tuning the workflow around where AI helps and where human judgment matters most. Feedback loops turn AI from a project into an operating discipline and move leadership from episodic intervention to continuous calibration. Most importantly, they protect your structural resilience by stopping bad pathways from becoming standard practice. Leaders who embrace this stop trying to grip the system harder and instead start helping the system learn how to succeed. Seven-day leadership reset. Let me make this practical for you. This is the exact point where many leaders agree with everything conceptually but then they change absolutely nothing operationally. They will tell me that control is slowing them down and that AI is changing the flow of decisions yet they never actually adjust their behavior. Monday morning rolls around, the calendar fills up with the same old meetings and the traditional model quietly resumes its hold on the organization. That is why I believe you need a seven-day reset rather than a massive transformation program. You don't need a six-month operating model, redesign deck or a series of expensive workshops to see a real difference. What you need is one week, one decision loop and one visible change to prove the system can work differently. Start by picking one recurring decision that still waits on leadership much longer than it should. Don't go for the biggest strategic move in the company or the most politically sensitive topic you can find. Instead choose a decision that happens often enough to actually teach you something about how your system functions. You might look at customer exception approvals, internal policy interpretations or even simple content sign-offs that currently get stuck in your inbox. Pick something real that currently slows down because your people still believe leadership needs to touch it before the work can move forward. Once you have that decision in mind, map out the actual flow of how it happens today. I am talking about the real-world path it takes, not the idealized version written in your process documents. You need to see who starts the request, who adds their input and who ultimately makes the final call. Look closely at what data gets used in which tools support the process but pay special attention to where the work sits waiting. You want to find where it bounces back for clarification and where the whole thing quietly depends on one person saying yes before anyone feels safe moving. That matters because most decision friction hides in unstated assumptions rather than formal rules and people often think they know where the bottleneck is but usually they only know where the frustration finally becomes visible. They rarely see where the structural drag actually begins. So once you see the real flow, your job is to remove exactly one unnecessary approval. But one change is enough to start the process without breaking the system. If you try to remove too much at once, people will get nervous and the system will start reading the change as a sign of instability. But if you remove one approval that exists out of habit rather than true risk, the positive effect becomes visible almost immediately. After you cut that approval, you must define one explicit decision owner for that specific loop. It cannot be a committee, it cannot be the leadership team and it definitely cannot be a vague partnership between business and IT. You need one owner who knows they have the authority to make the call inside agreed boundaries and who understands exactly when an escalation is actually required. Now you have to check the obvious question that most organizations skip entirely. Does that new owner actually have the data, the context and the system access they need to decide well? If the answer is no, then you haven't actually redesigned the system at all. You have just redistributed the pressure onto someone else. Ownership without access isn't empowerment. It is just structural theatre that sets people up for failure. So you have to fix the access issues too and then you need to set one single metric to track your progress. For most of the teams I work with, I suggest choosing either decision latency or a reduction in rework. You want to know how long the decision takes from the moment an issue is detected until action is taken. You also want to see how often the decision comes back because the boundary or the quality threshold was unclear. This gives you a clean before and after signal that proves whether your design is actually working. One week later you need to review what changed, but don't do this with a giant steam clearing committee. Sit down with the people who are actually inside the flow and ask them if the decision felt faster or if the quality held up. Find out if they felt clearer about who owned the outcome or if new types of confusion started to appear. You should also ask if the AI support became more useful or if the new speed exposed a different gap in your tools. Most importantly, find out if anyone still behaved as if they needed unofficial permission from above to move forward. That last question matters more than mostly does realize because the social memory of control often outlasts the formal design. People will still escalate problems because the old system trained them to do so for years. That isn't a sign of failure, but rather a form of feedback that tells you where your behavior still needs to reinforce the new design. If you want a deeper challenge, don't just review the workflow, you have to review yourself. Ask yourself if you stepped back when the new owner could have acted or if you waited for the metric instead of jumping in at the first sign of discomfort. You have to know if you reinforced the boundary or if you quietly overrode it because staying central still feels safer for your ego. This is the real replacement for control and it isn't about caring less, but rather about building a better architecture. If you do this well, you will learn that most organizations don't need more leadership attention in every workflow. They actually need fewer points where leadership attention is structurally required to keep things moving. That is the reset. Pick one loop, map it honestly, remove one approval and name one owner. Check their access, measure one signal and review the results one week later. If you audited one decision loop this way every week for the next quarter, you would know more about your leadership model than any strategy offside could ever tell you.
