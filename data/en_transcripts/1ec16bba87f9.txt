So what does a real governance model look like when your platform is fragmented? Identity is only partial and tenant boundaries still actually matter. It looks like a hub and spoke system. I'm not suggesting this because the diagram looks clean on a slide, but because it actually matches the messy operating conditions you are dealing with right now, you need one central place where governance decisions are designed, measured and enforced as a single system. And then you need local tenant teams to execute those decisions. These local teams have to work inside their own boundaries without inventing their own version of the rules every time they feel a little bit of pressure. We have to start with the hub. The hub is not an admin portal. It is not a shiny dashboard. And it definitely isn't a heroic central team jumping into every single tenant to clean up files manually. Instead, the hub acts as the ultimate governance authority. This group owns the policy design, the audit baselines, the label standards and the rollout criteria. They are the ones who decide what ready for co-pilot actually means, what evidence counts as proof, and how fast the security gap has to be closed. That shift changes the entire conversation. Because of this structure, global stops being a word for one magical screen and starts meaning one unified governance system. You get one standard for purview controls, one standard for how you ingest audits, and one standard for your sensitivity labels. There is one clear bar for what must be true before any specific tenant is allowed to expand co-pilot access to more users. Then you need the spokes. The spokes are your local tenant execution teams who take that baseline and apply it to their specific environment. They are the ones running the remediation, validating the logs, and reviewing broad access permissions. However, they do all of that work inside a very narrow design space rather than a free for all. If a local team needs to deviate from the plan, there is a formal path for exceptions. And if they want to move faster, they still have to meet the shared controls first. When regional or legal differences pop up, those issues get documented and approved instead of being quietly absorbed into some local workaround that nobody else knows about. The structure is vital because fully centralized models usually choke on local details, while fully local models drift into total inconsistency almost immediately. Hub and spoke avoids both of those failure patterns. The hub gives you the consistency you need, while the spokes give you the execution speed where the work is actually happening. Let's make this concrete. At the hub, you define the minimum purview control set every tenant must run before co-pilot is allowed to grow. You define exactly which audit events you expect to see and how often you will review them. You also create a shared label taxonomy for sensitive content so that a confidential tag does not mean five different things across five different tenants. You set the rhythm for reviews, perhaps monthly for your overall security posture, and more frequently in areas where the rollout is currently active. At the spoke, your team maps those high-level standards to the reality of that specific tenant. They find which sites still have broad access exposed and which business units are lagging behind on their labeling. They check if the audit is actually turned on and if the right records are visible to the security team. They look for local admins who might have introduced exceptions that were never officially reviewed and they decide if a pilot group can safely expand or if they need to stop and fix things first. And here is the hard rule because without a rule, this model is just a suggestion instead of actual governance. No co-pilot rollout happens in any tenant without validated audit logging, a completed oversharing scan, and a minimum baseline for label coverage. I am not talking about these things being planned or partially discussed. They must be validated. If a tenant cannot prove those three conditions are met, the rollout pauses. That might sound strict until you compare it to the massive cost of cleaning up a mess after users start finding sensitive content you never meant to expose through AI. When you look at it that way, these rules are just basic common sense. Another reason this model works so well is the timing. The longer you wait to set this up, the more clean-up debt you are creating for yourself later. Every tenant that rolls out ahead of the baseline becomes another massive backlog of exceptions and missing evidence that your team will eventually have to unwind. Leaders often treat governance as something that can mature after adoption is finished, but in a multi-tenant co-pilot world, that sequence is completely backwards. Governance has to lead the rollout or the rollout will create more debt than your teams can ever hope to absorb. If you are leading this at an enterprise level, your next move is very clear. Stand up the hub right now and define that baseline today. Assign your spokes and put your exception handling process in writing immediately. You have to make global mean that your decisions and your rollout gates work as one single system, even when the tenants themselves do not. A model only matters if it actually changes what gets enforced tomorrow morning. What leaders should measure before they scale? A governance model only holds together if it produces measurements that everyone shares. Otherwise, every tenant will claim they are making progress in their own language and leadership will get status updates without having any real control. Before you scale co-pilot any further, you need a small set of metrics that tell you if the architecture is behaving the way you think it is. Activity levels won't tell you the truth and adoption numbers definitely won't show you the risks. You should start with oversharing reduction. This is still one of the most obvious signs that your environment is actually becoming safer for AI. You need to look at broad access groups, open sharepoint sites and old permissions that grant way more access than the business actually needs. The goal here isn't to hit a perfect number but to prove that your exposure is moving down before your co-pilot reach moves up. If those two lines are moving in opposite directions, you are expanding much faster than you are fixing the underlying problems. The second metric you need is observability coverage. Can you actually see what co-pilot is doing across all your tenants in a way that supports a real investigation? Most research points to Microsoft Perview as the main place where core records like co-pilot interaction are stored but that assumes you have auditing turned on everywhere. Coverage is not something you can just assume is working. You need to know exactly which tenants have ordered ingestion active and which ones are still leaving you with partial visibility. If you have no logs, you have no governance and no accountability. That sounds blunt because it has to be. If a tenant cannot provide usable evidence of what is happening, that tenant is not ready to scale safely. You cannot govern what you are not allowed to inspect and you certainly won't be able to defend your actions if something goes wrong later. The third metric is your time to policy across all tenants. This is where leadership finds out if the Harben-Spoke model is actually real or just decorative. A central decision means absolutely nothing if one tenant enforces it this week while another waits a month to think about it. You should measure the time it takes from a policy being approved to that policy being effectively enforced in every single tenant. I'm not talking about the time it takes to announce it or document it but the time until it is actually live. For an AI program that moves this fast, that kind of delay is just another word for risk. A practical target for most organizations is under 72 hours for standard changes. If your cycle time is much longer than that, you don't have a scalable model yet. You just have a coordination problem that needs to be solved. Next, you need to look at your label coverage ratio. Do not let this turn into a vanity metric where one tenant hits high numbers while the others fall behind. What really matters is whether your sensitive content is labeled consistently enough that co-pilot's behavior isn't just based on user guesswork. You aren't looking for perfection here but you are looking for a comparable level of control across every tenant in the organization. Finally, you need to track your access drift rate. This metric tells you whether your cleanup efforts are actually sticking over time. After you finish a remediation project, how quickly do those permissions start to spread again? How often do those broad groups crawl back into the system? Many teams realize this too late because they measured the fixed ones and assumed the problem was gone forever. It wasn't. The only metric that matters is how fast the system starts to slip once you stop looking at it. That is your operating dashboard. You need to track oversharing reduction, observability coverage, time to policy, label coverage, and access drift. The rhythm of your reporting matters just as much as the numbers themselves. The hub should own the executive dashboard because leadership needs one single view of the entire posture. Meanwhile, the spokes should own the remediation because the actual work has to happen where the tenant boundary lives. One team reads the system and the other team corrects it. You must track these numbers before you expand, not after the fact. The risk isn't waiting for your next meeting or governance workshop. It is already active in every tenant where co-pilot can find exactly what your current controls are still allowing it to see. The two patents leaders keep missing. I keep seeing two specific patents in the field and they both happen because leaders treat co-pilot like a software launch instead of a major control event.
