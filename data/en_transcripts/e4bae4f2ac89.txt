They own the management group hierarchy, they own the shared infrastructure, the hub network, the directory integration, the central logging. They own the policies that apply across the entire organization. They publish modules, they maintain the module registry, they set standards. Application teams exist separately. Their job is building products, they own specific subscriptions, they own the resources, their application needs, they don't own the shared layer, they consume it. This separation prevents two things from happening simultaneously. Application teams can't break shared infrastructure by accident, they can't modify the networking layer and accidentally cut off connectivity for everyone. They can't change our back in a way that locks out the security team, but also the platform team doesn't become a bottleneck. Application teams have autonomy within their own subscription boundaries. They can deploy resources, they can configure their application. They can iterate quickly the multiple stacks pattern, reinforces this boundary. The platform team manages several stacks, one for connectivity, the hub network, the routing, the integration with on-premises or cloud providers. One for identity, service principles, managed identities, role assignments at the tenant level, one for shared services, log aggregation, backup infrastructure, disaster recovery. Each stack has its own life cycle, each has its own approval process, each is versioned separately. Application teams deploy their own stacks within the subscriptions they've been allocated. Their stack creates the resources specific to their application. Virtual machines, databases, storage, APIs, but these stacks operate within the governance boundaries set by the platform stacks. The networking stack has already created the virtual networks and subnets. The application stack deploys into those networks. The identity stack has already configured role assignments. The application stack uses those identities. The application team isn't reinventing the wheel, they're working within a framework. Deny settings on platform stacks enforce this boundary technically. The platform team sets deny-write and delete on the networking stack. Application teams can read the network configuration. They can deploy into the networks, but they can't modify the networks themselves, they can't change routes, they can't alter security groups in ways that would affect other applications. The deny setting makes the boundary hard. It's not a suggestion, it's a technical control. The governance model needs to be explicit. Teams need a document, or better, a living reference in your documentation system that answers three questions. What's shared the hub network, the directory, the log storage, the backup infrastructure, teams know this is owned by the platform team. Changes require platform team approval, but what's delegated? Application teams control their subscriptions. They can create resources, they can modify application-specific configuration, they can iterate what's forbidden, certain resource types, certain configurations, things that would violate policy or compliance. Teams know these are off-limits. The policies prevent them. The documentation explains why. This model scales because it's self-explanatory. New teams onboard into a landing zone. They read the documentation. They understand the boundaries. They understand what they own and what they don't. They understand what they can do and what requires escalation. The platform team doesn't need to brief every team individually. The model is codified, it's reproducible. It's in the infrastructure itself. Governance at enterprise scale works because it's not trying to control everything. It's controlling what matters and delegating the rest. Drift, compliance, and continuous validation. You've got your infrastructure defined in Bicep. You've got it deployed through a pipeline. You've got governance enforced at multiple levels. And then reality happens. Someone logs into the portal at 2am on a Sunday to troubleshoot a performance issue. They make a change. Just a quick SQ upgrade on a database. They plan to document it on Monday. They never get around to it. Now your template says one thing. Your actual infrastructure says another. That gap is drift. And if you're not watching for it, you'll never know it's there. Drift is the quiet failure mode of infrastructure as code. The template isn't wrong. The infrastructure isn't broken, but they're no longer synchronized. Over time, drift compounds, one change becomes two, two becomes five. Eventually, your infrastructure has evolved so far from your template that nobody's quite sure what the actual topology looks like. Deploying again becomes risky. You might override changes you didn't know about. You might break something. Deployment stacks address part of this. A stack tracks which resources it manages. When you run a deployment, the stack knows exactly which resources are supposed to be there. If a resource has been manually deleted, the stack detects it. If a resource has been manually created that wasn't in the template, the stack sees it's unmanaged. But here's the limitation. The stack can't automatically decide what to do about unmanaged resources. It can track them. It can report them. It can't fix them without explicit configuration. Azure Policy is the complementary piece. Policies define what compliant infrastructure looks like. A policy might say, all storage accounts must have encryption enabled. All networks must have network security groups. All applications must have monitoring configured. When you violate a policy, it doesn't prevent the action, unless you've set the effect to deny. But it does flag the resource as non-compliant. That flag appears in compliance reports. You know something's wrong. The discipline comes from continuous validation. What if deployments aren't just for before deployment previews? They're continuous monitoring tools. Run what if regularly? Every day. Every week. Compare what your template says should be there with what actually is. The difference is drift. When you see drift, you have a choice. Redploy the template to bring reality back in line or update the template to match reality. Either way, you're making a conscious decision. You're not allowing drift to accumulate silently. Compliance monitoring should be the same. Not a quarterly audit. Not a security review once a year. Continuous. Dashboards that show policy compliance in real time. Which resources are compliant, which are flagged as non-compliant, which policies have exceptions. How many resources are managed versus unmanaged? These dashboards need to be visible, not hidden in a compliance portal that nobody checks. They should be in your operations center on your team's dashboards, where people see them every day. The principle is straightforward but often violated. Infrastructure should match the template. When it doesn't, that's not normal operational variance. That's a problem. The problem might be that someone made a change they shouldn't have and now you need to investigate and understand why. The problem might be that the template is out of date and needs updating. The problem might be that the policy is too strict and needs adjustment. Whatever the problem is, it needs attention. This is the difference between treating your infrastructure code as documentation and treating it as truth. Documentation describes what you think exists. Truth is what actually does. The infrastructure as code approach only works if you're committed to keeping them synchronized. That synchronization is continuous work. It's drift detection. It's compliance monitoring. It's validation running regularly. It's not something you do once and move on. Compliance isn't a checkpoint you pass. It's an ongoing practice. Migrations and brownfield scenarios. Most organizations don't start with bicep. You inherit a reality. Someone deployed resources through the portal three years ago. Another team wrote arm templates that nobody fully understands anymore. A third team uses Terraform. You've got Azure resource graph queries that reveal often resources nobody remembers creating. You've got virtual networks configured through scripts that live on someone's laptop. But you've got a brownfield. The question isn't how to build perfect infrastructure from scratch. It's how to gradually transform what exists into something governed and maintainable. The migration isn't a cut over. This is critical. You don't wake up one morning and decide everything converts to bicep by Friday. That's how you break production. Migration is a journey. It happens incrementally. It happens in stages. Some workloads move first. Others follow on their own timeline. The typical pattern starts with new resources. Every new resource should be bicep, not eventually, from now on. When a team wants to deploy something new, they define it in bicep. They go through the governance process. They use the landing zone template. They integrate with the module registry. This decision alone that new things are always bicep begins the transition. Over time, new resources accumulate. The bicep portion of your infrastructure grows. Existing resources can be adopted into bicep through export. Azure has a feature that lets you export the template for any existing resource. You export a virtual network that was created manually through the portal. You get an arm template. That template becomes the basis for your network module. It's messy. The exported template includes every parameter, every property, most of which you don't actually care about. But it's a starting point. You refactor it. You strip out the noise. You parameterize the decisions. You add documentation. The exported template becomes your module. This adoption process works for individual resources or for entire resource groups. Export everything in a resource group. You get one large template that defines the whole group. Now you decide how to split it into modules. Maybe one module per resource type. Maybe one module per logical architecture layer. The refactoring is work, but it's work you can do safely. The resources already exist. You're not creating anything new. You're just creating a bicep representation of what's already there. The governance question surfaces immediately. What's your migration strategy? Which workloads move first? This isn't academic. It has real consequences. Some teams will have dependencies on other teams' infrastructure. If you migrate a networking infrastructure first, but the application teams aren't ready to handle parameterized networks, you create friction. Better to think through the dependency graph. Identify what can safely move independently. Start there. Build momentum. Let success in one area create demand in another. Brownfield environments are fundamentally messy. You're not cleaning a blank slate. You're renovating a house someone's been living in. They've made modifications. Things connect in ways the original architects didn't intend. You'll find resources that are no longer in use, but nobody's deleted them. You'll find manual configurations that don't match any documentation. You'll find policies that contradict each other. Bicep should be the target state, but the actual state is going to require cleanup. The principle is straightforward, but often tested in practice. Every new resource should be bicep. Not every important resource. Not every resource we have budget to migrate. Every resource. This means when developers want to deploy something they write bicep. When operators need to make changes, they modify bicep. When new capabilities ship in Azure teams who want them write bicep to implement them. The investment shifts from managing existing messy infrastructure toward building bicep definitions of new infrastructure. Gradually, in this takes time, measured in years, not months. The entire state becomes code driven. Old portal-created resources eventually get decommissioned and replaced with bicep versions. Legacy arm templates get refacted into modules. Manual configurations get captured as code. You don't finish the migration. You complete specific phases. Each phase leaves you in a better state than you started. More predictable, more governed, more maintainable. That's how you transform a brownfield into a controlled platform. The 2026 landscape, what's changing? The Azure platform doesn't stand still and neither does the role bicep plays in it. Looking at 2026, several shifts are happening simultaneously. They're not separate developments. They're pieces of a coherent movement toward infrastructure as the control plane of the organization. Azure blueprints officially disappeared. July 2026. This isn't a surprise that's coming. It's already here. Blueprints were Microsoft's first attempt at solving the "How do we govern at scale problem?" They bundled templates with policies and role assignments. They could assign those bundles to management groups. But they hit a ceiling. They weren't versioned like modern software is. They weren't integrated with CI/CD. They were difficult to maintain. Deployment stacks replace them. Stacks are native Azure resources. They're versioned. They integrate with everything else. They have state awareness. They work seamlessly in pipelines. The migration from blueprints to stacks matters because it signals that governance infrastructure is now treated like application infrastructure. Deployment stacks themselves are getting richer. The deny settings we've talked about, those hard protections against unintended modification are becoming the standard governance pattern. De deny settings prevents certain actions on stack managed resources. This shifts how people think about access control. You don't just ask who can deploy. You ask what they can modify once it's deployed. Life cycle controls around deletion and detachment are becoming more sophisticated. Organizations can now configure stacks differently in different environments. Aggressive cleanup in dev, conservative protection in prod, all expressed as configuration not as separate processes. You deploy infrastructure in bicep. Then separately you'd set up identity integration. It was fragmented. Now identity is part of the infrastructure definition. You define service principles in bicep. Managed identities, conditional access policies, role assignments, all in the same code base as everything else. Identity as code isn't theoretical anymore. It's operational as your verified modules continue expanding. More resources, more patents, more standardization. Where once AVM was a curated collection it's becoming the expected baseline. This convergence means architectural standards become tool independent. You can standardize on how networks should work, how identity should integrate, how compliance should be enforced and then teams can implement those standards in bicep or Terraform depending on their context. The standards aren't locked to a language. Infrastructure code is becoming the control plane. Not the mechanism for deploying things, the mechanism for governing them, for controlling them, for ensuring they don't drift, for assigning ownership, for tracking life cycle.
