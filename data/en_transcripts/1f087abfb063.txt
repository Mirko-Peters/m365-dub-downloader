You define the mapping, you maintain it and you're accountable for its accuracy. External groups are supported as an intermediate step when direct-entra-ID mapping isn't straightforward but they introduce their own complexity. They need to be maintained. They add another layer to audit. The cleaner path, wherever the source systems structure allows it, is mapping source permissions directly to existing enter ID groups. If your service now assignment groups already correspond to security groups in your directory, use that correspondence directly. The fewer translation layers between source permissions and Graph ACLs, the fewer places for drift to accumulate. Permission drift is the operational risk that most teams underestimate at deployment time. Your connector goes live, the ACL mapping is accurate. Users see exactly what they should. Six months later, someone's role changes in the source system. Their service now group membership updates, their Salesforce profile changes. But the connectors ACL, for the items they previously had access to, that doesn't update automatically. The source system moved on, the index didn't. Now you have a window where the data in the index reflects permissions that no longer match reality. Whether that window results in overexposure or under restriction, depends on the direction of the change. Both are problems. Ovexposure is a security incident. Under restriction is a productivity failure that erodes trust in the tool. The answer is a deliberate sync strategy, a process that monitors permission changes in your source systems and propagates those changes to the ACLs on indexed items within a defined window. What that looks like technically depends on your source systems change notification capabilities. Some support web hooks, some require scheduled polling, some require a full recoil to update permissions at scale. Whatever the mechanism, it needs to be designed, documented and treated as an ongoing operational responsibility rather than a one time configuration. When ACL mapping is ambiguous, when you can't determine with certainty which users should have access to a given item, the right default is restriction. Overrestricting access means someone submits a help desk ticket because they can't see something they expected. That's recoverable. Ovexposing access means sensitive data reaches someone who shouldn't have it. That's a different conversation entirely, one that involves your security team, possibly your legal team, and a timeline you don't control. Staged rollout is your proof of concept for the permission model. Deploy the connector to a small test cohort first. Verify that users who should see content see it. Verify that users who shouldn't don't do this deliberately with test accounts at different permission levels before you open the connector to the broader tenant. ACL errors caught in a pilot or configuration fixes. ACL errors caught in production or incidents. Capacity, throttling and the numbers that matter. For a long time, the capacity question was the first thing that stopped large-scale connector deployments before they started. Organizations would map out the systems they wanted to index, count the records, multiply by the seat-based entitlement model, and arrive at a number that made the business case collapse. The economics didn't work for anything ambitious. That changed. Microsoft now includes a 50 million item index entitlement per tenant as part of most Microsoft 365 and Office 365 subscriptions at no additional cost. Office 365 E1 through E5, Microsoft 365 E3 and E5 the F-series plans, government tiers, education plans, the entitlement is broad. Before this shift reaching 50 million indexed items would have required licensing at a scale that put the number above half a million dollars per year for many organizations. Now it's included, the economic barrier to large-scale indexing has effectively been removed. What that means practically is that the governance question replaces the capacity question. The constraint is no longer how much can we afford to index? It's, what should we index and why? That's a better problem to have, but it requires a different kind of planning. Without capacity as a forcing function, organizations tend to index everything which creates a different set of risks. Oversharing at scale, relevance degradation from low quality content, governance debt that accumulates faster than it gets managed. Treat the 50 million item ceiling as breathing room for a thoughtful strategy, not as permission to connect every system without a business case. Now the operational numbers, the ones your engineering team needs before they start building ingestion pipelines. Each indexed item is capped at four minibubbles of past text. That figure refers to extracted text content, not raw binary file size. Microsoft estimates that four minibubbles of past text corresponds to roughly 600,000 to 700,000 words. Around 1,400 pages at 500 words per page. For most structured records like incidents, tickets, and CRM entries, you'll never approach that ceiling. For large documents, detailed technical specifications, lengthy contracts, comprehensive reports, you might. Design your ingestion pipeline to detect oversized items early and either chunk them into logical subitems or summarize them before indexing. Schema complexity, 128 properties per connection. That's a hard ceiling, not a soft recommendation. If your source system exposes hundreds of fields, you're selecting a meaningful subset for indexing. That selection process is actually valuable discipline. It forces the schema conversation about which properties genuinely serve a co-pilot use case versus which ones exist, because the source system happens to carry them. For external groups used in ACL mappings, 100,000 groups per tenant, and a given user can belong to up to 10,000 external groups for search query evaluation. Group administration operations are throttled at 1,000 requests per second. If your permission model relies heavily on external groups at scale, design your provisioning and maintenance processes with those ceilings visible from the start. Throttling behavior itself deserves specific treatment because it's where ingestion pipeline silently fail if you haven't designed for it. When your connector exceeds graphs, rate thresholds, the service returns HTTP 429. Too many requests. With the retry after header specifying how long to wait before retrying, your pipeline must handle this gracefully, not as an edge case, as expected behavior during any significant ingestion run. A pipeline that crashes on a 429 or retries immediately without respecting the retry after window will fail under production load consistently, and the failure mode won't be obvious from the outside. The ingestion pattern that holds up at scale is delta-based sync rather than full recalls. Track the last modified date time field on your source records. On each sync cycle, only pull records that have changed since the last successful run. This keeps your API load proportional to the rate of change in your source system, not proportional to the total size of the index. Full recalls should be reserved for schema changes or permission model updates, situations where you genuinely need to re-evaluate every item. Running full recalls on a schedule because it's simpler to implement is how ingestion pipelines generate throttling pressure that degrades everything else running against the same tenant. The deprecation trap, what's breaking right now? There's a deprecation active in production environments right now that most organizations haven't fully dealt with, not a warning, not a future concern. A system that has already stopped working, quietly breaking security automations that teams built years ago and assumed would keep running. The Microsoft Graph Security Connector for Power Platform was deprecated as of April 29th, 2026. If your organization runs power automate flows, logic apps, or power apps that use this connector, and a significant number due because it was the standard integration path for security alert automation for years. Those workflows are failing when they try to invoke the connector's actions, not degrading, not returning errors you can handle gracefully, failing. The root cause is the intelligent security graph service that the connector was built on. That service has been decommissioned, there's no patch coming, there's no compatibility, Shim Microsoft is quietly maintaining, the underlying infrastructure is gone, and the connector went with it. If you're waiting for a fix, you're waiting for something that won't arrive. The migration path is direct but not trivial. You replace connector actions with HTTP calls targeting the Microsoft Graph Security API directly, specifically you're moving to two endpoints. Security alerts a V2 for alert queries and security incidents for incident level operations. The legacy, security alerts endpoint, the one that underpinned much of the old connectors behavior retires August 31st, 2026. If your migration plan involves that endpoint as a transitional step, your timeline is compressed, but the endpoint change is the simpler part of the problem. The harder part is the data model. The new API isn't the old API with updated URLs, it's a fundamentally different conceptual model. The legacy system was alert centric. Each alert was a discrete object you queried, filtered and acted on. The new model is incident centric, alerts still exist but they're nested inside incidents. An incident aggregates multiple correlated alerts into a single attack story, with related evidence objects, user states, host states, file activity, network connections, structured as typed collections, rather than flat properties on the alert object. That structural change reaches into every workflow you build against the old model. ODATA filters that targeted alert level properties need to be rewritten for the new field names and nesting structure. Evidence queries that previously used flat property paths now traverse object hierarchies. Downstream systems that receive alert data, ticketing platforms, CM schemas, dashboard queries, notification templates, all need to be updated to expect the new shape of the response. This isn't a line by line replacement job. It's a redesign of the data handling logic in each affected workflow, followed by validation that the right alerts and incidents are flowing through the right channels with the right field values. The practical first step is inventory. Map every flow, every logic app, every power app and every bot that touches the deprecated connector. Don't rely on memory or documentation that may be out of date. Query your power platform environments directly. The workflows that have already broken are visible, the ones that haven't run recently enough to surface the failure are the hidden risk. Prioritized by business impact, security alert, triage workflows that feed your SOC are higher priority than notification flows that generate weekly summaries. Get the critical automations migrated first, then work through the rest, running the new and old patterns in parallel during migration, where that's still possible, gives you a validation window before you decommission the old approach. The executive framing for this is simple. If your security automation depends on the old connector, you're not running a slightly outdated system, you're running a system that will fail without warning on any given day because the infrastructure it depended on is already gone. That's not a technical debt conversation, it's an operational risk conversation. The difference matters because operational risk has a timeline and that timeline is now. Microsoft's broader trajectory here is worth noting. The deprecation of the graph security connector reflects a consistent pattern, consolidating older, loosely governed integration surfaces into the core graph API governed by EntraID and modern authentication standards. The path forward isn't a new connector, it's direct API integration with standard app registration, appropriate graph security scopes and admin consent. That's the model going forward for security data integration and building to it now avoids the next forced migration. Real-time data is the clearest boundary.
