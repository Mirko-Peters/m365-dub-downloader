Two permission types govern how applications interact with graph. The distinction between them is a security decision masquerading as a technical one. Delegated permissions operate in a user context. The application acts on behalf of a signed in user. It's constrained by what that user can already see. No more. Application permissions or app-only permissions. Remove the user context entirely. The application authenticates as itself. It uses a service principle. It operates with whatever scopes were granted at consent time. Orgwide, without a user present, it lacks the natural guardrail of a human's access boundary. That distinction matters because app-only permissions power every meaningful graph automation we've discussed. Scheduled reporting jobs and governance enforcement workflows require app-only access to operate across the tenant without a human logged in. But here's the problem. Every one of those app-only grants is a potential lateral movement channel if the credentials are compromised. This isn't theoretical. An attacker who gets a token for an application with mail, read can read every mailbox in your organization. If they get directory, read all they can enumerate every user, group, and roll assignment you have. The permission service your automation relies on is exactly the surface and attacker maps when they're looking for high value access. Treating it as a developer convenience rather than a risk register item is an architectural mistake. The consent governance pattern that actually works starts with documentation. For every application with graph access, you need to document four things. You need the exact scopes requested and the business justification for each one. You need to know the data flows, the application participates in and what happens to the business if consent is revoked. This consent ledger gives your security team the visibility to assess what's actually exposed. It also gives your developers the discipline of justifying permissions before they request them, which predictably reduces scope creep. The app registration hygiene problem in most tenants is severe. Organizations that audit their registrations for the first time consistently find dozens of applications with excessive scopes and no-named owners. They find client secrets that haven't been rotated in years and apps with no documented purpose. Some were built by people who no longer work there. Others were created for a proof of concept that never went to production but was never deleted. Each one is an undocumented attack surface. The operational safeguards that close this gap aren't complicated, but they require execution. You need separate app registrations for development, test and production environments. That means different credentials, different scopes and different audit trails. You need scheduled secret and certificate rotation enforced through an automation pipeline. You also need to centralize graph audit logs and correlation IDs into your CM. Every API call made by every application in your tenant must be visible and queryable when an investigation requires it.
