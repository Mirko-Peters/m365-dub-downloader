Start with the reasons a company ends up with more than one tenant, because the list runs longer than you might expect. When two organizations merge, the company being bought usually brings its own tenant along. Untangling that takes years rather than weeks. And until it's done, both tenants keep running side by side. When a group owns several subsidiaries that run independently, each one often keeps its own tenant, its own rules, and its own administrators. The parent company owns them all on paper and inside Microsoft three hundred sixty five, they're still separate worlds. Then there are the structural reasons, and they're just as ordinary. A company might split part of itself off and hand that part a fresh tenant. It might be required by law to keep data inside a particular region. It might also stand up separate tenants for testing and staging so that a broken experiment never reaches the environment people actually work in. None of those are accidents, and each one adds another island to the map. A test tenant built for a two week project has a habit of outliving the project by years. The last reason is the one that surprises people. Departments and individual employees create their own tenants, often for a quick pilot or a product demo, and they do it without telling anyone. The good news is that Microsoft built a way to find these tenants, and it leans on clues your environment has been giving off all along. Finding the tenants you forgot about. This is the job Microsoft Entra Tenant Governance was built to do, and it starts with something called related tenants. The idea is simple instead of asking you to list every tenant your company owns, Microsoft studies the connections your tenant already has. Then it shows you the other tenants sitting on the far end of them. It leans on three signals to do that. The first is business to business activity, which covers guest sign ins, guest registrations, and administrative access flowing in and out between your tenant and other ones. The second signal is multi-tenant applications that covers apps registered in your tenant that reach into other tenants and apps from other tenants that hold permissions inside yours. The third signal is billing, and that's the one that catches people off guard. Two tenants can be connected for no reason other than a shared billing account. Nobody has to invite anyone and nobody has to sign in anywhere. If another tenant is paying for services through your billing account or yours is paying through theirs, the connection already exists for the system to find. It surfaces on the list without you doing a thing. The beauty of discovery is that it asks nothing of you. You don't have to remember the tenant's name, and you don't have to track down the person who set it up. Those connections do the work, and the list builds itself from evidence that was sitting there the whole time. One correction matters most here, though. A related tenant isn't the same thing as a tenant you own. It simply means there's a live or historical connection worth examining. Plenty of related tenants will turn out to be a partner, or a vendor, or a project from years ago that quietly wrapped up. Seeing them on the list is the whole point, because you can't make a sensible decision about a connection you never knew you had. Knowing which tenants you're dealing with is step one, and acting on that list turns out to be simpler than it sounds. Bringing tenants under one roof. Once you can see the tenants, the next step is deciding what to do with the ones you want to keep. That's where governance relationships come in, and the name makes them sound heavier than they are. A governance relationship is a directional link between two tenants. One tenant governs, the other is governed. And this isn't a merger because the governed tenant keeps its own users and its own data the whole time. Setting one up takes a three step handshake. The tenant that will be governed sends the other one an invitation. The other tenant replies with a request, and that request carries a policy template spelling out exactly what it wants to be allowed to do. The governance tenant then reviews it and accepts and the link goes live. What you get out of that is administrative access from a single place, using accounts in the governing tenant. That beats building and maintaining separate admin accounts in every tenant you manage. And it means fewer sets of credentials to lose track of. That matters because the alternative is grim. Without a relationship, managing several tenants means holding a separate set of administrator credentials for each one. Every extra set is another thing to protect, rotate and eventually lose. There are limits worth knowing before you plan anything. One tenant can govern many, and several tenants can govern one. But a tenant can't be both a governing tenant and a govern tenant. So you won't build a chain where tenant A governs tenant B, and tenant B governs tenant C. That might sound one sided, and it isn't because a governed tenant can always walk away. It can end the relationship whenever it chooses, and the governing tenant receives an email telling them it happened. Two more pieces. Finish the picture. Everything so far has been about moving between tenants. There's a separate set of tools for controlling what happens inside a single one, and the first of them is the administrative unit. An administrative unit is a container that holds users, groups or devices, and it limits an administrator role to just that slice of the organization. Picture a company with support staff spread across three regions with nothing in place, giving one of them the help desk administrator role hands that person power over every user in the company. With an administrative unit, you place the regions users inside the container and scope the role to that container. The person can then only help the people they actually support. A beginner trap hides in that setup, and it's easy to fall into if you assign a role to someone who isn't a member of the administrative unit, the scope of that role becomes the entire tenant. One missing membership turns a careful, narrow permission into a company wide one. Two smaller limits are worth filing away as well. Administrative units can't be nested, and adding a group to one brings the group into scope, but not the people inside it. The other tool in this section is guest governance, and it decides who can join your tenant from outside. Here, the default is more open than you would guess because every user in your organization, including existing guests, can invite external users.
