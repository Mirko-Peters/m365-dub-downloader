DLP is not a compliance checkbox, it's boundary enforcement across data pathways. Which connectors can talk to which connectors, which content can be processed by which experiences and which actions should be blocked, audited or allowed with friction. And it has to be designed like a system boundary, not a moral guideline. Here's what most people miss, connectors are not integrations, connectors are egress routes, they're how data leaves the governed backbone and finds its way into a consumer service, a personal mailbox, a random spreadsheet or now an AI prompt. So when a tenant says we'll just turn on DLP, but continues to allow unrestricted connector pairing in a single environment strategy, what they've built is not governance. It's conditional chaos with a different label. Power Platform makes this painfully concrete. DLP policies classify connectors into business and non-business groups so they can't be combined in the same app or flow. And that's the advertised mechanism. The actual architectural job is deciding what the business group even means per zone. A zone strategy is the only model that survives scale. Green zones for exploration, yellow zones for internal productivity, red zones for regulated workloads, same platform, different risk posture, different connector allowance, different evidence expectations. Otherwise you get the default environment gravity problem again. Everything lives in one place because it's convenient and you try to enforce enterprise controls on a playground. That doesn't work. It just trains people to root around the control, so the DLP control plane output is not a policy exists. Its unsafe pairings are impossible by default in the zones that matter. Now DLP gets even more interesting and more uncomfortable when you bring co-pilot into the estate. The common executive belief is that co-pilot is just a UI over Microsoft Graph. Therefore the existing permission model will handle it. That's only half true. Co-pilot's grounding honors access boundaries, but it also creates new processing surfaces. Trump's, summaries, citations and cross-app experiences where label enforcement is not always consistent. And we have real evidence of that. In January 2026, Microsoft tracked an incident, service advisory CW1226324, where co-pilot chat could incorrectly process confidential labeled emails from send items and drafts, despite configured DLP and sensitivity label controls. Microsoft fixed its server side and stated it didn't grant users access to anything they weren't already authorized to see. That statement is legally tidy, it's also architecturally irrelevant. The lesson is simple. Defects happen at the seams, so your DLP design can't assume labels always block everywhere. It has to assume partial coverage inconsistent enforcement across experiences and the need for monitoring to detect when the system's promise and the system's behavior diverge. That's what control plane thinking looks like. Controlls are layered, tested and observed, not declared. Practically that means you use two DLP modes in parallel. First, power platform connector governance at the environment level. Allow deny lists per zone, restricted high-risk connectors in production, and a clear exception pathway that is engineered as a workflow, not an email to a person. In wave-era tooling, Microsoft keeps adding more admin controls to do this, but the architecture remains the same. You decide where connector freedom exists and where it doesn't. Second, per view DLP for co-pilot and co-pilot chat policies that restrict processing based on sensitive information types and sensitivity labels. And here's the uncomfortable rule. Label-based controls only work if labeling maturity exists. If your tenant labels nothing, DLP protects nothing. If your tenant labels inconsistently, DLP behaves inconsistently. The system can't enforce what you didn't classify. So DLP becomes a forcing function for data hygiene, not because compliance wants it, but because AI makes poor classification visible. People don't fear a folder full of unlabeled documents until co-pilot can summarize them in five seconds. Now none of this means DLP is perfect. There are no limitations, coverage gaps across scenarios, propagation delays, and the reality that you can't always combine every condition in a single rule set the way you'd want. That's not an argument against DLP. It's an argument against betting your entire governance story on a single enforcement feature. The control-plane approach is to design DLP like a boundary layer with assumptions, where it's strong, where it's weak, and what compensating controls exist when it's weak. Identity decides who can attempt access, provisioning decides what can be created and where. DLP decides whether data pathways behave inside acceptable boundaries, and the principle that ties it together is the one most org's keep refusing to accept. Trust is engineered, not assumed.
