The typical enterprise starts here, alert fatigue. Monitoring fires, someone triages, they assign it, the assignee asks for context, then you get the escalation loop. The ticket bounces between teams because nobody owns the whole path. Meanwhile, users keep reporting the symptom, not the cause, so the queue gets heavier while the service gets worse. And buried inside that mess is a simple truth. Most of the incidents aren't mysterious. They're just unknown. They restart worthy, rollback worthy. Apply the known fix and verify worthy. But because humans are the bottleneck, everything queues. Work doesn't close. It turns. So the baseline flow most organizations run looks like this. Detect, triage, assign, remediate, document. It sounds orderly. In practice, it's a game of telephone. Detect is an alert with weak context. Triage is a person reconstructing context across three portals. Assign is guessing who's least busy. Remediate is someone doing the same command sequence they did last week. Document is either an afterthought or a copy-paced narrative written to satisfy process, not truth. Now the autonomy version, the one worth doing, changes the shape of the work. The agentic flow is, detect, diagnose, remediate, verify, close, report. Notice what's missing. There's no assigned step because the system doesn't need to find a human. And there's no document later because evidence is part of the run, not a chore you hope somebody remembers. But this only works if you take ownership seriously. Autonomy doesn't eliminate ownership. It just moves it. Someone still carries the pager. Someone still owns rollback. Someone still owns the change record when the agent makes a configuration update that technically counts as a change. Even if nobody typed the command. So before you let an agent touch remediation, you need to answer the questions most pilot teams avoid because they're inconvenient. What incident classes are in scope? What systems are allowed to be changed? What is the containment unit? Subscription, resource group, specific service, specific environment? What evidence is required before the agent is allowed to act? And what does verified mean for each fix? Because in IT remediation, the action is usually trivial. The blast radius is not. Restarting a service sounds harmless until it restarts the wrong tier, drops connections and triggers a cascade that looks like an outage. Rolling back a config sounds safe until the known good state is from three months ago and today's dependencies are different. Patching sounds responsible until the patch triggers a reboot during business hours because someone forgot to encode a maintenance window. This is why autonomy in remediation is the fastest way to expose whether your control plane is real. If you can't express a remediation action as a bounded, auditable, reversible operation, you shouldn't automate it. Not because automation is scary. Because automation is honest, it executes what you allow repeatedly at machine speed. So in this scenario, the objective isn't let the agent fix everything there. The objective is narrower and more defensible. Let the agent close the predictable incidents that already have deterministic runbooks with explicit thresholds and clean escalation. Diskspace remediation where a cleanup is defined and bounded, service restarts where the verification checks are clear and the rollback is bring it back up and page a human if the health probe doesn't recover.
