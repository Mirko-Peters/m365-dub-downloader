Today we're breaking down Azure network security groups or NSGs for short and by the end of this episode you'll understand what they actually do and why they matter. So what exactly is an NSG? Here's the simplest definition. An NSG is a collection of rules that controls what traffic can flow in and out of your Azure resources. It works at layers three and four of the network model, which means it looks at IP addresses, ports and protocols without digging into the actual content of the traffic. It only checks the envelope, not what's inside. Think of it like a bouncer at a club. You walk up to the door with your IP address as your ID. The bouncer checks where you're coming from, then asks what you want to do and that's where the port number comes in. Are you here to dance on port 80 for web traffic? Or are you trying to cause trouble on port 3389 for remote desktop? Based on that check, the bouncer makes the call allow or deny. Now here's something that surprises most people. NSGs are stateful. What does that mean? If you allow inbound traffic on port 80, the reply traffic goes back out automatically. You don't need a separate outbound rule for the response because the NSG remembers that connection and lets the return traffic through. Compared to traditional firewalls where you'd write rules for both directions, that's a huge time saver. Where do NSGs actually live? You can apply them at two levels, the subnet level or directly to a VM's network interface card, which is the NIC. Subnet level is the recommended approach. One NSG per subnet protects every VM inside it, so it's cleaner and easier to manage without worrying about individual machines. You attach the NSG to the subnet and every VM in that subnet inherits those rules automatically. NIC level is for special cases where you have two VMs in the same subnet that need different rules. Say one is a web server that needs port 80 open and the other is a database server that should only talk to the web server. In that case, you'd apply an NSG directly to the database VM's NIC for extra restrictions on top of the subnet rules. Here's the best part. NSGs are completely free. No extra cost, no per rule fees, no association charges. That's why they're the first line of defense in Azure and there's really no excuse not to use them. Let's open the hood and see how the rules actually work. How NSG rules work? Priority allow, deny and defaults. Every NSG has two sets of rules, inbound and outbound and those rules get processed in a very specific order. Priority numbers start low for highest priority and go up, so a rule at priority 100 gets checked before a rule at priority 200. The first rule that matches the traffic wins. If it's an allow, the traffic passes through. If it's a deny, it gets blocked and no further rules are checked after that. So the order of your rules matters tremendously. Now every NSG comes with default rules baked in. You get three inbound rules. Allow vnet inbound. Let's traffic from inside your virtual network, talk to each other. Allow as your load balancer inbound. Let's the as your load balancer send health probes to your VMs and deny all inbound blocks everything else from the outside. On the outbound side you get allow net outbound, allow internet outbound and deny all outbound. These all sit at very high priorities. 65,000, 65,000, 1,6,500. You cannot delete these default rules because they're baked into the system, but you can override them with custom rules that have a higher priority meaning a lower number. So if you want to allow RDP traffic from the internet, you create a custom rule at priority 100 that allows port 3389 and because that rule gets checked before the default deny, the traffic passes through. The custom rule priority range goes from 100 to 4296 and here's a practical tip. Leave gaps between your priorities. Use 100, 200, 300 instead of 100 and 102. If you need to insert a new rule later, you can slide it in at 150 without re-numbering everything. It's a small habit that saves a lot of headache down the road. Each rule has several components, source and source port, define where the traffic is coming from, destination and destination port define where it's going. Protocol specifies TCP, UDP or any. Action is allow or deny. Priority determines where it sits in the evaluation order. And a name so you can identify what it does. Now one feature that makes NSG's really powerful is service tags.
