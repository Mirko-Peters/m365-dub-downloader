Most organizations come to fabric with the same comforting assumption. If we get the permissions right, the numbers will be right. They are wrong, your real problem is not who can open a report. It's what that report thinks revenue means this week. Fabric feels dangerous because it industrializes meaning. Once a metric definition leaks into the platform, it doesn't stay local. It propagates, mutates, and quietly competes with every other definition you never retired. So the useful question is not how do I lock fabric down? The useful question is, how do I keep my data model from drifting faster than my governance can follow? If you're responsible for enterprise data trust, this is for you. Because fabric is secure by design, your data model will still drift. The only real choice you have is whether that drift is observed and governed, or silent and catastrophic at AI speed. Why fabric feels like too much power? Most people meet fabric through the marketing diagram, one lake, many workloads, everything unified. They translate that as a tool story. Architecturally, it is something else. You've collapsed engineering, warehousing, BI and AI into a single plane all wired into an intra as the decision engine, one tenant, one identity fabric, one logical lake, every role assignment, every workspace, every shortcut participates in a shared authorization graph that is a lot of power in one place. So the first questions you hear internally are completely predictable. Who can see what? Who owns this model? Why do we already have two answers for the same KPI? Those questions aren't new. What's new is the speed at which fabric lets you get to them. Historically, your architecture protected you from yourself through friction. ERP lived over here, the data warehouse team lived over there, Power BI sat on top, usually a release cycle behind. To change a core metric, someone had to fight their way through ETL, schema changes, queue processing and a deployment window. That slowness was annoying, but it also throttled semantic drift. Every change heard just enough that people argued before they shipped a new definition. Fabric removes most of that friction. Direct Lake lets the Power BI semantic model sit almost directly on top of delta tables in one lake, no scheduled imports, no fragile refresh window. Self-service workspaces let domain teams spin up their own lake houses, warehouses and models without ever filing a central ticket. Cloning a workspace or semantic model is a couple of clicks, not a quarter's work. You didn't just modernize performance. You modernize the propagation of meaning. Here's the pattern that shows up in tenant after tenant. Adoptions start small and controlled. One or two central workspaces, a lake house or warehouse, a curated semantic model, a handful of official reports. Everything feels orderly. Then acceleration kicks in, more teams want access. They ask for just a copy of the semantic model to tweak a filter or just our own workspace to move faster. Direct Lake makes that copy basically free, so does one lake's shared storage. Semantic drift follows, those copied models diverge, one region excludes certain customers, one business unit adds a manual adjustment. Another team redefines active customer to meet their local target. The names on the measures don't change, the DAX does. Finally trust collapses. Executives walk into a steering committee and discover three different truths about revenue, churn, risk, all sourced from the same ERP all flowing through the same fabric tenant all protected by the same RBIAC model. Security worked. Nobody broke in. The platform behaved exactly as designed, but the meaning moved. This is why fabric feels like too much power. It isn't that the platform is out of control, it's that your previous architecture hit the fact that you never had a robust way to govern semantics in the first place. The friction made the gaps survivable. Now that friction is gone, the gaps are visible. Direct Lake is a good example of this acceleration. In a traditional import model, changing a schema or measure definition had a natural governor, the refresh process. Break the model and the refresh fails. Someone notices there's a clear failure point. Direct Lake connects the semantic model almost directly to the storage engine. The data is the delta table and the semantic model might keep working just enough to be dangerous. Column still exists, relationships still resolve. Only a handful of reports show subtle shifts in filters and totals. You remove the mechanical canary, self-service workspaces compound this. When every domain can spin up its own engineering, modeling and reporting stack, you've effectively created dozens of parallel semantic layers over the same physical data. Some are carefully modeled, some are copied from wherever someone had access, some are experiments that accidentally become production because an executive bookmarked the report, easy cloning is the final accelerant. Every time someone says, "We'll just clone it and tweak a bit for our needs." You've created another fork of meaning with no life cycle plan. There's no compilation step where a central architect has to approve the new definition. Most organizations respond to this unease by reaching for the wrong lever. They double down on access control. More groups, more conditional access, more workspace restrictions, more reviews of who can see which report, all of that is fine. Necessary even. But here's the uncomfortable truth. You can have perfect RBIAC and completely untrustworthy numbers. You can pass every security audit and still have no idea which revenue metric your CEO should quote to the market. The perceived risk is external, hackers, leaks, regulatory fines. The lived daily risk is internal, nobody believes the dashboards. Fabric didn't invent that risk, it just stopped hiding it behind batch windows and silo tools. So before we go any further, hold on to this distinction. Platform security answers, who is allowed to touch this object? Governance of meaning answers, what does this object actually say? Fabric gives you an excellent answer to the first question. It gives you almost no answer to the second. That's why it feels like too much power. Once you see that clearly the next move is obvious, you have to separate the layers you've been blurring together, security, data governance and semantic governance. Only then can you decide what fabric actually secures for you and where your data model is guaranteed to drift. Security versus data governance versus semantic governance. Once you stop blaming fabric for being too open, you can ask the only question that matters. What exactly is Microsoft securing for you and what isn't even in scope? Most organizations blur three completely different layers into one vague word, governance. Architecturally, those layers are separate systems with separate responsibilities. The first layer is platform security. This is Microsoft's job-entra handles authentication. Conditional access decides under which device, network and risk conditions are token is issued. The workspaces and item permissions express who can administer, contribute or view. One-leg security and role-based access decide which tables, folders or rows a given identity can touch. Underneath that you get encryption, address, TLS and transit, multi-geoboundaries, audit logs, DLP and sensitivity labels through per view and a compliance envelope that already satisfies regulators who are far more aggressive than your internal audit team. In other words, the control plane and data plane are well defended. If fabric were fundamentally insecure, Microsoft wouldn't be running its own financials on the same architecture. That distinction matters because it means your core risk is almost never is the platform safe. The platform is as safe as it is going to get. Your real problem is the second layer, data governance. Data governance is your job. This is where you decide who owns which data sets, which domains exist, which workspaces are allowed to touch regulated data, how long data is retained and how classification flows. You define read and write boundaries on boarding and off boarding, life cycle for lake houses and warehouses and what happens when a project ends but its data assets live on. You decide whether PHI sits only in a healthcare domain. You decide whether payroll data can ever be shortcut into a general analytics lake house. You decide whether every production data set has a named owner or just belongs to BI. When people say we need better governance, this is usually the layer they think they're talking about even if they only ever touch it via tickets to the central team. But there is a third layer and this is where fabric quietly hurts you. Semantic governance, semantic governance answers questions that platform security and data governance don't even try to address. What does customer mean in this model? At what grain is revenue defined? Which filters are always applied when we report active anything? Which team is allowed to redefine that logic? This is the ignored layer, the layer of metrics, business logic, naming and grain. The layer where DAX lives where SQL views define golden tables where notebooks materialize KPI logic in code because there was no certified model available. Most catastrophic fabric failures happen here not in the platform security layer. Nothing in interest stops you from having five different total revenue measures or with the same display name, each applying slightly different filters. Nothing in one lake security complains when three domains create their own customer dimension with conflicting surrogate keys. Nothing in purview lights up when someone silently changes the definition of churned customer from 90 days of inactivity to 30. Fabric doesn't just host semantics. Public industrializes meaning. Once a DAX measure a SQL view or a curated table is shared and referenced its definition stops being local it becomes a dependency. Every downstream report excel workbook data flow and co-pilot answer inherits that meaning until someone forks it, renames nothing and quietly diverges. This is where semantic drift becomes dangerous. Schema drift, columns added, types changed is noisy. Queries break, refreshes fail, engineers get paged, someone notices, semantic drift is silent, the columns are still there, the data types still match, security is still enforced. But the calculation behind net sales has gained an exclusion or lost an adjustment or change time windows. Only the people who lived through the change even remember it happened. Security tooling is blind to this. Data governance catalogs it at best as another data set. Semantic governance asks a different set of questions, which semantic models are authoritative for core entities like customer product revenue, who is allowed to publish or change those definitions, how do we signal to the rest of the organization, which meanings are reusable at scale, how do we detect when those meanings drift. Without explicit answers fabric will happily let every domain define its own truth, secure it perfectly, classify it correctly and serve it at speed. So when you look at your fabric tenant and feel that mix of power and unease, remember the layering, platform security is Microsoft's problem and they've largely solved it. Data governance is your problem and most of you have at least a partial handle on it. Security governance is nobody's problem by default, which means it's where reality quietly diverges from intent. Once you accept that, the next step is to be precise about what fabric actually secures for you and then catalog the forms of drift that walk straight past that perfectly functional security model every single day. What Microsoft fabric actually secures? Now that the layers are separated, we can finally answer the boring but essential question, what does fabric actually secure for you in deterministic terms? Work with identity and access every interaction with fabric flows through Entra, a user, a service principle, a managed identity, they all authenticate there. Conditional access decides whether that token is allowed under your rules. Compliant device, trusted network, MFA, risk level acceptable. Only once that token exists does fabric even enter the picture. Inside fabric that identity hits workspace roles and item level permissions. Workspaces, define, who administers, who can edit, who can contribute data, who only views. Items, lake houses, warehouses, semantic models, notebooks, add their own ACLs on top. One-leg security sits underneath as the data plane gatekeeper. At that layer, you can decide that a particular table is visible to one group and invisible to another. You can constrain access to specific folders in files or enforce row and column level rules at the storage engine, not just in a report. So if someone asks, can user X query table Y through Spark, SQL or direct lake? The system can give you a clear rule-driven answer. That part is solid. Move up a level and you have the data plane itself. One-lake is the logical lake, shortcuts, mirrored sources, native delta and park a files, but from a security perspective, it's still just objects and ACLs. A warehouse table, a lake house table, a KQL database, they're all governed by roles and permissions that fabric and enter evaluate on every request. Workspaces provide a kind of blast radius boundary here. A badly written notebook can still be dangerous, but only inside the capacity and the access scope you've given that workspace. Once add a logical overlay, they don't enforce security by themselves, but they group workspaces so you can reason about where regulated data shouldn't live. On the consumption side, the same model repeats a power BI semantic model, whether it's import, direct query or direct lake, still respects the underlying identity, workspace role and any row or object level security you've defined. If a user isn't allowed to see a customer segment at the table, they won't see it in the visual. The column is masked or hidden at the one lake layer, co-pilot doesn't magically resurrect it in a chat. From Fabrics point of view, every engine, spark, SQL, DAX, co-pilot is just another client of the same authorization fabric. Compliance instrumentation wraps around all of this, per view can scan fabric, register it as a data source, classify assets and apply sensitivity labels that then flow downstream. Activity logs tell you who touched which artifact, when and from where. CLP policies can flag or block data moving out of safe zones, exports to Excel, downloads of PBX, sharing outside the tenant. If you want to answer who has accessed this PHI table in the last 30 days, or where does this highly confidential data set flow? The combination of fabric logs and per view lineage can give you a satisfactory audit trail, so viewed purely as a platform, the picture is reassuring. Authentication is centralized, authorization is consistent, data access is controllable down to rows and columns. This is observable, compliance envelopes are present. If you stay inside that frame, the natural instinct is to keep tightening it, more labels, more policies, more reviews, more conditions. But this is where the earlier distinction becomes lethal if you ignore it. Fabrics is very good at securing objects that is completely agnostic about what those objects mean. You can have two semantic models, both carefully labeled, both living in the right domain, both with restricted access, both with full audit trails, and still have them implement revenue in mutually exclusive ways. From a security standpoint, nothing is wrong. From a decision standpoint, everything is broken. This is why the right question is not, is fabric safe, but what exactly are we securing? The platform can guarantee only the right identities connect. Only allowed tables, folders and rows are returned, only approved paths are used to move data, only labeled exports leave the boundary. It cannot guarantee that the metric you're staring at means what you think it means, that it meant the same thing last quarter. But the AI agent you just enabled is grounded on the correct version of that meaning. So when you hear we need better fabric governance translated, you almost never mean we don't trust entry. You mean we don't know which definitions we've actually put into production, the security model is done, you inherit it. The governance of meaning is not. You design it, or you operate a perfectly secure platform for uncontrolled semantic drift. Where governance breaks, the four drift patterns. Once you accept that fabric secures objects, not meaning you can start naming the ways that meaning quietly walks away from intent. There are four drift patterns that show up in almost every series tenant, access drift, model drift, metric drift, ownership drift. Each one is predictable, each one is cumulative, and each one walks straight past your perfectly functioning security model, start with access drift. On day one, access is simple. A few core groups, a couple of workspaces, clear roles, over time, reality intervenes. Someone important needs temporary access to a workspace just for this quarter. A project team needs broader read rights until we stabilize. Contractors arrive, external partners get guest accounts. Nested groups come in from Entra that nobody fully understands. Because fabric is a collaboration platform, the path of least resistance is always the same. Just add them as a viewer. We'll clean it up later. Later never comes. Those exceptions accumulate. Groups contain other groups, people change roles, but keep access in case they need to help with something. Service principles get granted broader rights because nobody wants to debug a failing pipeline in the middle of the night. Your security posture on paper is least privileged. Your effective access graph in Entra is anything but. The drift here isn't just that more people can see more things. It's that your mental model of who can touch which semantic layer stops matching the actual configuration. You're no longer governing. You're guessing. Now model drift. This is the physical shape of the data. Schemas, tables, relationships and views. A new source system is onboarded. A team adds a temporary staging table that turns into a de facto goal table because somebody built a report on it. A column's meaning changes, but the name doesn't. Someone optimizes a table for performance and silently drops attributes that downstream models depend on. None of this is malicious. It's normal engineering churn. In a traditional warehouse that churn was gated by ETL processes, integration tests, release cycles, in fabric, engineers, analysts and even power users can all participate in changing the shape of the data with fewer barriers. So the tables, your semantic models point at, are not static objects. They are moving targets. Not explicit model contracts and impact analysis. Every schema tweak risks creating a forked reality. One part of the organization referencing the new shape, another still living in the old. Then there is metric drift. This is the semantic layer itself. DAX measures, SQL defined KPIs, calculated columns, business rules embedded in notebooks. Metric drift is what happens when multiple teams use the same words. Revenue, customer, churn, risk. And implement them with different filters, grains or business assumptions. One team excludes internal transfers from revenue. Another doesn't. One region reports churn on a 30 day in activity window. Another uses 90 days. Finance defines active customer as billable in the last period. Sales defines it as any customer with an open opportunity. In isolation, each definition is locally rational. At enterprise scale, they are mutually incompatible. Fabric accelerates this drift because it makes it trivial to clone semantic models, tweak a measure and publish a new version without any central arbitration. Every fork looks trustworthy. Every fork can be certified inside its own workspace. Every fork shows up in co-pilot as a viable source of truth. Finally, ownership drift. This one is less visible but architecturally fatal. On the day a data set or semantic model is created, somebody owns it. There's an engineer and analyst, a product owner. Over time, people change roles. Teams reog, projects end, contractors leave, the fabric item remains. This is accumulate often lay houses, abandoned semantic models, half maintained pipelines. Reports nobody admits to owning continue to refresh because they might be used by someone important. When something breaks, nobody is accountable. When a definition needs to change, nobody has the authority. When AI starts consuming those models, nobody feels responsible for what the agent is saying. Ownership drift turns every other form of drift into unpayable security debt because here is the uncomfortable law of large fabric environments. Drift is not a failure. Unobserved drift is. Diffests will widen, models will evolve, metrics will fork, people will move on, self-service, domain teams and agile delivery guaranteed. If you design as though drift is avoidable, you will always be surprised, always be reactive and always be tempted to blame the platform. If you design on the assumption that drift is constant, then the question changes. Not how do we stop this, but how do we see it, measure it and decide which meanings we are willing to let scale. These four drift patterns are not theoretical, they show up in recognizable, painful ways. So to make this real, we are going to walk through five enterprise scenarios where everything we've just described plays out in public. Finance arguing over revenue, healthcare exposing PHI in the wrong lake house, retail turning self-service into shadow analytics, manufacturing turning one lake into a junk drawer, and AI confidently weaponizing every ungoverned definition you ever shipped. Concrete anonymized but architecturally identical to what's already brewing inside your tenant. Scenario one, finance, same revenue, three answers. Let's start where drift hurts fastest. Finance. Assume for a moment that the plumbing is perfect. You have one ERP, the chart of accounts is clean enough to survive audit, there's a well-understood data export or integration pattern. Finance walks in with a slide that shows revenue for the quarter, 1.02 billion. Sales shows 987 million with a breakdown by region. Operations shows 1.05 billion with an explanation of capacity strain. The chair asks the only question that matters. What is our revenue? Every number is defensible from the perspective of the team that produced it. None of them are aligned. You can watch the psychology in the room shift. First, they question the tools. Is this a fabric issue? Then they question the teams. Why are you all using different numbers? And finally, they question the entire analytics estate. If we can't agree on revenue, what else is wrong? You have just experienced trust collapse. Notice what did not fail here. ERP was fine. Pipelines were fine. One lake was fine. Entra and workspace security were fine. Incedivity labels and audit logs were fine. The platform delivered consistent data to every team, secured access correctly and enforced your compliance envelope. What failed was semantic governance. There was no authoritative, certified semantic model for revenue that all three teams were obligated to reuse. There was no metric owner empowered to say, "This is the enterprise definition. Fabrics simply made the absence of that discipline visible. Fast. In the legacy world, the friction would have slowed this down. It would have taken months for each team to get their own cubes, their own extracts, their own bespoke logic deployed. The inconsistency would still exist. You just might never see all three numbers on the table at the same time. In fabric, the path from idea to propagated meaning is short and smooth. So the lesson here is not "stop sales from building models" or "lock everything down in a central finance workspace." The lesson is simpler and more uncomfortable. If you don't govern semantics, fabric will happily let every domain industrialize its own truth. You'll end up with three revenue numbers, all secured, all audited, all cataloged, and none of them reliably reusable at enterprise scale. This is why in mature tenants, you start seeing a hard distinction between certified semantic models for core entities and KPIs owned and curated by a platform or domain steward, and everything else, local, promoted, experimental, explicitly not authoritative. Without that distinction, you're not doing self-service. You're manufacturing semantic drift at scale. And no amount of tightening access rights will fix a board deck with three answers to the same question. Scenario 2. Healthcare. PHI in the wrong lake house. Finance drift costs you trust. Healthcare drift costs you your license. So take the same architectural pattern and move it into a regulated clinical environment. You roll out fabric in a healthcare organization. On paper you do the right things. There is a clinical domain. There is a research domain. There is an operations domain. PHI is supposed to live only in tightly controlled clinical lake houses and warehouses with hardened workspaces, restricted groups and very nervous compliance officers watching purview. But project reality does not care about your diagram. Across functional analytics initiatives spins up, reducing emergency department wait times, for example. It needs scheduling data, triage codes, lap turnaround times, maybe some patient journey information to see where delays occur. The project team does the natural thing in fabric. They create a new workspace. It sits in the operations domain because that's who's sponsoring the work. They add a lake house. Different teams run to their preferred models. Marketing pulls numbers from their workspace. Pricing pulls theirs. Finance tries to stick to the original certified model, but they've quite added a few measures of their own to keep pace with ad hoc asks. Everyone is technically using fabric. Nobody is using the same semantics. The first sign of trouble is not a big debate. It's a subtle mismatch. A VPC is two different same-store sales percentages in two separate decks and asks, why is your chart showing minus 1.8? And theirs is minus 0.5. Nobody can answer cleanly without opening decks. The conversation that follows is never about decks. It's about trust. Which model is correct? Who owns the definition of same-store sales? Why do we have five different versions of promotion uplift? The honest answer is architectural. In the rush to empower self-service, nobody drew a hard boundary between reusable governs semantics that represent enterprise truth. And local contextual semantics that are allowed to diverge. Fabric didn't create the shadow analytics. It industrialized it. In the Excel era, analysts did all of this anyway, just on their laptops. You knew it was a risk, but it was mostly trapped in files and email threads. In fabric, every fork is a first-class object. Every fork is shareable, discoverable, and consumable by AI. Shadow analytics graduates into shadow truth. Notice again what did not fail. Workspace security was fine. The access was fine. Performance was often excellent. Per view could see all the assets. What failed was semantic governance and platform boundaries. Self-service without semantic boundaries is not empowerment. It is drift with a friendly UI. The mature pattern in retail tenants looks very different. There is a small set of certified domain-owned semantic models for core commercial concepts. Sales, margin, same-store sales, promotion uplift. These are treated as APIs, not convenience layers, and if you want to use those meanings, you reference those models. Other teams are explicitly allowed to build their own metrics, but they must give them different names and those models are never certified as authoritative for the core APIs. In other words, you don't stop analysts from moving fast. You stop them from silently redefining shared words. If you skip that step, fabric will faithfully implement your self-service mandate. And you will wake up one quarter with excellent tooling, fast reports, and an organization that can no longer answer a basic question. When we say same-store sales in this meeting, who's meaning did we just import? Mario 4, manufacturing one-lake as a shared junk drawer. It is not reading parquet files, it is selecting a semantic model, a measure, a filter context. It is choosing one implementation of revenue over every other one that exists in your tenant. How does it choose? By design it optimizes for ease of use, relevance and connectivity. It faithfully reflected the ambiguity you allowed to exist between revenue and commissionable revenue. It was in your belief that revenue meant one thing tenet wide. The same pattern shows up in risk and churn. Another team, months earlier, built a simpler churned customer flag in a different workspace with different thresholds. Both end up in fabric, both are labeled churn. If the simpler flag happens to be in the more active workspace with more dashboards and more daily usage, it will often be treated as the default. Again, from the outside this looks like AI is unreliable. From the inside it is semantic governance debt being called in with interest. Data agents amplify this further. When you build a fabric data agent you select up to a handful of sources, semantic models, lake houses, warehouses, maybe an ontology when I queue mature. You wire tools around them, answer this class of questions, trigger this workflow, summarize these metrics. If you feed an agent a mix of certified and non-certified models because that's what we had, you've just encoded your drift into an API. Every bot built on top of that agent in teams, in custom apps, in operation centers will inherit those ambiguities. Every automation that takes an AI answer and turns it into action will be grounded on whatever meaning was easiest for the agent to reach at configuration time. So here is the high level pattern. Before AI, semantic drift costs you trust in reports and meetings. After AI, semantic drift drives decisions and automation at speed without you in the loop. That is the actual risk curve. And this is why MVPs who live in this space keep saying a version of the same thing. AI doesn't break your data governance. It removes your ability to hide from how bad it already is. Co-pilot is not a hallucination engine. It is a semantic mirror. If you're uncomfortable with what you see in that mirror, the work is not to tune prompts. It is to decide finally which meanings in your fabric tenant are allowed to exist at scale. And to constrain AI to those layers until you've paid down the rest of your semantic debt. Governance is not permissions, redefining the fabric operating model. By now one thing should be obvious. You do not have a permissions problem. You have a meaning problem. So if you respond to everything, we've just walked through by asking your fabric admin to tighten access, you've missed the point. Governance is not the same thing as permissions administration. Permissions answer? Can this identity touch this object? Governance answers. What is this thing who owns it? And when is it allowed to be reused? Those are different questions. They need different teams. Most organizations today run fabric with one of two operating models. On one side you have the central BI police. Everything goes through one team. They own every dataset. They own every model. They gate every change. Self-service is tolerated, but only on the margins. The result is predictable. Long queues, frustrated domains, a flourishing black market of extracts and side systems. On the other side you have pure self-service anarchy. Every domain owns its own workspaces, builds its own models and answers its own questions. The platform team manages capacity and maybe sets some basic guardrails, but semantics are not their problem. The result is also predictable. Most local wins, slow enterprise failures and AI agents grounded on whatever model somebody happened to click first. Fabric needs a third thing. A fabric platform team. Not a ticket queue, not a reporting factory. An architectural function. This team's job is to design and maintain the semantic and structural rules of the environment, not to build every report. They define domain boundaries, which domains exist. Finance, sales, HR operations, clinical retail, whatever matches your organization and which workspaces are allowed to belong to each? They decide with business partners where regulated data is permitted to live and where it is not. They make temporary workspaces, a conscious time-boxed construct instead of an accident, they own workspace strategy. Not in the sense of approving every creation but of defining patterns. Domain aligned workspaces, not endless project aligned ones. Clear dev, test on prod tiers for anything that matters. Explicit isolation for highly regulated domains so that PHI cannot quietly drift into someone's analytic sandbox because it was convenient. They define certification rules. What qualifies a semantic model as certified? What qualifies a table as master data? Who can apply those endorsements and under what process? How do you ensure that there is exactly one certified definition of customer and revenue per domain and that everything else is explicitly second-class? They maintain semantic standards, naming conventions, default grains, time intelligence policies, handling of adjustments and exclusions. The boring work that prevents churn, churned and churn rate from all meaning different things in different workspaces while using identical display names and critically they do all of this as a platform. They do not sit between domains and their work, they sit underneath it. Their mandate is not, you must file a ticket to add a measure. It is we will make it easier to reuse the right meanings than to reinvent them and we will make it visible when you drift. In practice that means designing fabric so that core entities and KPIs live in shared, domain-owned semantic models, discoverable and clearly certified, local teams consume those models by reference, not by cloning and forking when they just need one more slice. If they truly need a different meaning, they declare a new metric with a new name and they own it explicitly. It also means that this team arbitrates common entities across domains. Customers shows up in sales, marketing, finance, support, do you want four incompatible customer dimensions or one shared backbone with domain specific extensions? The fabric platform team forces that conversation before every domain builds its own version. Architecturally this is the difference. You are not governing who is allowed to open Power BI, you are governing which meanings are allowed to be reused at scale. Access control is necessary, it stops the wrong people from touching the right things. Semantic governance is what stops the right people from trusting the wrong things. Once you adopt that posture, everything about your fabric operating model changes. You stop seeing workspaces as folders where people put stuff and start seeing them as surfaces where particular meanings are allowed to exist. You stop measuring success by number of dashboards and start measuring it by percentage of consumption that hit certified semantics. You stop asking your admins to be gatekeepers and start asking your platform team to be designers of an authorization and meaning fabric that reflects how your organization actually makes decisions. And once you have that, you can talk about something practical. How do you stand this up without freezing the tenant for a year? You don't need a five year program. You need a clear charter, some domains and a Dave and 30 pumps 90 plan that turns governance from a slide into a set of irreversible decisions about how fabric is allowed to behave. On that charter you define domains, not just for security, for meaning finance, sales, HR, operations, clinical, retail, whatever maps cleanly to how your organization actually makes decisions. Each domain gets two explicit owners. A data owner accountable for what data is allowed to exist, how it's classified, how it flows, a semantic owner accountable for what core entities and KPIs mean in that domain, which models are authoritative and what gets certified. Certified models are governed, owned and authoritative. They represent the meanings you are willing to let AI executives and downstream systems reuse at scale, they have an explicit owner, documented logic and a change process. Promoted models are useful but not trusted. They are allowed to exist, they are allowed to help people think, they are not allowed to silently redefine revenue or customer for the organization, everything else is experimental and you treat it that way.
