This creates a compounding problem because agents don't just access data. They aggregate it. Data classification has to be a technical boundary, not just a tagging exercise. Sensitivity labels are the signals that control what agents can do with information. These labels need to be applied consistently across the entire organization, meaning every internal doc, customer record, and strategic plan must be classified. The agent relies on these labels to understand what it's touching. If your labeling is spotty, the agent operates in the gaps and treats unclassified sensitive files as fair game. The enforcement tool here is data loss prevention or DLP. Most DLP frameworks were designed to stop human mistakes, like blocking a user from emailing a confidential file to a personal account. Agents make different mistakes. An agent won't try to mail a spreadsheet to Gmail. Instead, it will read a confidential file, summarize the sensitive parts, and include that summary in a new report for a much larger audience. Since the agent generated the new report, it isn't marked as confidential, so the DLP doesn't stop it. The sensitive data leaked through aggregation instead of a direct transfer. Your DLP policies have to evolve to handle this. When an agent creates a document, that file should inherit the highest sensitivity level of any data used to build it. If the agent reads confidential customer info to write a summary, that summary is now confidential. This rule must be explicit and enforced automatically. Then there are information barriers. Marketing shouldn't see product roadmaps and sales shouldn't see internal pricing. With human users, these silos usually hold because of social norms and the fact that nobody wants to be caught snooping. Agents don't have those social restraints. If an agent has the technical permission to cross a boundary, it will do it because the prompt told it to find relevant information. It doesn't care which side of the fence that info is on. You have to enforce these barriers at the tool level as technical constraints. The agent should be allowed to access department A or department B, but never both at once. It needs to be restricted to specific databases and file paths by design. This is the prerequisite layer. You can't build a governance model for agents if your foundation is shifting. You need consistent labels, DLP that understands AI behavior, and technical barriers that actually stop access. The governance operating model rolls and approval gates. Governance isn't a compliance checkbox, it's an operating model. Most organizations are trying to govern agents without actually redesigning how decisions get made, and that's where the model breaks. You need defined roles, not informal, not will figure it out. You need explicit roles with clear authority and accountability. First, you need an AI owner. This is someone from the business side who's responsible for whether the agent actually solves the problem it's supposed to solve and whether the investment is justified. Then you need a technical owner. They handle the implementation, the configuration, and the day-to-day operation. Finally, you need a risk and compliance owner. Their job is to ensure the system doesn't expose the organization to regulatory security or operational risk. These aren't just IT roles, they're cross-functional. The AI owner might be from the department deploying the agent while the technical owner is in IT, and the risk owner is shared between compliance, legal, and security. These roles matter because they create decision accountability. When something goes wrong, you know who owns it. When a cost question arises, you know who answers it. When a risk decision needs to be made, you know who has the authority to make it. Once the roles are set, you build an approval gate for new agents. But here's the thing, it's not a single gate. It's a structured process that gates complexity proportionally. It starts with a use case submission. The team proposing the agent documents what problem it solves, who it affects, what data it will access, and what actions it will take. That submission goes to risk classification. You have to decide if this is low-risk, medium-risk, or high-risk based on the EUAI Act categories and your own risk appetite. If it's low-risk, like drafting meeting notes or summarizing content, it moves fast through the next gate. Technical review confirms the configuration is secure, and the data access is scoped correctly, and then it gets approved. High-risk agents need a different gate entirely. If the agent is screening job candidates, evaluating employees or making credit recommendations, it's not moving fast. How will you detect if it's doing either? You need human in the loop design documented before the agent goes live, and you need escalation rules explicitly defined. You need evidence that you've tested the system for bias and accuracy, and you need proof that a human will review outputs before they affect decisions. Low-risk agents can move faster, but they still need monitoring. You're not approving them once and forgetting about them. You're approving them for pilot operation with the understanding that you'll monitor usage, cost, and behavior after 30 days you reassess. Is it solving the problem? Is it staying within budget? Are there incidents? If the answer to those questions is yes, you can move it to a broader rollout. Beyond the approval gate sits the Central AI governance board. This isn't just ITO compliance. It's a cross-functional committee with representation from IT, business, legal, security, compliance, HR, and finance. This board doesn't approve every agent. The approval gates handle that. These monthly reviews aren't for punishment, therefore learning. You need to know which agents are delivering value and which ones are burning credits without any equivalent benefit. Not tomorrow, not after a meeting. 60 seconds. That requires clarity. You need to know which person can activate the kill switch and whether it requires a single authorization or two sign-offs. When an agent is sending unapproved emails or accessing restricted data, you don't have time to figure out the shutdown process. You need to execute it immediately. This isn't bureaucracy for its own sake. This is the operating structure that separates organizations that actually govern their agents from organizations that deploy them and hope nothing goes wrong. It's the discipline that matters. Start by looking at how your people actually work. You aren't guessing. You're observing. You need to know which roles use co-work the most. The way you buy credits matters more than you think. It isn't because the price per credit changes that much. It's because the terms you accept shape everything that happens next. You need a strategy before you ever sit down to negotiate with Microsoft. Credits come in three models. You need to understand each one to know which mix to use. Pay as you go is your flexible option. You don't commit to anything and you get billed monthly for what you actually used. You're paying the full list price of one cent per credit but you have total freedom. If a project ends or you need to scale down, there is no penalty. This is the right choice for experiments or teams that can't forecast their consumption yet. Pre-purchase plans or P3 are the opposite. You commit to a block of credits upfront for a year to get volume discounts. At 300,000 credits, you get 5% off. At 300 million, that discount hits 20%. Large enterprises can often negotiate another 10 or 15% on top of that. But here is the catch. You're buying a fixed pool. If you don't use those credits by the end of the year, they expire. They don't roll over. They're just gone. You are betting on your forecast. Capacity packs sit right in the middle. You buy a fixed allocation of interactions. Usually 25,000 a month and it renews monthly. It's more stable than pay as you go but you don't get the deep discounts of a P3 commitment. Most mature organizations use a hybrid approach. They buy a capacity pack for their baseline. The work they know they'll do every month. They layer a P3 commitment on top for their expected growth. Then they keep pay as you go, enabled for the overflow. When the baseline hits the cap, it pulls from P3. When P3 is empty, it's built to pay as you go. The economics work because the bulk of your work is covered by discounts. But you aren't penalized for growing faster than expected. Negotiation is where your leverage lives. Most organizations just accept the published pricing. They shouldn't. If you have 500 or more co-workers, you are a material part of Microsoft's revenue for this product. They want your multi-year commitment because it gives them predictability. That is your negotiating room. Benchmark your usage against the discount curve. If you're forecasting 5 million credits a year, you're already in discount territory, but enterprise customers can often push for 25% off the list price. Ask what the volume pricing looks like and don't assume the first number they give you is the final one. The contract terms matter just as much as the price. You need to clarify what happens if you go over your P3 commitment. Does the price jump back to the list rate or stay at your negotiated rate? Ask if unused credits can carry forward if you're showing a clear growth path. You also need to know the renewal timeline and if there's a minimum size for the next year. Finally, remember that vendor lock in is real. Co-pilot credits are specific to Microsoft. If your entire business becomes dependent on this specific model, your switching costs will be astronomical. That doesn't mean you avoid the commitment but it does mean you should build redundancy where it matters. If a critical workflow depends entirely on co-work, you're exposed. If that same workflow could run on Azure OpenAI or another tool if needed, you've protected yourself. The procurement decision isn't just a financial one, it's architectural.
