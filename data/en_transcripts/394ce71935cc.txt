To understand where the money goes, you first need to understand the unit of consumption. And for AI, that unit is a token. A token is roughly three to four characters of English text, not a word, not a sentence, a fragment. The word enterprise is about two tokens. A full paragraph is somewhere between 75 and 150 tokens depending on word length and punctuation. A standard page of business text, around 750 words, runs approximately 1,000 tokens. A dense technical document with longer words, code snippets, or specialized terminology can push 300 to 500 tokens per page. This matters because every co-pilot interaction has two token components, what goes in and what comes out. Input tokens carry the prompt, the context, any documents you've attached, any previous conversation history in the session. Output tokens are the response the model generates, and they're priced differently. On Azure OpenAI, where token pricing is actually visible to customers, GPT-5 runs 1.25 per million input tokens, and $10 per million output tokens. That's an 8x price differential between reading and writing. Output tokens aren't just more expensive. They're often where the waste concentrates because a vague prompt generates a long verbose response. More on that shortly. Now, M365 co-pilot doesn't expose this math to you. The term idle token waste doesn't appear on any invoice. It's not a line item, Microsoft surfaces to admins, and there's no dashboard that calculates it for you. But it's real, it's measurable and aggregate, and it's almost certainly one of the largest controllable cost variables in your AI deployment. Idl token waste is tokens that get billed, but don't contribute meaningful value to any output the user actually sees or uses. Four categories drive most of it. The first is background completions that never reach the screen. The model generates a suggestion. The user switches tabs, closes the window, or types over it before it renders. The inference already ran. The tokens are already spent. The second is suggestions that display but get discarded immediately. The co-pilot auto-complete that appears, gets read in half a second, and gets rejected with a backspace. Generation cost is identical whether the suggestion takes 10 seconds to read or zero. The third is unnecessary context overhead. Large persistent instruction blocks, system prompts injected on every single request, entire repository indexes that get scanned even when the task only touches two files. This context accumulates quietly in the background of every interaction and charges at the full input token rate every time. The fourth and the most expensive is a genetic chatter. Agent workflows that loop through planning, calling, evaluating, and replaning without converging on a better result. Each loop is a full token event. A poorly designed agent that runs six planning cycles when two with suffice doesn't just cost more. It costs three times more and it produces the same output. It lives in the architecture of how agents loop, how context gets injected, how background processes fire, you can fix it with engineering. What's harder to fix is what happens at the keyboard. Most users interact with co-pilot the way they learn to interact with Google, broad query, scan the results, refine, repeat, type something vague, see what comes back at a follow-up, narrow it down. The first message goes in as input. The response comes back as output. The follow-up message goes in carrying the full prior conversation as context. The next response comes back longer now because the model is synthesizing more history. Reasoning models make this worse. When a model runs extended chain of thought processing, working through a problem step by step before producing a final answer, it generates what are called thinking tokens. What traditional automation actually costs? Before you can make a rational decision about where AI spend belongs in your automation portfolio, you need an honest baseline for what the alternatives actually cost, not the marketing version, the unit economics version. Robotic process automation has been the enterprise automation workhorse for the better part of a decade. The cost structure is straightforward. The hybrid automation architecture, the architecture question is no longer binary, you're not choosing between deploy Co-pilot everywhere and stick with RPA. The organization seeing the best outcomes in 2026 are building something different entirely. A layered stack where each component handles the work is cheapest and most effective at performing. Think of it as four distinct layers, each with its own cost profile and responsibility. The interpretation layer sits at the top. This is where language understanding lives. You have incoming email with unstructured text or a document in PDF format with inconsistent layouts or a customer support chat thread mixing technical details with emotional context. These inputs don't fit predefined templates. They require judgment. They require someone or something to read intent and extract meaning. This is where LLMs operate. Classification. Is this email a complaint or a question or a request for information? Extraction. What are the key entities and what does the customer actually need? Summarization. What's the one paragraph version of this 50 page contract? The interpretation layer is pure AI work. The cost scales with token consumption but the value it produces, converting messy unstructured input into clean, structured signal is what makes the entire downstream workflow possible. The execution layer does the mechanical work. Once you've extracted meaning from that email, someone needs to update the CRM record, create a ticket in the helpdesk system, move a file from inbox to archive folder, post a transaction to the general ledger, log an action in the project management system. These are deterministic, rules-based predictable operations. They don't require judgment. They require precision and consistency. This is where RPA or deterministic scripts operate. The cost here approaches zero once deployed because you're not reasoning about anything. You're just moving data and triggering workflows. The marginal cost per execution is fractions of a cent or in the case of a Python script, fractions of a millionth of a dollar. The supervisory layer handles what neither AI nor RPA can safely own. Exceptions. Approvals. High stakes decisions. The email that came in wasn't a standard request. It's a special circumstance requiring human judgment. The transaction looks like a duplicate, but might not be. The contract contains a clause that deviates from standard language and needs a lawyer's opinion before it's committed. Humans handle these cases. The cost is whatever the fully loaded labor rate is for the person doing the review. It's expensive compared to AI or RPA, but it's necessary because the reputational cost and legal liability of getting these cases wrong far exceeds the labor cost of handling them thoughtfully. The control layer is the infrastructure that makes the whole stack defensible. Governance, audit trails, compliance logging, observability into what's happening at every stage. Which AI model ran? What were the input tokens and output tokens? Which RPA bot executed? Did a human approve this transaction before it was posted? What happened when the system encountered an exception? This layer doesn't execute work. It certifies that work happened correctly. The decisions are traceable. That mistakes can be identified and corrected. It's not cheap, but it's how you prove the regulators, auditors and your own executive team that automation is happening under control. The practical mechanics are worth walking through. An email arrives. The interpretation layer. An LLM running a classifier task reads it, determines intent and extracts structured data. Cost fractions of a cent. Output, a decision about what to do next and the key information needed to do it. The execution layer, RPA, or a deterministic script takes that structured data and executes the workflow. It updates the CRM, logs the action, roots the case to the appropriate queue or person, cost near zero marginal expense. The supervisory layer reviews exceptions. The 5 or 10% of cases where the interpretation wasn't confident or the situation requires judgment. The human person makes the final call. The control layer logs everything, creates an audit trail, flags anomalies. The cost advantage of this hybrid model is compound. You pay AI prices, which are variable and token dependent, only for the interpretation work, where human labor would be even more expensive. You pay near zero execution prices for the deterministic work, where RPA or scripts are vastly cheaper than asking an AI to do mechanical tasks. You pay human labor rates, which are expensive, but only for the exceptions and decisions that actually require human judgment. You're not forcing expensive reasoning engines to do mechanical work and you're not using cheap deterministic scripts for tasks that require contextual understanding.
