Most organizations begin with formal structure by looking at the org chart, role descriptions, and approval matrices. That feels logical, but it only gives you the intended model rather than the actual operating one. If you want to understand where power actually sits, you have to start with friction. Look for the places where decisions feel slow, strange, or oddly dependent on specific people, because friction always leaves a trail. That trail usually tells you more about real power than any policy deck ever will. Start by picking a few decisions that actually matter. You don't need to track everything, so just choose a handful of decisions important enough to reveal how the model really functions. This might include access changes for sensitive content, cross-functional project approvals, or budget-related requests where multiple teams and ownership lines intersect. Then ask very simple questions about the process. Who initiates the request? And who has to be consulted before anything moves? You need to know who gives the formal approval, but more importantly, you need to find out who actually unblocks the movement. When the process becomes unclear, who gets pulled into fix it? Find out who knows where the trusted content lives, and who has the technical power to change the access or the workflow if needed. And sequence matters because it separates declared authority from operational influence. Once you do this a few times, you'll notice that patterns begin to repeat. The same names keep showing up in teams, threads, and sharepoint spaces, and the same coordinators or admins reappear at the exact moment a decision either moves or stalls. This is your first real map, and while it isn't a visual one, it is a structural map that traces real dependency. The next step is to compare business responsibility with technical reach. Think one critical business area and ask if the people accountable for outcomes have the access they need to see, verify, and act. This isn't a theoretical question. It's a practical one. If a leader owns the outcome but cannot see the relevant content or trigger a workflow change without rooting through someone else, then the power has already shifted somewhere else. This is where the concept of effective permissions becomes useful. You don't need to read technical logs to understand the core question, who can actually access what right now? It's not about who should have access or who used to have it, but who possesses the ability to act in this moment. And look at where communication concentrates. When things get important, where do the conversations keep converging? Identify the people who bridge multiple teams repeatedly and translate ambiguity into movement. You aren't trying to prove who is politically influential, but rather identifying where the organization has become dependent on human centrality to compensate for weak structural flow. Finally, move to content stewardship. You need to know who owns the critical sharepoint spaces, who updates them, and who decides what information is current enough to trust. After the formal owners with the real stewards in healthy structures, those roles are closely aligned, but in fragile ones, they drift apart. When they drift, information power starts moving away from formal accountability. If you focus on decision tracing, access comparison, and stewardship, you will have more than enough to see where power really sits.
