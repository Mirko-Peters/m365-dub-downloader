What is the model context protocol? Before we go further, you need to understand what MCP actually is. It isn't a database, it isn't another API, and it isn't a traditional connector. MCP stands for Model Context Protocol. At its core, it is a standardized way for AI systems to discover and use the tools you already have. The big idea here isn't just about how to fetch data, but how to let an AI see what is available, understand what it can do, and perform tasks without needing custom code for every single scenario. Anthropic introduced MCP in late 2024, and within just a few months, it was adopted by OpenAI, Google, Microsoft, and Amazon. This happened because every major AI vendor realized they were facing the same infrastructure nightmare that ruined the API world. One MCP server shows a set of tools that are described in a standard format. The action plan, starting your MCP journey, you need a starting point, not a vision, not a transformation roadmap, a starting point. Monday morning, what actually happens? Here is the realistic timeline for organizations moving from where they are now to operating MCP at scale. Month one, audit and prioritize. Start by understanding what you actually have, not theoretically, concretely. Run an inventory of every custom connector, every integration service and every bespoke middleware component your organization maintains. This isn't an exercise in documentation, it's an exercise in honesty, what is actually running, what is in production, what is half maintained and barely understood. You need to identify what is so critical that losing it would break the business and contrast that against what is so peripheral that you are not even sure what it does anymore. Map each integration against a simple criterion. Is this a candidate for MCP? Some integrations are so high throughput or so latency sensitive that they will never move to MCP so you should leave those alone. Other integrations exist only because point-to-point was the only option available five years ago. Those are your candidates. They do not need the performance characteristics of rest. They need the governance and discoverability benefits of MCP. Separate the two groups, you are not retiring everything, you are identifying what could benefit from the shift. Month one, two, metadata cleanup. This is where most organizations delay or skip work. Don't. Before you even think about deploying MCP, your data versus metadata must be written for AI to read. Go through the tables you have identified as MCP candidates and ensure every field has a description written in plain English. Explain what it represents, explain why it exists. These are not technical definitions, they are business meanings. Instead of phone or contact pH, you write customer's primary contact phone number used for support outreach and fraud verification. This work feels tedious because it is. It is also non-negotiable. You are not just cleaning up documentation. You are building the foundation for autonomous systems to reason over your data effectively. Spend the time, get it right. The weeks you invest here prevent months of agent behavior problems downstream. Month two, design your first server. Pick a non-critical domain. Support data is often a good choice and analytics is another. You want something where mistakes have a limited blast radius. Something where you can experiment and learn without regulatory pressure. Design what that MCP server should expose, not everything in that domain, a thoughtful subset. Which capabilities should AI agents invoke autonomously? Which operations require human approval? Which data is sensitive enough that access should be restricted? Write it down. Don't build yet. Design first. Month two to three. Governance framework. Before the server goes live, you need the guard rails in place. Define who owns the server, who approves changes, what approval workflows apply to which operations. You need to determine what audit logging is required and how you will detect misuse. If something goes wrong, you need a plan for how to respond. Write actual policies, not aspirational guidelines. Inforcible policies that your teams will implement. This is the governance infrastructure that keeps MCP from becoming chaos. Month three to four. Pilot deployment. Deploy the MCP server to a pilot group. One business use case. One team, one AI tool. Measure what changes. How long did it take to build the automation compared to if you had used custom connectors? What issues emerged? What worked better than expected? You need to see which governance controls needed adjustment. This isn't production at scale. It's controlled learning. Month four plus deliberate expansion. Once the pilot succeeds, expand gradually. Add another domain, build another MCP server. Retire the custom connectors that MCP now handles. This isn't a race. It's a structured migration. Each new domain teaches you something. Each new server improves your pattern. Each retired connector reduces your maintenance burden. The timeline matters less than the sequence. Order then improve metadata, then design, then govern, then deploy, then expand. Skip steps and you create problems. Rush and you build on weak foundations. The structural shift. What's actually changing? You can understand MCP technically and miss what is actually changing. The protocol is JSON RPC. The architecture is hub and spoke. The tools are discoverable. The governance is centralized. You can comprehend all of that and still not understand the fundamental shift happening underneath. The shift isn't technical. It's philosophical. For 20 years, integration teams asked a specific question. How do I connect this system to that system? That's the question that defined the work. You have Salesforce over here. You have a data warehouse over there. How do you make them talk? You build a bridge. You write code that understands the Salesforce API. Understands the data warehouse schema, translates between them and handles the errors. That's the integration problem. That's what consumed engineering effort and mental cycles for decades. That question is still valid. You still need to connect systems, but it's no longer the organizing question. The organizing question is shifting. Now the question is, what capabilities should this system expose? That is a different question entirely. It is not how do I connect? It is, what do I want others to do with this system? Not what can I technically make possible? But what should be possible? Not how do I write the code? But what does the surface look like? This isn't semantic wordplay. This is a structural shift in how you think about the problem. When you ask, how do I connect? You are thinking about point to point. You are solving a specific problem. You are writing code. You are building the bridge. The code solves that particular problem and then you are done until the next problem arrives. This mindset optimizes for the speed of individual solutions. It focuses on velocity for the current task and getting the connector built. But when you ask, what should this system expose? You are thinking about platforms. You are designing surfaces. You are thinking about all the potential consumers and what constraints should be visible. You are deciding what should be safe and what shouldn't. You are thinking about governance and discoverability. This mindset optimizes for sustainability. It focuses on reusability and letting the system evolve without breaking everything downstream. These questions produce different results. The first question produces a collection of custom connectors. Each one solves one problem. Each one embeds the thinking of the person who built it. Each one handles errors in its own way. Each one implements security differently. Together, they form a patchwork. It works. But it is not cohesive. It is not governed. It is not sustainable at scale. The second question produces platforms. Design surfaces. Explicit boundaries. Intentional choices about what is available and what isn't. Consistent governance. It requires more upfront thinking, but the result scales. The result evolves. The result can be governed. MCP isn't forcing this shift, but it is enabling it. By making it possible to expose tools through a standard protocol that AI can discover, MCP removes the constraint that made point to point feel necessary. Before, you needed custom code for each connection because every consumer was different and every consumer had different requirements. But with MCP and AI automation, you can have many consumers accessing the same tools through a standard interface. That makes platform thinking practical. The organization succeeding with MCP are the ones making this mental shift consciously. They are not deploying MCP as a replacement for custom connectors. They are deploying MCP as a different way of thinking about integration. They are asking different questions. They are designing surfaces instead of building bridges. This is also why you cannot just bolt MCP onto your existing integration infrastructure and expect success. The technical integration is easy. Plug in the connector. Done. But the thinking shift is hard. It requires you to look at your systems differently. You have to ask yourself what capabilities you want to expose rather than just what you need to connect. You have to think about governance and boundaries explicitly rather than implicitly. You have to design for discoverability rather than just for functionality. The organizations that treat MCP as a technical implementation fail quietly. They deploy it, they get some value. But they don't unlock the velocity gains because they haven't made the conceptual shift. The organizations that treat it as a thinking shift, as a fundamental change in how integration should work, those are the ones that get the disproportionate returns.
