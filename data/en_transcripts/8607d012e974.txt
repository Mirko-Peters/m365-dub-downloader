Most organizations treat governance as a gate, a checkpoint, something you pass through before progress can continue. You run and audit, you find problems, you stop. That's the instinct. The LinkedIn case study I want to examine today is perfect for understanding why that instinct fails. An organization discovered 847 orphaned SharePoint sites, zero data classification, and a C-suite commitment to co-pilot that wasn't moving. The governance team's reaction was immediate and predictable. Stop, we need to fix this first. But that reaction reveals something deeper, a foundational misunderstanding about how collaboration systems actually evolve. This episode is about what happens when you stop treating governance as a gate and start treating it as the track the deployment runs on, how you move from not ready to ready enough by sequencing risk intelligently instead of waiting for perfect conditions that will never arrive. The foundational misunderstanding, organic growth versus architectural intent, SharePoint and team's environments don't grow according to architectural plans. They grow organically. This is not a feature of the system. It's a consequence of how work actually happens. A project starts, someone creates a site. People collaborate, documents accumulate, the project ends, the people move on. The site remains. Months later, the site's owner leaves the organization. Nobody transfers ownership, the site is now orphaned. But it still exists, it still holds documents. You get what the case study showed, 847 sites, no documentation, no clear ownership, no life cycle. Most organizations assume this indicates a broken system. That's something went wrong, it didn't. What it indicates is that the system was never designed to manage its own evolution. Often sites are not evidence of a governance failure. They're evidence that governance systems were never architected to detect and remediate often assets in real time.
