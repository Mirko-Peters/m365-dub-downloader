A semantic model usually holds several tables rather than one giant sheet, and each table has a clear job. Two kinds of tables show up again and again. Dimension tables describe the things in your business so products, customers, stores, and dates all live there. They exist so you can filter and group your data. Fact tables hold the events and the numbers like each sale, each order, each temperature reading. Their whole purpose is adding those numbers up on their own. Those tables are just separate lists. A relationship is what ties them together. A relationship lets a column in one table group the data in another. So a month column in a date table can group the sales in a sales table. Picture two tables sitting side by side with a single thread between them. And that thread is the relationship. Every relationship has a one side and a many side. The one side is your dimension table where each row is unique. The many side is your fact table where the same product or date can repeat thousands of times. One detail trips people up here. Nothing in the two tables. A table as dimension or fact, and the relationship itself decides which is which based on that one to many shape. Once the links are in place, filters travel along them. Click a single country on a report and the filter walks from the country table through the relationship into the fact table, and the numbers on the page shrink to match. Clean links make that travel fast and predictable, while messy links make the same. Click slow and confusing. Get a relationship wrong and a filter stops where you expect it to keep going. So your totals miss rows and nobody can see why. Then there are measures, and this is where the model starts doing real work. There's a particular way to lay out all these tables that power BI clearly rewards, and once you see it, a lot of confusing advice suddenly makes sense. That's what we look at next. Star schema. Minus the jargon, the layout. Power BI rewards has a name and it's called a star schema. It comes from the world of relational data warehouses, and the idea is old enough to be trusted. Every table in the model gets classified as either a dimension or a fact, and then you connect them. The name comes from the shape. Put your fact table in the middle, spread your dimension tables around it and draw the relationships between them. The result looks like a star, with one busy table at the center and simpler tables around the edges. The reason this shape works so well comes down to what a report actually does. Every visual on a report does one of two things it filters and groups, or it adds things up. A good model gives you tables built for each job. Dimension tables are what you filter and group with, while fact tables are what you summarize when the model matches the shape of the questions, the answers come back fast. For a beginner, the practical payoff is simple. A star schema is the reason power BI wants clean separate tables instead of one messy spreadsheet. You can flatten everything into a single giant table. And for a quick personal analysis, that works fine. The trouble is that one white table gets slow, hard to trust, and painful to change as it grows. Splitting it into a fact table and a few dimensions keeps it quick and keeps it honest. Every beginner eventually hits where the data lives, and whether power BI copies it or fetches it fresh each time. Import, direct query and direct lake. This is the choice I told you to hold on to, and it's the one that decides how quickly your reports load and how current your numbers stay. It's called the storage mode. And the important part is this. It's a setting on each table rather than on the whole model. You can even mix and match inside one model. The first option is import, and it's the one most people meet first with import power. BI copies the data into the model, stores it on disk, and loads it into memory. When someone views a report because everything sits in memory, it's quick. Power BI also compresses the data hard, often around ten to one. So roughly ten gigabytes of source data can shrink to about one gigabyte. The catch is freshness, because imported numbers are only as current as the last refresh, so you schedule refreshes to keep them up to date. A full refresh throws out all the data and reloads it, which takes time and resources, so you don't want to run one every five minutes. The second option is direct query and it takes the opposite approach. Nothing gets copied. The model keeps only the structure, the tables, the relationships, and the measures. And every time someone views a report, it asks the source database for the numbers directly. That means the data is always live, and it means size is no longer a wall you hit. It also means you inherit the limits of the source. Direct query formulas are restricted to whatever the source can understand. Calculated tables are off the table, and heavy use can slow the whole thing down because every click becomes a live query. Picking between them comes down to two signals. The first is size. When the data is too big to copy and refresh in a reasonable window. The second is speed of truth. When the business needs numbers within seconds or minutes, and scheduled refresh is not enough for a sense of that limit. Scheduled refresh runs up to eight times a day on shared capacity and up to forty eight times a day on premium capacity. If your business needs fresher numbers than that, you're looking at Direct query. The third option is newer, and it only exists inside Microsoft Fabric. It's called Direct Lake, and it was built for the big data problem. Instead of copying everything, it reads Delta table sitting in one lake, the single store for analytics data in fabric and it loads only the columns a query needs into memory. The effect is speed that feels like import without the full copy and the heavy refresh. The detail that makes directly click is what a refresh really does. An import refresh copies the entire data set. Again, a direct lake refresh copies only metadata, which Microsoft calls framing, and it just points the model at the newest version of the files. That kind of refresh can finish in seconds, which is why directly keeps data fresh. Without a nightly copy job, there's a trade off you should know before you get excited. Direct Lake needs a fabric capacity subscription, which is a paid tier, while import and direct query run on any power BI or fabric license, including the free one. So Direct Lake isn't the free upgrade, it's the option you reach for when your data is big, your refresh window is tight, and you already have fabric capacity in place. That cost matters because storage mode is also a bill, and copying terabytes you rarely open is money spent on data nobody looks at. You're not locked into a single answer either. A model can mix storage modes across its tables, which is called a composite model. A common pattern puts the small dimension tables in import for fast filtering and the big fact table in direct query so it stays live. That gives you quick slices and current numbers at the same time. If that felt like a lot. Keep one plane. Rule of thumb in mind if your data is small enough to copy and you can live with a scheduled refresh, use import. If you need live numbers or the data is too large to copy, use Direct query. If you're in fabric, your data lives in one lake and you want big data to feel fast, use direct lake. Notice what just happened.
