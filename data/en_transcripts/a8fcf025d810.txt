Putting it all together, a real workflow example. Let's look at how this actually works in practice. Imagine you're producing a 60-second corporate video about digital transformation. You have one executive character, a man in his 50s wearing professional attire, and the project requires three scenes with 12 total shots. You have a two week timeline to get from a final script to a finished delivery. Week one, planning and reference creation. On days one and two, you write the script and break it down into a shot list. You need 12 shots across three scenes and each one needs a clear emotional beat and specific camera movement. Nothing is left a chance here. You document the camera angle, what the character is doing, and the exact emotion you want to capture so every shot has a clear purpose. By day three, you open co-pilot and ground it in your project assets. You feed it the script, your brand guidelines and your color palette, along with any visual notes you already have. Instead of asking for creative descriptions, you tell co-pilot to generate parametric data for all 12 shots. You want specific optical settings like a 35mm lens at f/1.4 with a 5600K key light from camera left. These descriptions aren't suggestions, they are the constraints that keep the look consistent. On day four, you build your master character document. You need four reference images of your executive showing the front, back side and a close-up of the face. Use the same lighting and the same outfit with a neutral expression for all of them. You also establish a lighting anchor which is the specific color temperature and light position that defines how he looks. You document the wardrobe exactly, noting it's a charcoal gray tactical turtle neck with a fitted crew neck, because any ambiguity here will cause problems later. On day five, you move into seedance to create the master reference pack. You upload your four images and assign your tags, labeling them image one through image four for the different angles. Run a test generation with two simple shots to make sure the identity stays the same across different frames. If the face starts to morph between renders, your references aren't tight enough yet. You might need to fix the lighting or the background consistency before moving on, but this early work prevents hours of wasted time later. Week one, execution, shots one to six. During day six and seven, you generate shots one through three. These are your establishing shots, so keep the camera static or use a very gentle arc movement. Use your parametric descriptions as your prompts and bind them to your image tags. Review every shot the moment it finishes. You're looking to see if the character matches the references, if the motion is smooth and if the lighting hits your anchor. If everything looks right, you move forward. If a shot fails, you adjust it while the context is still fresh in your mind. On days eight and nine, you tackle shots four through six. These are medium shots with slightly more complex motion like a slow dolly in or a turn toward the camera. You have to watch for character consistency across these three shots and make sure they flow with the first three you already finished. Play all six in a row to look for subtle color shifts or changes in body proportions. This is your first real continuity check and if you see the character drifting, you need to tighten your references or dial back the motion. Execution, shots seven to twelve and finalization. On days ten and eleven, you generate shots seven through nine. These are closeups with much higher motion complexity involving hand gestures or the character turning away and back. Your motion strength will likely push toward a four or a five here. If you filmed a motion reference, use it now but if not, you have to prompt carefully, tell the AI to perform a slow, deliberate turn to the left with hands visible and no fast transitions. Day twelve is for shots ten through twelve. These are your final shots and they are usually the most demanding. You might use a crash zoom or a complex interaction between a hand and an object. You aren't trying anything new at this stage, you're just executing the techniques that have already proven to work. On day thirteen, you perform a full sequence review. Play all twelve shots in order and look closely at the details. You're measuring the width of the jaw, checking the spacing of the eyes and looking for any drift in the outfit color. If the sequence holds together, you approve it. If the character looks off in one or two shots, you go back and regenerate those specific frames.
