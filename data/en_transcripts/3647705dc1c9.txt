Role based access control answers three questions about every resource you own, and it asks them again on every single request. Those questions are who can get in, what they're allowed to do once they're inside and how far their reach stretches. That sounds like a lot of machinery, and it isn't. Microsoft describes it as an authorization system built on Azure Resource Manager, the service that receives a request and decides whether to carry it out. Authorization is simply the decision about what a person who already signed in is allowed to do. Picture a building with one shared front door and plenty of locked rooms. Everyone comes through the same entrance since signing in proves who they are, and then each person carries a keycard for the rooms. They need some cards, open one storage closet and some open an entire floor. Nothing in Azure is open. By default. Access exists because someone created a role assignment and it disappears when someone deletes that assignment. No switch flips on a person and no permission turns itself on while you're not looking. So when a teammate can suddenly see a resource they've never touched, the explanation is almost always the same. Somewhere in the hierarchy, an assignment exists, and finding it tells you the whole story. One thing worth clearing up early is that this isn't a paid extra or a premium feature. Microsoft states that Azure RBac is free and included with your subscription. You meet it in the portal on a page called Access Control, where the IAM label shows up at every level of the hierarchy. The payoff is that you can hand someone exactly the access their job requires and nothing more. One person manages virtual machines, another looks after the networks, and a database team manages the databases, all without tripping over each other. The four sizes of scope scope comes in four sizes, and Microsoft lists them from the widest down to the narrowest. They are the management group, the subscription, the resource group, and the resource. A management group is a container that sits above subscriptions, and it exists so large organizations can arrange many subscriptions into one tree. Most beginners never touch one, and you still need to know it's there, because access granted at that level reaches every subscription underneath it. Next in the hierarchy comes the subscription, which is usually the first boundary you meet. It holds your billing, your service limits, and a set of resources that belong together. And for most people, it's the only boundary they ever think about. Below that sits the resource group, a folder inside a subscription that holds related resources. So teams tend to organize them by project, by environment, or by department. The smallest unit of all is a single resource. And it means one thing. It can be one storage account, one virtual machine, or one database. The portal shows this as a drop down, and the level you pick there decides how much of the tree a single assignment covers. Inheritance is the rule that makes this hierarchy matter, and it runs in one direction only. Access given at a parent level flows down to everything underneath it. Assign reader at the management group level, and that person can read every resource in every subscription inside that management group. The reverse never happens because access given deep in the tree doesn't climb back up. The additive rule surprises even more people. Azure works out your effective permissions by adding up every assignment you hold, so a broad assignment cancels out a narrow one. Take someone who holds reader at the subscription level and contributor on a single resource group. Their effective access for that subscription is contributor, because the sum of the two roles is contributor, and the reader assignment changes nothing at all. That's why adding a small role to fix a big problem so rarely works. Group membership adds one more layer. Since assignments made to groups are transitive. If you sit in a group and that group sits inside another group holding an assignment, you inherit those permissions whether anyone meant you to or not. Owner is the biggest of the five. It grants full control over every resource at the scope, and it also grants the ability to hand out access to other people. That second power is the part that changes how you should use it. Contributor grants the same full control over resources with one difference that matters. Someone with reader can look at resources and can't change a single thing, which makes it the safest starting point on any project. Privilege is the name for that habit, and it means giving someone the smallest role at the smallest scope for the shortest time that still lets them do their work. The reason it matters isn't tidiness or paperwork. Limiting the role and the scope limits what an attacker can reach if that account ever gets compromised. So a small assignment means a small blast radius. Microsoft's own guidance puts it plainly avoid assigning broader roles at broader scopes, even when the broader option is more convenient in the moment, because convenience today becomes blast radius tomorrow. Microsoft recommends a maximum of three subscription owners, because every extra owner adds another path for a compromised account to spread. Least privilege isn't a project you schedule, and it's a habit you apply at the moment, you create an assignment. There's one more source of confusion, and it has nothing to do with custom roles. The word role shows up in two places in Microsoft's world, and they're separate systems with separate assignments. Azure roles control access to Azure Resources, which means virtual machines, storage networks, and databases. Microsoft Entra roles control access to directory resources, which means users, groups, domains and licenses. The scopes don't line up either. An Azure Role can be assigned at management Group, subscription resource group, or resource scope. An entry role is assigned at the tenant level, at an administrative unit, or on a single object, such as one application. If you're ever unsure which system you're standing in, ask what the person needs to change. Changing people, groups, or licenses puts you in Microsoft Entra and changing virtual machines, storage or networks puts you in Azure. Plenty of people hold roles in both systems at the same time.
