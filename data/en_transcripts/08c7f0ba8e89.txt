So what does a metadata schema actually need to do for an AI system? There are four dimensions that consistently have the highest impact on retrieval quality, and they map to four different failure modes when they're missing. The first is time and recency. The retrieval layer needs to distinguish between a policy that's currently in effect and a policy that was valid three years ago. Without a reliable effective date, last-reviewed date, or version status field consistently populated across your content, the semantic index treats a deprecated draft as equally authoritative as the current approved document. It has no basis to prefer one over the other, so it retrieves both, blends them, and produces an answer that's partially outdated and entirely untrustworthy. The second dimension is authority and reliability. Not all content is created equal, and the retrieval layer needs to know that. A document published by Legal and tagged as the system of record policy is not the same as a working draft someone saved in a project folder. A proposal that went through approval workflow is not the same as a brainstorm from a team's chat. Without source system tags, owner roles, document type classifications, and approval status fields retrieval treats them identically. The model synthesizes across authoritative and non-authoritative sources without any signal that they shouldn't carry equal weight. The third is topic and taxonomy. When content isn't tagged with standardized business concepts, product lines, regions, departments, process areas, retrieval can't scope correctly. Someone asking a question about imia pricing shouldn't get results from APAC. Someone asking about HR onboarding policy shouldn't get results from the IT onboarding checklist. Without a govern taxonomy applied consistently, the model can't make those distinctions. It retrieves on semantic similarity alone and occasionally mixes contexts that should never be combined. The fourth dimension is confidentiality and audience. This one connects directly to the security concerns from the previous section. Sensitivity classifications and intended audience fields are not just governance metadata. They're retrieval constraints. When these fields are missing or inconsistently applied, the system has no policy level signal about who should and shouldn't see a given piece of content beyond raw permissions. And as we established, raw permissions in most tenants are far too permissive. Now here's what a practical schema review actually covers. You're looking at five field families, provenance fields, source, author, owner, system of record approval status, freshness fields, created date, last reviewed, effective date, expiration date. Authority fields, document type, version, policy tier, review status, access and sensitivity fields, classification, region, business unit, PII flag, and retrieval fields. Topic keywords, language, product, jurisdiction. Each of those field families is a control point in the rag pipeline, prefiltered by document type and approval status before you run semantic search and you've already eliminated a huge class of retrieval errors. Rewanked by recency and source authority and the model sees better evidence, root queries to different corpora based on topic and jurisdiction tags and cross domain contamination drops significantly. Building scalable ingestion pipelines. Clean retrieval depends on clean content reaching the index and clean content reaching the index depends on an ingestion pipeline that doesn't collapse under the weight of real enterprise volumes, real world content velocity, and the geographic distribution of a global hybrid workforce. The framework that holds up at scale is the bronze silver gold pattern, borrowed from data engineering, and applied directly to knowledge graph ingestion. Bronze is raw. You're pulling content from SharePoint, OneDrive, Teams, Exchange, and Connected line of business systems in its original state. Unvalidated, potentially duplicated, inconsistently tagged. Nothing from bronze feeds the knowledge graph directly. Ever, bronze is the intake layer, not the intelligence layer. Silver is where cleansing and confirmation happen. Duplicate detection runs here, structural normalization runs here. You're not yet adding business semantics, you're removing noise. Documents without owners get flagged. Content with missing provenance fields gets quarantined for review rather than past forward. Stale content past its effective date gets tagged for exclusion from the semantic index. The silver layer is your quality gate, and the discipline you apply here determines whether the gold layer is actually useful. Gold is business ready and semantically enriched. This is the content that feeds the knowledge graph. It has consistent metadata across all five field families we covered. It has past quality validation. It carries entity tags that map it to the ontology. It's the content the semantic index reasons over, and it's the content co-pilot draws from when it generates a response. Only gold. Now, the operational challenge for hybrid teams distributed across time zones is keeping that pipeline current without running full rescanse every time something changes. Full rescanse are architecturally incompatible with scale. They're expensive. They introduce latency between content creation and index availability, and they create synchronization problems when content is changing in multiple regions simultaneously. The answer is Delta queries and Wepphook notifications. Delta queries let your pipeline ask Microsoft Graph what changed since the last time I checked. Rather than give me everything again. Wepphook notifications push change events to your pipeline the moment they occur rather than waiting for a scheduled poll. Together, they let you maintain a continuously current semantic cache without the overhead of periodic full scans. If you're building ingestion pipelines for a global deployment and you're still polling on a schedule, you're building for a world that doesn't match your actual content velocity. Chunking strategy is the part of ingestion that most architectures underinvest in, and it directly affects retrieval quality in ways that don't surface until you're debugging poor answers in production. Fixed-sized chunking, splitting documents into blocks of a defined character or token count is fast to implement and genuinely problematic in practice. A fixed-sized chunk that splits mid-paragraph, mid-procedure, or mid-argument strips the context that makes a passage interpretable. The model retrieves the chunk but can't reason over it coherently because the chunk doesn't represent a coherent unit of meaning. Chunk by semantic units instead. Sections. Procedures. API endpoint definitions. Policy clauses. Each chunk should represent a complete thought, and each chunk needs to carry local metadata. Section heading document type version effective date so that when retrieval surfaces it, the re-ranking layer has the signals it needs to evaluate its authority and recency without having to go back to the parent document. The moment data starts flowing into your knowledge graph at enterprise scale, you encounter a problem that pure metadata work can't solve on its own. Different systems use different identifiers for the same real-world thing. Salesforce knows a client as ACMECorp, SharePoint has folders labeled ACME and ACMECorp. It exists a 17 separate, unconnected records each containing partial context, non-containing the full picture. Entity resolution is the process of determining when different records across different systems refer to the same real-world entity and merging them into a single canonical representation. It's not deduplication in the traditional sense, it's not just catching exact string matches. It uses contextual signals, shared relationships, overlapping attributes, co-occurring references to make probabilistic determinations about identity across heterogeneous sources. Dunwell is what turns a collection of related fragments into a coherent node. Recent research on LLM generated knowledge graphs makes the value concrete. Applying entity resolution to graphs built from unstructured content eliminated roughly 40% of entities in relationships, not by discarding useful information, but by collapsing redundant representations of the same thing into single canonical nodes. And despite that reduction in raw graph size, question answering performance improved consistently across multiple rag methods. Smaller, cleaner graph, better answers. The relationship between completeness and quality isn't linear, it runs the other way. There's a precision recall trade-off in entity resolution that matters more than most architects appreciate until they've hit both failure modes. Over-optimizing for recall, capturing every possible match creates what practitioners call monster entities. Knowing the architecture is one thing, knowing where it breaks, specifically, predictably, and repeatedly across organizations that look like they were doing everything right. Is what separates implementations that deliver from implementations that get quietly decommissioned 18 months after launch? The first failure is the one we've referenced throughout this episode without dwelling on directly, the boil, the ocean pattern. A team spends four to six months designing a comprehensive enterprise ontology. Every entity type is modeled, every relationship is mapped, every edge case is accounted for. The term store is immaculate. The documentation is thorough, and then nobody uses it because the first production use case never shipped and the window of executive enthusiasm closed while the architecture team was still debating whether deliverable and artifact should be separate node types. The graph becomes technically impressive and operationally irrelevant. The fix isn't to model less carefully. It's to ship something before perfecting everything. A minimal ontology supporting one working use case outperforms a comprehensive ontology supporting zero. The second failure looks different on the surface but comes from the same root cause, building without users. The technical team constructs a sophisticated graph integrates it with teams and SharePoint, demos it to stakeholders and declares it ready. Adoption numbers come in at single digits. The diagnosis is usually the same, limited user research before design, no co-design with the frontline staff who were supposed to benefit, success criteria defined in technical milestones, rather than behavioral outcomes. A graph that knowledge workers don't trust or don't find in their natural workflow doesn't change how they work. It sits adjacent to how they work and people root around it. The third failure is a tool selection error that's more common than the knowledge graph community usually acknowledges full knowledge graphs excel in specific conditions. When explainability matters, when complex multi-hop relationship traversal is required, when contextual boundaries need to be enforced at the entity level. For generic semantic similarity, finding documents that are topically related to a query, vector search delivers most of the value at a fraction of the implementation cost and complexity. Organizations that reach for a full knowledge graph when vector search or a well-structured search index would have solved the problem faster, haven't made a technical mistake. They've made a scoping mistake. The right architecture for the problem at hand beats the most sophisticated architecture for a problem you don't have. The fourth failure is governance drift. The taxonomy starts clean. The metadata schema is consistent, enrichment quality is high. Then, six months into operation, a team creates a new site with a slightly different naming convention. Another team starts filing documents directly in libraries that were supposed to require content type selection. A third team skips the approval workflow for a new agent because the deadline was tight and it seemed low-risk. None of these individually breaks anything. Collectively, they reconstitute the inconsistency the initial cleanup removed, and it accumulates silently until users start noticing that answers have become less reliable. By the time the degradation is visible in quality metrics, the root cause is distributed across dozens of small decisions that nobody flagged as consequential. The fifth failure is the one that kills adoption fastest, building the knowledge graph as a standalone experience rather than an embedded one, a portal that requires users to navigate away from teams, away from their CRM, away from wherever their actual work lives, to query a separate interface. That portal gets used for demos and not much else. Graph-powered intelligence needs to surface where decisions get made, not where the technology team prefers to expose it, and the sixth as we've established in both the metadata and the rag sections, implementing either layer without the other. Rag without metadata retrieves noisily. Metadata without rag relies on generic model priors. The dependency runs both directions, organizations that treat them as alternatives, instead of compliments consistently under perform organizations that build them as a single coherent pipeline from the start.
