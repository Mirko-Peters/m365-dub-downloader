Her brain has to act as the data hub and because she is busy, she misses a critical conversation that happened three weeks ago in a channel she didn't even know existed. She walks into the renewal meeting with an incomplete picture, the customer feels misunderstood and the deal falls through. Now, imagine the same scenario with a properly designed graph discovery system. The VP asks one question, "What is the complete context for this renewal?" Behind the scenes, the system doesn't just search a single index. It runs a multi-hop query across the entire organizational graph. First, it links the customer and the CRM to the specific team in EntraID. Then it pulls every Teams channel and one-drive folder connected to that group. Finally, it grabs the support tickets associated with those specific users. In a few seconds, it has mapped out every corner of the company where this customer exists. But mapping the data is just the start. The system then analyzes the relationships between these pieces. It notices that a specific feature request mentioned in EntraID is the same one being debated in a Teams chat and noted in the CRM. This is where discovery becomes a knowledge engine. It isn't just finding files, it is understanding how they relate to each other. The VP doesn't want a pile of documents. She wants a coherent narrative of the customer relationship. The AI builds this story using sub-query decomposition. It breaks the big question about renewal risk into smaller pieces like, "What are the open issues?" And "What is the recent sentiment?" It answers each one by hitting the right silo and then weaves those answers together. This synthesis only works because the graph understands the connections. It knows that a support ticket can be linked to a specific user and that a one-drive folder has metadata that ties it to a project. The result is that the VP no longer has to be the bridge between systems. She gets a full clear picture in five minutes instead of 30. She makes a better proposal and has a much higher chance of keeping the client. This is the hidden strength of graph discovery. It doesn't just make things faster. It makes the whole organization smarter by surfacing the connections that humans usually miss when they are trapped in silos. It builds a bridge across departments and transforms raw data into actual intelligence. This foundation of connected data is what allows us to build reasoning systems on top of it. That is where we move into rag pipelines and the world of multi-hop reasoning. The graph rag shift. Reesoning over relationships. The scenarios we've been discussing work because graph discovery doesn't just pull data from a pile but instead it retrieves data that actually understands its own internal structure. This distinction matters enormously when we look at how AI systems process what they find and it's why we need to talk about the shift from traditional rag to something much more powerful called graph rag. Most semantic search systems today use what we call flat rag and the architecture behind it is very straightforward. You take your documents, break them into chunks, convert those chunks into vector embeddings and then you store those vectors in a database. When a user asks a question, the system converts that question into a vector, finds the nearest neighbors in that space and hands those text chunks to an LLM. The problem is that the system treats all text as interchangeable units. So it doesn't care if a chunk came from a meeting note or a support ticket. It has no way of knowing that two different chunks are related because they mention the same customer or project because the system is purely semantic, meaning it matches on general meaning rather than actual structure. This approach has real limitations that show up quickly in a professional setting. It works beautifully for simple questions where you just need to find one specific document but it falls apart on questions that require reasoning across different relationships. If you ask about the dependencies between your product roadmap and the customer feedback from last month, the system has to guess at the connection. It can't inherently understand that a roadmap item is tied to a piece of feedback just because they mention the same feature so it can only match on semantic similarity and hope for the best. Graphrag is different because instead of treating all text as flat chunks, it builds a literal graph. The notes in this graph are entities like customers, features, products, teams, and time periods while the edges represent the relationships between them. You might have a node for a customer that is connected to a feature they requested which is then connected to a roadmap where that feature appears and a specific team that owns it. When you build this graph over your organizational data, you've created a map of not just what was said but how every single piece of information is connected. Here is how that looks in practice. When documents are ingested into a graph-rag system, an extraction layer runs first to identify the entities and relationships within the text. This layer uses an LLM to find mentions of customers, products, and decisions, but it doesn't just pull them out in isolation. It captures how they relate to each other so a support ticket becomes a node connected to a customer and a product while a feature request links directly to a roadmap entry. Once this graph is built, the way we retrieve information changes fundamentally. Instead of just finding the most similar vectors, the system can now follow specific paths through the graph to find answers. If you ask about a customer's needs, the system can move from the customer node to their support tickets, then to the features they requested, and finally, to how those features appear in your roadmap. It is reasoning through relationships rather than just matching on keywords or general concepts. The payoff for LLMs is enormous because when you hand a model a set of flat text chunks, it has to figure out the relationships on its own. That process is context heavy and very prone to errors, but when you hand an LLM a subgraph of connected entities, the model can reason with much more clarity. It knows that two pieces of information are connected because they share a specific entity, which means it doesn't have to guess or infer the connection. Graphs also create a secondary benefit that people often overlook, which is that they allow for summarization at a massive scale. And if your graph is huge, you can't feed the whole thing to an LLM without hitting a context window limit. So graph-rag systems use a technique called community detection. They identify clusters of tightly connected entities and generate summaries for each of those communities. When a user asks a question, the system retrieves these summaries instead of the raw graph, which keeps the semantic meaning and the relational structure while using much less space. This solves the context window problem entirely. Instead of struggling to fit a massive graph into an LLM, you strategically shrink the data by moving from raw details to abstracted summaries. You keep the parts that actually matter for answering the question and you throw away the noise. The computational overhead of building and maintaining these graphs is a real factor to consider, but the benefit is just as real. You get reasoning capabilities that pure vector retrieval simply cannot match. And in the end, you are trading indexing complexity for much higher retrieval intelligence. This raises the central question that architects have to figure out, which is how you build and maintain these graphs without drowning in the cost of running them. Lazy graph construction performance versus cost. The answer to that cost question determines whether graph-rag is actually viable for a real business. Full graph-rag is computationally expensive because you have to extract every entity from every document, identify every relationship, and then cluster the graph to generate summaries. The indexing cost multiplier is staggering, often reaching about 1,000 times the cost of traditional vector indexing. If your vector only rag costs $100 a month to run, a full graph-rag system could jump to $100,000. And for most organizations, that is a complete non-starter. This is where lazy graph-rag changes the equation for everyone, instead of building the entire graph up front. You build it on demand only when a query actually requires that level of detail. Most of the time, your data just sits as vectors in storage where they are indexed for semantic similarity and nothing more. But when a user asks a complex question that needs relational reasoning, the system detects the need and triggers the lazy graph construction. Here is how the process works. A user asks a question that involves multiple hops across the organization, and the system recognizes that this requires reasoning, rather than just simple retrieval. It takes the top results from an initial vector search to find the candidate documents, and then it extracts entities and relationships only from those specific files. It builds a small, focused subgraph that is just large enough to answer the question at hand. And once the answer is delivered, that temporary graph is discarded. The cost difference is dramatic because you aren't processing your entire corpus during the indexing phase. Full graph-rag requires mapping every relationship in your entire database, but lazy graph-rag only performs extraction on the documents that are actually relevant to the current query. The computational work shrinks by orders of magnitude because you are only processing what matters in the moment. To put some real numbers on this, full graph-rag indexing costs usually run at about 100 times the cost of a standard vector pass. Lazy graph-rag adds only about 10% overhead to the retrieval phase. So if your base retrieval takes 100 milliseconds, the lazy construction adds only 10 milliseconds of work. That is the difference between a $100,000 monthly bill and a $10,000 bill, which finally makes graph-rag economically viable for most companies. The trade-off is that you do lose some of the benefits of having a pre-built graph ready to go. A graph that was constructed with global knowledge understands the overall structure of your organization and knows which communities are isolated or connected. It can generate summaries of entire domains, whereas a lazy graph built on demand only understands the local context around the current question. But here is the insight that most architects miss, which is that the big picture isn't always what the user actually needs. When someone asks a specific question, they usually need local reasoning rather than a global perspective on the entire company. They want to understand how the specific entities in their question relate to each other. And they don't need to know about every other entity in the system. A focused graph built on demand is often more than enough, and the system trades global optimization for local precision and cost efficiency. This represents a fundamental shift in how we think about building these systems. In the old paradigm, you build the best possible graph upfront and just paid the high cost. But in the lazy paradigm, you build just enough graph to answer the question. It is a shift from trying to anticipate what users might ask to simply responding to what they are actually asking. The operational agility here is also a major benefit. In a full graph-rag system, when your organization changes or new product launches, you have to rebuild the entire graph in a heavy operation that can take days. In a lazy system, the extraction logic automatically applies to new data as it comes in. When a user runs a query, they get current information because the extraction happens at query time rather than index time, which means you don't have to deal with battery-fresh cycles. This makes lazy graph-rag the practical bridge between vector-only-rag and the full graph model. You get relational reasoning when you need it without paying the high cost when you don't and you get operational agility without sacrificing the quality of the answers. Most importantly, it makes graph-rag affordable enough that architects can actually put it into production. Now that we've solved the cost problem, we have to address a different constraint, which is geography. Organizations today don't operate in a single region and data often lives in multiple countries with different rules. This brings us to the 2026 data residency requirements that are currently reshaping how we think about discovery infrastructure. The EU Data Boundary, residency in the AI era. We have spent a lot of time talking about performance and discovery speed, but by 2026, you cannot have a conversation about enterprise search architecture without also addressing regulation. The EU AI Act does not care about your latency benchmarks because it only cares about where your data lives and who can see it. The European Union created something called the EU Data Boundary and on the surface, it sounds straightforward. Data from EU customers stays in EU data centers. But the reality is far more complicated once you layer in AI systems that require inference and logging infrastructure. Here is the fundamental tension. Your Microsoft 365 tenant sits in an EU region, which means SharePoint, Teams and Exchange, all run from Frankfurt, Dublin or Amsterdam. The data itself never leaves Europe, but when you enable co-pilot or build semantic search on top of that data, the AI model needs to process information somewhere. The LLM inference might happen in a different region and the logs that track what the model did and what it accessed might go somewhere else entirely. That is where the data boundary creates friction. The EU data boundary applies to most of Microsoft 365 and core services like SharePoint and OneDrive commit to keeping customer data within the region. But there is a critical carve-out because certain services are excluded from this commitment. These excluded services are exactly the ones that power semantic search in AI. When you use Microsoft 365 co-pilot, the inference does not happen on your local servers that happens on the global infrastructure of Azure, which includes data centers outside the EU. When your semantic search indexes documents and stores embeddings, the embedding model might be running in a data center in the United States. The logs from these operations, including which documents were retrieved and what context was fed to the model, might be stored in a region that does not have the same data protection guarantees as the EU. This creates a compliance gap. Your primary data lives in the EU, but the intelligence layer that processes and reasons over that data might not. From the perspective of a regulator, this looks like data is leaving the boundary. The customer sees their file in an EU data center, but the AI that accesses it is running outside the EU and the logs of what the AI did are stored outside the EU as well. This violates the principle that customer data should be protected under EU law. The risk here is not just regulatory but operational. If your inference runs in a non-EU region and there is a security incident, you have to notify regulators under different legal frameworks. You might be subject to law enforcement requests from countries outside the EU. Your customer data, which they thought was protected by GDPR, is actually accessible from jurisdictions with different privacy standards. This is why architects need to understand the strategy for geolocking tenants. Geolocking means constraining every layer of your semantic search system to operate within approved geographical boundaries. It is not just about where the primary data sits but about where every copy of that data, every vector embedding, every log file and every inference operation happens. To implement this, you have to make architectural choices. You cannot just enable co-pilot and assume the defaults from Microsoft will satisfy regulators. You have to explicitly configure your tenant to use only EU region services. For inference, you might need to use smaller models that can run locally within your data center rather than calling cloud-hosted APIs that span global infrastructure. For logging, you have to ensure that all operational logs are captured and stored within the region. This creates a trade-off. Running inference locally in a smaller data center or using a smaller model might mean lower latency or better inference quality than using a massive global service. You are accepting some performance constraints to maintain regulatory certainty but that is the choice you have to make if you want compliance with 2026 EU standards. The alternative of ignoring the data boundary and letting your AI infrastructure span globally is increasingly untenable. Regulators are scrutinizing this closely and the fines for violations can be severe. More fundamentally, customers in the EU do not want their AI to run outside their legal jurisdiction. This does not mean you cannot have a performant modern semantic search system in Europe. It means your architecture has to be explicit about boundaries and it has to make intentional choices about where each component runs. It has to treat regulatory requirements as architectural constraints from day one not as something to bolt on later. Compliance is not just about where data sits, it is about how the entire intelligence system is governed. Metadata lineage, proving the AI's why. Compliance requirements shift once you understand that the AI generating answers needs to prove where those answers came from. This is lineage and by 2026 having it will not be optional. The EU AI Act and related regulatory frameworks are making auditability a legal obligation rather than a technical nicety. But understanding why this matters requires stepping back from the infrastructure and looking at what happens when an AI system makes a mistake. Imagine your semantic search system retrieves a document and feeds it to an LLM. The model generates an answer that a user acts on, but later it turns out the document was outdated or the context was misinterpreted. A customer makes a decision based on this answer and it costs them money, so they demand to know why your system told them something that turned out to be wrong. This is where lineage becomes critical. Without lineage, you are in an impossible position. You can see the answer the system generated and you can see that it was based on some document, but you cannot trace back to show exactly which document it was or when it was added to the index. You cannot prove who had access to it, whether the user was authorized to see it or how recent the information was. You are forced to say you do not know and in a liability situation that is the worst answer you can give. With proper lineage, you can answer every question. You can show the exact document that was retrieved and prove when that document entered the graph via a delta event. You can show the specific embedding that was calculated and the vector that was matched. You can demonstrate that the user requesting the answer had proper permissions to access that source document and you can even trace back to show which previous query or update created the data in the first place. This chain of evidence is what regulators call full data lineage tracking. It is the ability to reconstruct exactly what happened when a system made a decision at any point in the future. For high-risk AI systems under the EU AI Act, this is mandatory. By 2026, you must maintain records that show the data lineage for any output that affects the rights or opportunities of a person. The mechanics of capturing this lineage start at the graph API layer. When a document is ingested via a delta query, that delta carries metadata, including the timestamp when the change occurred, the user who made the change and the specific properties that changed. This delta event becomes the authoritative source of truth marker for when data entered your system. When that data is converted to a vector embedding, you have to attach provenance. So the embedding has metadata pointing back to the delta event that created the source document. When retrieval happens, you log which embeddings were selected, what the similarity scores were, and what context was assembled. When the LLM generates an answer, you capture the prompt that was sent to the model, the model version, and configuration, and the exact tokens that were generated. All of this information is linked back through a chain from the answer to the prompt, the retrieved context, the embedding vector, the source document, and finally the delta event. This chain is decision lineage. Decision lineage is what makes the system defensible. When someone asks why the AI said that, you can follow the chain backward and show the decision tree. You can show the exact data that influenced the output, and if the data was incorrect, you can identify when it became available and who had access to modify it. If the user was not supposed to see that data, you can prove it was a security failure rather than a data problem. If the source was outdated, you can show exactly when it should have been refreshed. This level of auditability requires discipline. Every component in your discovery pipeline has to be instrumented to capture and pass forward this lineage metadata. Your ingestion system has to log which delta events it processed, and your embedding model has to tag vectors with their source. Your retrieval pipeline has to record which items were matched and why, and your LLM interface has to log prompts and generations. None of this happens automatically because it has to be built in deliberately. The operational burden is real. You are storing metadata alongside your primary data, and adding logging overhead to every query. You are creating compliance records that have to be retained for years, but the alternative of operating without lineage is no longer acceptable in 2026. Regulators expected, customers expected, and auditors will demand it. Understanding lineage requirements forces you to a critical decision. You either build this infrastructure yourself or you buy a solution that already has it baked in. Buildverse buy. Native connectors versus custom pipelines. The decision to build lineage-aware discovery infrastructure eventually brings you to a fork in the road and most architects delay this choice until they run into a wall. You have two real options here. You can use Microsoft's native graph connectors and lean on their existing ecosystem, or you can build a custom pipeline that gives you more control, but demands constant maintenance. Microsoft's approach is purpose-built for enterprises already living in Microsoft 365. Their connectors handle the heavy lifting like schema mapping, permission resolution, and batching logic, and they even manage throttling automatically, so you don't have to. Because they handle the complexity of the graph API, you get the benefit of their experience at scale. They have run into almost every edge case imaginable and fixed most of them over the years. These connectors come with built in monitoring and diagnostics, which means when something breaks, you can contact Microsoft support instead of debugging the code yourself, but there is a critical constraint you need to consider. Microsoft's native connectors are designed for a specific set of data sources like SharePoint, OneDrive, and Teams. They work beautifully if your data lives in Microsoft 365, but what happens if your critical knowledge lives in Salesforce or Confluence? If your customer feedback is spread across Zendesk, Gira, and custom line of business applications, the native connectors simply won't reach those systems. You could try the Microsoft connector marketplace and hope someone built a connector for your specific source, but depending on third-party connectors, introduces a lot of risk. If the author stops maintaining the connector or your data source updates and breaks the integration, you are stuck. This is where custom pipelines start to look attractive. You write the code that connects to your data sources directly, which gives you total control over the extraction and transformation logic. You can handle edge cases specific to your organization, and you can prioritize which data to index first and how aggressively to refresh it. Since you aren't constrained by Microsoft's design decisions, you can implement whatever integration pattern makes sense for your specific architecture. But here is the problem. There is a hidden cost that most architects underestimate, and that is the operational burden. A custom pipeline isn't a one-time project you can just finish and forget. It is a service that requires ongoing maintenance because your source systems will change and APIs will eventually be deprecated. If your source system pushes an update that changes the API, your pipeline breaks immediately. You have to diagnose the failure, write code to handle the new API version, and then test and deploy the fix. Meanwhile, your search index is getting stale because the pipeline isn't running. Think about the personnel cost for a moment. You need engineers who understand both your source systems and the graph API, and these people have to be on call when the pipeline fails. They have to maintain deep documentation, so the next person who touches the code understands how it works. If you have multiple custom connectors for multiple data sources, this scales into a massive engineering burden. You aren't just building a one-off integration anymore. You are running an entire platform. Then there is the risk of divergence. If you build a custom connector, your implementation of graph-rag or security trimming will be slightly different from how Microsoft does it. You will discover unique edge cases and bugs that require custom workarounds. Over time, your custom code becomes a pile of special cases and messy conditional logic. New engineers coming into the code base will struggle to understand why a bizarre check exists online 247 and the code becomes fragile. Simple changes start to break subtle dependencies that nobody remembered were there. Microsoft's first party connectors avoid this trap because they are standardized. Microsoft handles the complexity, so your job is just to configure and monitor them. The investment curve is shallow. You spend some effort upfront learning how the connector works, but then the maintenance burden stays low for the long haul. The strategic question is simple. What is your core competency? If you are an organization that values deep integration across many different systems, and you have strong engineering resources, building custom pipelines might be the right call. You gain flexibility and control, but you are also committing to those ongoing maintenance costs. If Microsoft 365 is your primary system and you want to minimize operational complexity, using native connectors makes more sense. You gain simplicity, but you lose coverage of non-Microsoft data sources. There is also a timing dimension to consider. When should you prioritize completeness versus immediate freshness? A native connector will give you freshness quickly because Microsoft has already optimized them, but you might have to wait for them to expand support to your specific data sources. A custom pipeline lets you ingest everything immediately, but the freshness might lag because you are building the entire infrastructure from scratch. Most mature organizations settle on a hybrid model. They use native connectors for core Microsoft 365 systems because those are heavily used and Microsoft's optimization is too valuable to ignore. They build focused custom connectors for critical non-Microsoft sources and avoid trying to integrate every possible data source. They accept that some systems will stay separate and that is okay. This pragmatic approach balances completeness, freshness and the operational burden. Once you have settled on how data flows into your discovery system, the next constraint is the infrastructure itself, infrastructure collocation, cutting the network cord. Once you have decided which data sources to integrate and how to handle compliance, you face a constraint that architects often overlook until they are debugging production latency issues. That constraint is the physical location of your infrastructure. Every network hop adds latency when your vector database sits in one data center, your graph API gateway sits in another and your LLM inference runs in a third, you are paying a latency tax on every single query. These aren't theoretical delays. A round trip between data centers can add 50 to 200 milliseconds of pure network overhead. When your target retrieval latency is 250 milliseconds total, losing 150 milliseconds to network hops means you have only 100 milliseconds left for actual computation. That is a massive squeeze on your performance budget. The solution is architectural. You need collocation. Your vector database and your graph API end point need to live in the same data center or at the very least the same geographic region. This isn't just a preference. It is non-negotiable if you want to hit sub-second discovery targets. When these systems are collocated, you can make multiple calls to the graph API to pull fresh data or check permissions without incurring network latency between each call. The calls happen locally across internal network fabric instead of traveling across internet scale distances, but collocation alone isn't enough. The communication protocol between these systems matters enormously. Traditional HTTP, which underpins most rest APIs, was designed for request response patterns over the public internet. Each request opens a connection sends headers, waits for a response, and then closes. For a single query, this is fine. But in discovery pipelines, you are making dozens of calls to check scopes, fetch delta tokens, and retrieve permissions. If each of these calls opens a new HTTP connection, you are drowning in overhead. This is where GRPC becomes critical. GRPC uses HTTP2 under the hood, which multiplexes multiple logical streams over a single TCP connection. You open one connection to the graph API gateway and send dozens of requests through it simultaneously. The server streams responses back as they are ready, which means no connection overhead and no handshake delays. It is just efficient by directional communication. The latency savings from switching to GRPC are dramatic. Measurements from production systems show that the same discovery pipeline running over HTTP can take 300 milliseconds. If you switch to GRPC, that same pipeline drops to 80 milliseconds. That is a 3.75x improvement. The difference isn't in the computation itself. It is purely in how efficiently the infrastructure moves bits. This creates a ripple effect through your entire architecture. If you are using Python for your discovery gateway, you are already at a disadvantage. Python is excellent for data science and rapid prototyping, but it isn't optimized for the networking patterns that modern semantic search demands. Python's global interpreter lock means that handling multiple concurrent requests introduces contention, adding GRPC to a Python service adds overhead from the language runtime's perspective. This is why serious production deployments are shifting to compiled languages like Rust or Go for the discovery gateway layer. These languages are built for concurrent networked workloads. A Go service using GRPC can handle thousands of concurrent requests with minimal overhead. A Rust service can be even more efficient, offering performance characteristics that approach the theoretical limits of the hardware. The gateway becomes invisible. It is just a fast pass through that handles multiplexing and connection pooling without adding latency. This architectural shift represents a maturation in how enterprises deploy semantic search. You aren't trying to do everything in one language anymore. Instead, you are using the right tool for each layer. You use Python for data science and model training, but you use Go or Rust for the performance critical networking layer. Specialized services handle each function. The operational impact is also significant. A Python-based gateway that is struggling with concurrent load requires adding more instances and distributing requests across them, which adds operational complexity. A Rust gateway handling the same load runs on a single instance with headroom to spare. It is a simpler deployment with fewer things that can go wrong. But here is the thing. Collocation and efficient protocols only matter if the systems themselves are designed to take advantage of them. This brings us back to the discovery pipeline design. The queries you make to the graph API have to be structured to minimize redundant calls. You batch requests where possible you cache responses and you pipeline operations so that subsequent calls only happen when you actually need their results. The infrastructure enables this efficiency, but your application design has to execute it. This foundation of collocated infrastructure, modern protocols, and efficient gateway implementations is what makes the next phase possible.
