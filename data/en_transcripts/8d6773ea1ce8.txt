Using agent blueprints from theory to practice. Now you understand what the control plane does, how conditional access enforces policy and how the registry provides visibility but where does the policy itself come from and who decides what agents can and cannot do. A blueprint isn't a feature but a governance artifact that represents a structured decision about what a category of agents is allowed to do. It defines how their permissions are scoped and what controls apply to them, encoding organizational policy directly into the identity system. This replaces the old model of relying on manual processes or just hoping that teams will follow the guidelines. Think of building a blueprint as answering a series of specific questions about what the agent is supposed to do. You need to define this in concrete operational terms rather than marketing language. An IT support agent triages tickets, gathers diagnostic information and suggest solutions and that is its entire scope. When you authorize flows from that single definition and everything outside that scope is restricted, the blueprint captures this in five distinct dimensions starting with identity which covers how the agent authenticates. It tracks whether the agent uses a managed identity, a federated credential or a certificate focusing on the token issuance mechanism. This ensures the agent's credential can be rotated automatically rather than requiring manual intervention. Permissions then define exactly what the agent can touch such as whether it can read from one specific SharePoint site or the entire environment. Policies define the controls that wrap around those permissions including conditional access logic, DLP rules and approval workflows. These aren't decided for every individual agent but are set at the blueprint level and inherited automatically. Life cycle rules specify how the agent evolves over time, determining when it should be reviewed and if approval needs to be renewed quarterly. It also sets the maximum lifespan and determines what triggers the agent to be disabled automatically. This data captures the contextual information that makes governance possible by identifying who owns the blueprint and what risk tier it falls under. This metadata is searchable and queryable which is how you answer questions about which agents can access customer data. Organizations typically design blueprints along risk tiers to provide a practical way to segment governance complexity. Low-risk blueprints govern agents that read internal knowledge bases like documentation, FAQs and process guides. These agents don't touch sensitive data and can't modify anything so they operate with minimal human oversight because the downside of them acting autonomously is just wrong information rather than system damage you can approve these quickly and let them run. Medium-risk blueprints cover agents that can read and modify internal systems such as an HR workflow agent or procurement agent. These need human approval for certain operations especially rights. And while they can read data autonomously, modifications require explicit authorization. You review these quarterly or whenever their permissions change to ensure they stay within their intended scope. High-risk blueprints protect agents that touch sensitive data or execute critical operations like an agent accessing customer financial information. Every single operation requires human in the loop approval meaning the agent cannot act autonomously at all. It exists to assist, gather information and recommend but the human always makes the final decision. Here is a concrete example of an IT support agent in the medium-risk tier designed to triage support tickets. Its permissions include read access to service now tickets in the knowledge base but it is restricted from closing tickets or accessing customer data. If the agent encounters a critical issue like a production outage, its approval workflow forces it to escalate to a senior technician. The life cycle is reviewed quarterly and any changes to its permissions require explicit security team approval. The governance benefit is multiplication because you design the policy once and hundreds of agents inherited. When you need to tighten controls or respond to a security incident you simply update the blueprint. Every agent using it inherits the change immediately which eliminates per agent reconfiguration and prevents policy drift.
