With limited time, which lead or deal should a seller focus on first? Predictive scoring, which leads and deals deserve attention. Now, a seller can only make so many calls in one day, so a long list of leads creates a simple problem. Where do you start? Predictive lead scoring gives each lead a score based on patterns from past sales results and it works like a priority number that helps sort the pile so sellers can spend their first hour on leads that show stronger signs of becoming customers. The system learns from the sales data already in Dynamics 365 sales by looking at past leads that turned into opportunities or customers, then compares their details with leads currently in the system. Depending on how your company records sales work, that could include the lead source, company details, job role, the activities around the lead, or other fields your team uses. This isn't magic. It asks a simple question based on past data. When leads with details like these appeared in the past, how often did they move forward? The answer becomes a score that helps a seller sort a crowded list. Imagine 200 leads arrive after an event and without scoring, someone might work through the list by date, by name, or by instinct. With predictive lead scoring, the seller can begin with the leads that look more like earlier successful leads than work down through the rest with a clearer plan. That doesn't mean low scoring leads belong in the bin. A new market, a new product, or a lead with incomplete details can receive a lower score even when the person could become a great customer. The score helps direct attention but doesn't decide who deserves respect, effort, or a reply. Opportunity scoring works in a similar way, but it looks at deals already moving through the pipeline. An opportunity is no longer just a possible customer. It may include an estimated amount, a close date, people involved, sales activities, and a stage such as qualification or proposal. Predictive opportunity scoring uses patterns from earlier sales outcomes to estimate how likely an active deal is to close. You can also see whether that score rises or falls over time, and that movement can start a useful conversation. A score that rises may match fresh activity and progress, while a score that drops can tell the seller to check what changed rather than waiting until the expected close date passes quietly. A high score isn't a promise though. No score can tell you that a buyer has approved a budget, chosen your company, or signed a contract. Real sales work still depends on customer needs, competition, timing, trust, and the people making the decision, so treat the score like a signpost that helps you navigate, not a goal. The quality of the score depends heavily on the quality of the data. If sellers leave lead sources blank, never update deal stages, enter close dates that don't mean anything, or record wins and losses in different ways. The model has bad data to learn from. A system can't find reliable patterns when the history tells mixed signals. Clean records don't need to be perfect. They just need to be consistent enough that the past resembles the real sales process. When a team agrees on what a qualified lead means, when stages change and how it records outcomes, predictive scoring has a much better chance of producing useful guidance. Here's a scenario you've probably seen. A deal looks totally healthy on paper, but the customer relationship is slowly going cold. Relationship analytics catches that early by looking at contact activity around an account, lead, or opportunity. It takes your team's communication patterns and turns them into a clear health view, so you can see whether people are staying connected or starting to drift apart. Think about a large account with several people involved. One seller emails the project lead regularly, a colleague meets the finance contact and a manager talks with an executive. Those separate interactions tell a wider story and relationship analytics brings that story straight into your sales record. So where does this data come from? The system pulls information from Dynamics 365 sales and Microsoft 365 when your organization connects those sources and LinkedIn adds more relationship context if your company uses the related LinkedIn tools. You don't need to chase every single signal. Instead, look for changes. Maybe an account that used to hold regular meetings now shows very little recent activity or a newer opportunity starts building contact with several people. Those patterns need different attention. Relationship analytics shows this through a bubble chart, which at first glance may look like one of those screens people open, not at, then never visit again. But the idea is simple. Each bubble represents a customer relationship and its position and size help you spot accounts that need attention or deals where engagement is growing. A large opportunity with limited recent contact should be a warning sign, but it doesn't prove the deal has failed. The buyer might be busy, a decision might sit with another team or a planned meeting might still happen. Still, the seller now has a reason to look closer, which is far better than finding out months later that the customer chose someone else. Relationship health isn't about judging the customer. Let's talk about conversation intelligence. A sales call often contains details that never make it into the CRM record. The seller might write good conversation, send proposal, but the actual call could include a concern about price, a mention of a competitor, a question from finance and a clear deadline from the customer. Conversation intelligence helps the team look beyond that short note. It works with recorded sales calls that your organization is allowed to record and the tool analyzes the spoken conversation to bring forward topics and patterns like product names, tracked words, questions, competitor mentions, and sentiment. Instead of searching through a long recording from start to finish, a seller or manager can jump straight to the moments connected to those signals. That makes the call easier to review. Suppose a customer repeats a question about how a product works with their current systems. The seller may answer it well or they may rush past it because they want to finish the presentation. When that question appears in the call review, it gives the team something real to discuss. Sentiment needs careful handling, conversation. Intelligence can detect signals about tone, but it can't read every human situation correctly. A customer may sound direct because they are busy and another might sound positive while privately leaning toward a different supplier, so treat sentiment as a clue. If the tool detects a change in tone during a pricing discussion, the seller can listen back and ask what the customer reacted to. The answer might point to price, timing, scope, or nothing serious at all. The recording provides the context that a single label can't provide. For managers, this changes coaching. Without a recording, a manager often hears the seller's memory of the call. The buyer liked the demo, the pricing conversation went fine, and a follow-up is coming. Now, a manager can review those moments with the seller. The conversation becomes specific.
