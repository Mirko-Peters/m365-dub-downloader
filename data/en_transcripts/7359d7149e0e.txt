Example two, power automate approval chains and the invisible approval collapse. A better place to see this risk is inside approval workflows where automation looks the most convincing while actually getting weaker underneath. I recently worked with a global company that moved their procurement approvals into power automate and the early results were great. Request moved through a standard path, managers got instant notifications and finance finally had visibility, giving leadership a perfect example of digital progress they could show off to the board. For a while that was the story, but the process itself was never actually redesigned. It was just wrapped in a digital skin. Over the next 18 months, that approval setup expanded to absorb every single historical exception the business had ever created. Regional variation stayed in place, legacy policies were never retired and special rules for specific vendors remained active because nobody wanted to remove a branch that had a political sponsor. Instead of simplifying how decisions were made, the flow became a massive container for all the ambiguity the business refused to resolve. By the time we stepped into review it, the company was running 27 different versions of the flow with over 40 exception parts. On paper, the procurement process was fully automated but in reality, the actual approval time had drifted from two days to nine. The process looked digital, but the outcome was objectively slower. That gap is where the danger lives because leadership could see a live dashboard, they stopped questioning whether the process actually worked, the standard response became, "We already automated that." And once that sentence takes root in a company, the internal review usually stops. The process enters a protected status where the mere presence of automation is treated as proof of health, even though the flow was just executing years of unclear handoffs and fuzzy ownership. That is why I believe one leading indicator matters more than almost any dashboard people are currently tracking. The exception rate. If you want one honest signal for processed debt in an automated environment, you have to start there. The exception rate is the number of manual overrides and flow breaks divided by the total number of automated executions. This metric does not care how polished your app looks or whether your adoption numbers are high. It tells you whether reality is refusing to fit the path you designed. If you are under 5%, you are usually in a healthy range. Between 5 and 15%, you should start asking what changed in the business. If you are above 15%, you aren't scaling consistency anymore. The answer is to put friction back into one specific place where it creates visibility instead of waste. The 30-day fix inject friction where it creates insight. So what do you do with this? The answer isn't more bureaucracy and it definitely isn't a giant redesign program that stalls your progress for six months. You need a small control surface. You need to add it fast, specifically in the places where your system currently hides too much. I call this a friction injection layer. The goal here isn't to slow down work across the board, but to place short deliberate checkpoints where they can expose ownership and drift before those problems start to compound, start with ownership tagging. Every flow, every automated intake, and every co-pilot facing knowledge pattern needs a named business owner instead of just a technical maintainer. That is a hard distinction to make. While platform teams can support the automation, they cannot be the ones who silently inherit accountability for business logic they don't actually control. If nobody in the business owns the outcome, the system will keep running while the process behind it slowly degrades. Next, you have to put expiry and review rules in place. A massive amount of low-code mess survives simply because nothing ever expires, which means teams remain open and flows remain live long after the original business need has changed. You should set a simple review rhythm where any workflow or automation showing no meaningful activity for 90 days triggers a review. This doesn't require a massive board meeting. It just requires a single decision to keep, simplify, archive or remove the asset. That one move creates structural exits and that is exactly what most fast automation setups are missing today. Then you need to make exception logging the default. This matters because leaders usually only see the successful runs. Even though the rest of the organization feels the manual work happening around the edges, manual overrides and human fixes happen every time the official path stalls. And if those moments stay invisible, your process dead stays invisible too. When every override becomes a data point, you stop arguing from anecdotes and you finally start seeing the true shape of the mismatch. After that, add lightweight approval for new automation patterns rather than every individual build. This is where most governance programs go wrong because they turn every small change into a gate, which eventually forces people to root around governance completely. You don't need that. You need patent review. If someone wants to introduce a new category of approval logic or a new AI-supported workflow, that should pause briefly for a check. However, repeating an already approved patent inside clear boundaries should never be slowed down. That balance matters because your job isn't to create drag. Your job is to move friction upstream before hidden complexity spreads downstream into your operations or your audit logs. The leadership upside here is actually bigger than just gaining control. You get cleaner visibility into what is actually running and you get much clearer accountability the moment something starts to drift. This creates better signals for simplification instead of endless patching. It also creates the conditions for faster delivery later on. Mostly because your teams will stop stacking new automations on top of structures that nobody fully trusts. From there, the operating rhythm stays simple. You review the exception rate monthly and if it crosses the warning range, you investigate. If it crosses the chaos threshold, you stop scaling that process and simplify the work before you automate anything else around it. That is the discipline. It is an anti-automation.
