When you create a VNet, the first big decision is picking an IP range. You do that using CIDR notation. CIDR is short for classless interdomain routing, but all that really means is you're saying, "I need this many IP addresses." Why does this matter? Because the range you choose determines how many resources you can fit in your virtual network. Here's how it works. CIDR uses a slash and a number like 16 or 24. That number tells you the size of the address space. A 16 gives you about 65,000 IP addresses. That's a lot. A 24 gives you about 250, which is perfect for a small team or a single application tier. So when you're designing your VNet, you need to think about how many resources you'll have and how you want to group them. Now you can't just pick any IP range. Azure follows the RFC 1918 standard, which sets aside private IP ranges for internal networks. You have three options. The 10.xx-dx range is the largest given you plenty of room to grow. When you deploy a resource inside a VNet it gets a private IP address by default. That's the address it uses to talk to other resources inside the VNet. Think of it like an internal phone extension. Only people inside the building can reach it. Private IPs are only accessible from inside the VNet or from networks connected to it. If you're sitting at home on your laptop you can't reach a private IP in Azure. It's not on the internet, it's inside your private network. So how do you make a resource reachable from the internet? Now public IPs come in two flavors. Static and dynamic. A static public IP stays the same even if you stop and restart the resource. A dynamic public IP can change. And when you do need them, think about whether you need static or dynamic and whether a prefix makes sense. Network security groups controlling traffic. So you've got your vnet, your subnets, your IP addresses sorted out. But how do you actually control what traffic is allowed in and out? That's where network security groups come in. And NSG is the gatekeeper that controls what traffic enters and leaves your resources. Think of it as a bouncer at the door of a club, checking IDs, looking at the guest list, and turning away anyone who shouldn't be there. Every piece of traffic that tries to reach your resource has to go through this bouncer first. NSG's work with rules. Each rule says either allow or deny based on four things. The source IP address, the destination IP address, the port number, and the protocol. So you can say allow traffic from my office IP address on port 443 or deny all traffic from the internet on port 22. That kind of control. Now here's a powerful feature. You can attach an NSG to a subnet or directly to a single VM's network interface. If you attach it to a subnet, that NSG applies to everything inside, every VM, every database, every resource, it's a blanket policy. If you attach it to a specific VM's network interface, it only applies to that one machine. You can even do both. A subnet level NSG for broad rules and the NIC level NSG for exceptions. The default rules that Azure creates are pretty sensible. They allow all traffic within the vnet, so your resources can talk to each other. And they deny all traffic from the internet, so nothing from outside can reach your resources unless you explicitly allow it. That's a good starting point. Let me give you a common example.
