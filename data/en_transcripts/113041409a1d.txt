The persistent agent as a new identity type. We need to step back for a moment. Most organizations are only just starting to grasp what is actually happening here. This entire conversation about governance, observability, and oversight. It isn't really about managing tools better. It's about something much deeper. Agents are no longer tools. They are identities. This isn't just a change in how we talk. It's a change in how we build. When you treat an agent like a tool, like Slack or Salesforce, you manage it as a capability. You turn it on. You configure it. You delete it when you're done. The organization stays the same because tools just extend what humans can already do. They don't change the nature of the workforce. But when an agent becomes an identity, everything shifts. It gets its own credentials in your directory. It has its own permissions and its own audit trail. It has a life cycle. At that point, it becomes a participant in your workforce. It isn't just helping a human anymore. It is executing work on its own. It makes decisions, consumes resources, and holds authority. This changes everything about your organizational structure. Think about your human workers. They have identities in active directory and specific roles they fill. They have responsibilities and they are held accountable for the work they produce. You manage them through HR systems, track how long they've been there, and offboard them when they leave. Now look at your agent workers. They will have identities in entra and specific permissions scopes instead of job titles. Their responsibilities are written into code instead of job descriptions. Accountability is baked into an audit trail instead of a performance review. You will manage them through governance systems rather than HR. But the parallel is unmistakable. This creates a massive management challenge. Large companies are used to managing thousands of people through a clear hierarchy. There is a command structure where everyone knows who reports to whom and how approvals work. Agent workers do not fit that model. An agent doesn't have a manager. Its authority comes from its configuration and its duties come from its design rather than an assignment. You might have thousands of these agents running at the same time. Some are specialized, some coordinate with others, and some only trigger when a specific event happens. They aren't organized around people. They are organized around workflows. So how do you actually keep control? How do you maintain visibility over thousands of autonomous workers moving at machine speed? The answer is that you don't manage them like humans. You govern them. Agent 365 provides the infrastructure but the actual policy has to come from your leadership. The people who understand your risks and your processes have to decide how agents are created and when a human needs to step in. This makes the question of accountability very difficult. When an agent makes a mistake, who is actually responsible? Is it the person who built it, the team that deployed it or the business owner who signed off on the workflow? In theory, it's everyone. In practice, you need a clear ownership model where every agent has a human owner. That person is responsible for monitoring the agent and pulling the plug if things go wrong. From architecture to implementation, understanding why you need persistent agents with governance layers is a start. But it isn't enough. Understanding doesn't build systems. Execution does. And execution is where most organizations fail. The shift from, this is what we should do, to, this is what we're actually building requires a different kind of thinking. The theory says you need three layers, a runtime where agents execute, a control plane where they're governed, a set of identity and policy mechanisms that make everything auditable. In practice, building those layers requires sequencing decisions that most architects get wrong. The mistake is starting at the wrong level. Organizations often begin by asking which model they should use or which cloud provider is best. They wonder if they should build custom infrastructure or use a platform. But those are the wrong first questions. They're optimizing for decisions when they should be optimizing for learning. Start instead at the smallest possible scope. One agent, one workflow, one controlled environment, not production, a sandbox where you can experiment without risk. The goal isn't to build something that scales. The goal is to build something that teaches you what actually matters. Pick a workflow that your organization already understands. Not the most complex one. Not the one with the highest business impact. Pick something you can explain in a single sentence. Maybe it's a customer service agent that handles basic inquiry routing. It could be a code review agent that flags common patterns or an expense report processor that validates receipts. The workflow should be achievable with current technology. The constraints should be obvious. You're building to learn not to win. In that sandbox, implement the identity layer. Create an Entra agent ID for this agent. Don't use a shared service account. Don't use an administrative identity. Give this agent exactly the permissions it needs to do that one workflow and no more. This is the moment when you discover what least privilege actually means in practice. It's harder than it sounds. Your agent will need access. You didn't anticipate. And it'll hit permission boundaries. You didn't know existed. You'll need to iterate on what you granted. This is valuable because this is how you learn what governance actually requires operationally. Implement the session layer next. Use the SDK you've chosen and configure session persistence. Make the agent resumable. Test the recovery path. Close the browser. Restart the machine. Verify the agent can recover its state. This is where you discover whether your session storage is working correctly. You'll see if your backup and recovery mechanisms are adequate and if your data synchronization is actually synchronizing. Only after those two layers are working, do you add orchestration? Can your agent call another system? Does it have the identity to authenticate? Can it pass the response? What happens when the system is calling is down? These aren't theoretical questions. They're operational realities that will break your system in production if you don't understand them now in the sandbox. Then comes governance, instrument logging, configure audit trails, set up dashboards that let you see what your agent is doing. Run it for a week, generate thousands of events, try to reconstruct what happened during that week from the logs alone. You'll discover gaps. You'll find events that aren't logged. You'll discover that your audit trail doesn't tell the story. You need it to tell. Fix those gaps now in the sandbox, not after you've deployed. Only when those four layers are working in isolation, do you think about combining them? And only when you've combined them in a single controlled environment, do you begin planning the next iteration? The scaling reality is this. You won't scale a system that wasn't designed for scale.
