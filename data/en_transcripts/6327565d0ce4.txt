The main types of extensions. So where do extensions actually come from? It's not just one place, and knowing the difference helps you pick the right one for your business. The first type is called an app extension. Think of it as a packaged add-on that handles a specific job inside Business Central. For example, a shipping app can connect your orders to a carrier. A payment app can take card payments or send details to a payment service. A reporting app can add the reports your team needs every month. An app extension is like buying a ready-made tool for a job you already know you need done. You install it, set it up for your company, and your users see the new function right inside Business Central. It might add its own pages and fields, or place options inside pages people already know, like sales orders, purchase orders, or customer cards. Some app extensions come from Microsoft themselves. They supply add-ons for needs that don't apply to every company or every country. A local legal reporting pack is a good example. Tax rules, invoices, payments, and official documents differ from place to place, so one country may need functions another never uses. That separation keeps the standard product focused, while still giving companies a way to meet local rules. Then we have ISV extensions, ISV stands for independent software vendor. It sounds like a big technical phrase, but it really just means a software company that builds an app for many Business Central customers. An ISV might focus on warehouse work, retail, manufacturing, document handling, payroll links, electronic invoices, or industry reporting. Instead of building the same function from scratch for every customer, they create one app that many companies can buy or subscribe to. These apps usually appear in Microsoft App Source, the online marketplace for Microsoft Business Apps. App Source is useful when your need is common. Usually lots of companies want to connect Business Central to the same shipping service, bank or payment provider. Instead of paying someone to build that connection just for you, you may find an existing app with support, setup notes, and regular updates from whoever built it. That can save a ton of work. Still, an App Source app isn't automatically the right choice just because it exists. You need to check that it fits your process, your country, and your version of Business Central. A good app solves a clear problem without forcing your team to change every part of their daily work around the app. The next type is a per tenant extension, often shortened to PTE. A tenant is your company's own Business Central environment, so a per tenant extension is an app built for your company alone, not published, for every Business Central customer to install. Imagine a food distributor with a very specific way of calculating delivery charges. Prices might depend on temperature control transport, delivery zones, returnable crates, and customer agreements built up over years. That process is probably too unusual for a standard shipping app. So a Business Central partner or developer can build a per tenant extension around that process. The app stays within that company's Business Central setup and it can match the way that company works. It may include a one-off link to an older system, a special approval process, or a screen built around a job only that business does. But custom doesn't always mean better. A per tenant extension gives you a close fit, but someone has to own it. That means keeping notes, testing changes, and arranging support when your company changes the process, or Business Central gets updated. If another company could use the same function, an existing ISV app might bring less long-term work. So how do you choose? Start with the Business need, not the type of extension.
