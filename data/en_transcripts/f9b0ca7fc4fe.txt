When the approval status stored on the item, it's harder to lose the trail. You're not hunting across flow run histories and emails to figure out what happened. But lightweight approvals have a hard boundary. They are not a compliance workflow engine. They don't model complex branching, conditional attestations, dynamic evidence collection, or multi-path escalation. They also don't replace your need for centralized monitoring. They're a front and gate that works when the policy is simple and the blast radius is contained. Now custom power automate approvals. This is where people go when they need real workflow behavior. Branching based on metadata, parallel versus sequential approvals, conditional routing, reminders, SLA's exception handling, and recorded justifications. And yes, power automate can do it. The problem is scale and life cycle. A power automate approval flow is a software artifact. It needs versioning. It needs a deployment model. It needs ownership that survives turnover. And it needs to be designed so that an approver can't be bypassed by someone cloning a flow and tweaking one condition. The uncomfortable truth is that power automate makes it easy to create production logic without production discipline. That's why compliance teams end up with approval sprawl. Dozens of nearly identical flows, each slightly different, each with its own connector assumptions, each with its own failure mode. So the rule is strict. If you use custom flows for compliance, you standardize them as managed assets. Solutions, environments naming owners and run visibility. No personal flows, no, I'll just fix it in my account. EZN says, "Temperties in beer, how is it?" That's not automation that's delegated risk. Now back-end orchestration logic apps are Azure Functions. This is for when the workflow is either high volume, high complexity or high consequence. High volume means you can't tolerate per-run UX, throttles, and fragile connector behavior. High complexity means you're transforming content calling multiple systems using cues and you need deterministic retreatries and idempotency. High consequence means a mistake creates legal exposure. Records declaration, hold enforcement, regulated exports, or irreversible state changes. In those cases, the workflow belongs in an orchestration layer that behaves like software. Source control, deployment pipelines, unit tests where possible, structured logging, and a clear separation between decision and execution. This is where the approval becomes a service boundary. The approval interaction can still happen in SharePoint or Teams because humans need a place to click buttons. But the system that interprets that approval and performs downstream actions lives in the back-end where you can actually control it. And here's the anti-pattern to call out clearly. Approval logic scattered across personal flows. That design fails three ways at scale. First, it destroys audit defensibility because you can't prove which version ran and why. Second, it breaks continuity because the flow dies when the owner leaves or changes their password or loses a license. Third, it creates inconsistent enforcement because two teams will implement the same policy differently and both will swear they're compliant. They aren't. So the decision matrix is simple. If you need a lightweight gate and the state should live on the item, use lightweight approvals. If you need richer branching, but the process stays inside the team boundary use power automate approvals but only as a governed managed asset. If the workflow crosses systems, crosses tenants, touches regulated data at scale or must survive organizational entropy, move it to back-end orchestration and treat approvals as inputs, not as the engine. And that sets up the next scenario because external collaboration is where approval stop being workflow questions and become boundary questions. Scenario three framing. External sharing is a controlled zone, not a per-side preference. External sharing is where SharePoint stops being collaboration and becomes a boundary negotiation with your legal team. And the foundational mistake is predictable. Organizations treat external sharing as a per-side preference, a toggle, a convenient setting the site owner can adjust when the partner relationship feels urgent. That model is not governance, it's delegated risk with a ribbon on it because once you allow external sharing as a local preference, you don't get one external sharing posture. You get hundreds, each one with different link types, different expiration behaviors, different guest controls and different expectations about what safe means. Then the business asks for co-pilot. And suddenly your partner side is no longer a quiet place to upload documents. It's an AI-grounded knowledge surface that can summarize, search, and surface content at machine speed. So the framing for scenario three is strict. External sharing is a controlled zone. A zone is not a site template. A zone is a policy boundary with dedicated defaults, dedicated monitoring and dedicated lifecycle rules. It exists because external collaboration is not a feature request. It's a threat model. Start with the problem reality. Partner workspace is happened constantly. Vendors, joint ventures, auditors, contractors, client delivery teams. And even if IT blocks external sharing broadly, users will still share externally. They'll email attachments, use personal storage or spin-up shadow tools. So no external sharing is not a strategy. It's denial. The strategy is provide an approved path that is safer than the bypass. That path is the external collaboration zone. It's a dedicated site or hub structure where every site inherits hard and defaults. No anonymous links required authentication, enforced expiration, restricted download where needed, and labels that reflect the data types allowed in that zone. If the business needs to collaborate with partners, they do it here, not in random internal project sites, not in HR team sites, not in someone's personal one drive. This is also where you stop pretending that site owner education scales. Owners change. They get busy. They forget. They inherit sites. They didn't create. And they will make exceptions under pressure because the partner relationship matters more than your policy document. So the zone has to survive convenience pressure by design. Policy is inherited and enforced, not configured repeatedly. Now the uncomfortable truth about external sharing. It's not just about guests. It's about links. Most leakage happens through links that outlive intent. Links created for a short term partner. Links forwarded to the wrong person. Links never revoked. Links that keep working after the contract ends because nobody remembered they existed. So the zone model treats link creation as an auditable action with life cycle. Exploration is the default, not the exception. High risk labels require shorter expiration. And if the business needs no expiration, that's an exception with explicit owner approval and ongoing review. Then guest life cycle. Guests aren't users. They're temporary risk allocations. So every guest needs sponsorship. And that sponsorship has to be enforceable. If a guest loses their sponsor, they don't remain in the tenant as a ghost identity with access. Nobody can explain. They get reviewed and removed. If a partner project ends, guests get deprovisioned automatically, not when someone remembers. That means external collaboration zones need life cycle hooks, start date, end date, renewal prompts and a clean tear down path. Otherwise, your tenant becomes a museum of expired partnerships with valid access. Now add GDPR and data sovereignty pressure because this is where regulators stop accepting we didn't mean to. If PII enters an external zone, you need deterministic controls that prevent it from spreading. Sensitivity labels that restrict sharing behavior DLP rules that block obvious exfiltration and an audit trail you can produce without panic. External collaboration is where least privilege becomes real because you are now collaborating with identities you don't fully control which is why the zone is not optional. It's the only way to separate internal collaboration ergonomics from external boundary enforcement without trying to do per-site exceptions forever. And yes, business units will ask, why can't we just enable external sharing on this one internal site? Because this one internal site becomes 50. Then 500, then it's normal, then your data classification doesn't mean anything because anything can be shared from anywhere with the right owner clicking the right button. That is the drift model. So scenario three starts with a design decision, not a tool decision. External collaboration happens in controlled zones with hardened defaults and everything else is internal by default. Once that line exists, you can implement it with Entra B2B controls conditional access sensitivity labels and DLP and that's the next section. The architecture that makes cross-tenant collaboration survivable. Scenario three, architecture, cross-tenant collaboration with least privilege and continuous audit. The architecture for external collaboration has one job. Make working with outsiders behave like a controlled system, not a series of one-off exceptions. Start with Entra B2B because that's where the boundary becomes enforceable. Guests need a defined onboarding path and that path must be policy-driven. Who can invite? Which domains are allowed, what terms apply and what happens when the sponsor disappears? A guest is not a user object you tolerate. It's a time-bound access allocation with an accountable human attached to it. That's why sponsorship and life cycle matter more than the invite itself. If the sponsor leaves, access should not quietly persist. The system must trigger an access review, then remove the guest if no one reclaims ownership. That is not bureaucracy. It is how you prevent former partners still downloading files from becoming a quarterly tradition. Then conditional access. External collaboration without conditional access is just a shared folder with better marketing. Require MFA for guests. Block legacy authentication. Apply device controls where you can. And most importantly, separate the guest experience by zone. External collaboration sites should enforce stricter access conditions than internal project sites because the threat model is different. Treat this as deterministic. If it's a guest, the access decision is harder by default. Now cross-tenant patterns. Most organizations default to B2B guests everywhere because it's familiar. But in some partner relationships B2B direct connect makes sense to organizations with mature identity, deliberate trust, and a need for persistent collaboration without endless guest churn. The point isn't which feature wins. The point is boundary clarity. If you use direct connect, you document the trust relationship, you constrain it to the collaboration zone and you monitor it like a production dependency. Because that's what it is. Next permissions. This is where entropy explodes so the model has to be boring. Group-based access only. Guests assigned to guest access groups never directly to share point items. Owners assigned through owner groups, not individuals, and guest access should be limited to the minimum roles that make the project function. If a partner needs edit rights, fine. But granted through a controlled group that you can review and rotate, not through a pile of direct permissions that no one can inventory later. Then sensitivity labels because they encode the collaboration contract into the content itself. In this zone labels shouldn't be decorative. They should drive enforcement outcomes. Encryption, restricted sharing, and download controls were required. The label is how you prevent someone copied the file to a different site and now it's ungoverned from becoming the default data flow. When content moves, the label travels and when the label travels your policy intent travels. Auto-labeling can help here, but only inside boundaries you can defend. Models will misclassify. They always do. So use auto-labeling for obvious cases and high-confidence patterns and make humans own exceptions. The worst design is pretending the model is a compliance officer. It isn't. It's a classifier with probabilistic outcomes. Now DLP. In external collaboration, DLP isn't optional friction. It's the runtime guardrail that stops the obvious leakage paths. PII copied into a partner space, regulated documents shared by link, or content exported through connectors that bypass SharePoint's native controls. And the stratification still applies. PII policies behave differently than source code policies. Your finance team's tolerance for false positives is not your engineering team's tolerance designed for that reality. A key pattern here is allow with justification in the external zone, but only when the business has a defensible need. The justification isn't for feelings. It's for evidence. If an exception happens, you want a named human, a reason, and a timestamp, you can query later. Otherwise, you don't have governance. You have vibes. Now, continuous audit. External collaboration is where you need near-real time detection, not end-of-quarter review. Stream the right signals. Guest invitations. Guest sign-ins. Link creation. Sharing events. Download spikes. Permission changes and DLP overrides. Then correlate them with identities which user, which guest, which service principal, which site, which label. If you can't join those dots, you can't investigate quickly and you will lose the narrative when something goes wrong. And watch for the specific anomalies that matter in cross-tenant work. A guest suddenly accessing a new site outside their normal zone, a wave of anonymous links appearing where they should be forbidden, or a service principal reading across multiple external sites in a short window. Those are not interesting logs. They're breach precursors. So the architecture closes the loop. Entra controls the identities. Conditional access controls the sessions. Group-based access controls the permissions. Sensitivity labels control the content behavior. DLP controls the egress and Sentinel controls the story you'll need when leadership asks what happened. Agents in the enterprise where autonomy fits, where it must be caged. Now the part everyone wants to talk about usually in the least useful way, agents. Most organizations treat agents like the next UI. A chat box bolted onto a site. Asked anything about this library. That's fine, but it's not the enterprise problem. The enterprise problem is that agents turn SharePoint from a workflow surface into a decision surface and decisions create liability when nobody can explain why they happened. So the framing is simple. Agents are interfaces first. They are not pipelines. They sit at the edge, helping humans find, summarize, and initiate. They do not replace deterministic enforcement. They do not own compliance outcomes. And they absolutely do not get to freestyle changes across systems because the prompt sounded confident. Where agents fit is the reasoning layer when the output is advisory or bounded. Knowledge retrieval on a site. Guided actions that map to approved workflows. Triage. Which policy applies? Who owns this? Where is the latest approved version? What template should we use? Which intake form is correct? Those tasks reduce cognitive load without changing system state. Everything clicked for enterprises when they realize the pattern. The agent can decide what to ask, but it must not decide what to do. If it's going to do something, it does it through a cage. A controlled tool set with explicit permissions, explicit inputs, explicit logging, and an approval gate when the action crosses a boundary. That cage can be technical. A set of graph scopes limited to one-side collection read only by default, write actions routed through an orchestration service that validates policy and state before it commits. It can be organizational. Agents require sponsors have life cycles and get deleted when the sponsor disappears. But the cage must exist because autonomy without containment isn't innovation. It's conditional chaos. The other mistake is treating agents as smart users. They're not. In architectural terms, an agent is a non-human identity with tool access and a probabilistic reasoning engine. That means it inherits the two worst properties of enterprise systems. It can act quickly and it can be wrong quickly. So the enterprise model treats agents as identities, registered, permissioned, monitored, and reviewed. The same rules apply as with service principles and managed identities. Leased privilege, group-based access and zero standing privilege. If an agent needs to perform privileged actions, it does it under just-in-time elevation and logs every action with correlation IDs you can join in Sentinel. Now the risk model because this is where leader stops smiling. There's a failure triangle that always shows up with agents. Oversharing, untrusted input, and external actions. Oversharing means the underlying SharePoint permission model is messy and the agent faithfully surfaces what it can see. That's not an AI failure. That's your access model showing up at machine speed. Untrusted input means the agent reads content it didn't control, uploaded files, emails, partner documents, pages edited by anyone. If you don't assume malicious or misleading content will enter the system, you will eventually learn that assumption was wrong. External actions means the agent can call tools, send messages, change permissions, create links, move files, trigger flows. The moment an agent can act outside its own boundary, prompt injection stops being a theory and becomes an incident type. So the enterprise pattern is to break that triangle. You reduce oversharing with the group model labels and DLP you already built. You treat untrusted input as normal and sanitize what the agent can use as instructions. And you tightly constrain external actions, read before write and write only through policy enforcement services. Now, a practical architecture view. Agents become entry points into your existing automation stack. A site agent can answer questions, then offer approved actions that map two quick steps or orchestrated workflows. A knowledge agent can propose metadata fixes but humans confirm. An admin agent can serve as drift signals but enforcement runs through deterministic controls and log remediation that keeps your system stable. Humans get speed but governance stays real. Because the truth is agents don't reduce governance needs. They amplify them. The blueprint that survives growth. Scalable SharePoint automation isn't more flows or more AI. It's enforced intent.
