The six habits every FinOps team shares. The foundation lays out six principles, and they make the most sense when you read them as habits rather than rules. The first is that teams have to collaborate, which means finance, engineering and the business all look at the same numbers instead of arguing across a wall. The second habit is that business value drives the decisions. You aren't chasing the lowest possible number, because a service that is cheap and breaks every week isn't cheap at all. The third is that everyone owns their own usage, so the person who built a resource is the person who watches what it costs. The fourth habit is about the data itself. Cost information has to be timely, accurate and available to the people who need it. A report that shows up three weeks late can't change a decision that's already been made. The fifth is that a small central team enables the whole practice, sharing best practices and holding the standards. The real work stays close to the teams doing it. The sixth habit is the one people forget, and it's about using the flexibility you're already paying for. The cloud lets you shrink when demand drops and grow when demand spikes. So the goal is to match what you pay to what you actually use. That blend of a central core and local ownership is the norm, not the exception. In the Foundation's twenty twenty six survey, eighty one percent of teams ran either a centralized enablement model or a hub and spoke model. That's a formal way of describing a small core group that coaches everyone else. That doesn't mean the central team does the work itself. It means the central team draws the map and hands out the compasses, because one hundred people watching their own costs will always beat five people watching everyone else's. The ownership habit sounds abstract until you watch at work. A team that tags its resources and checks its own monthly number starts choosing smaller machines on its own. Nobody sends a memo because the cost finally belongs to a person instead of a spreadsheet. The collaboration habit works the same way when finance and engineering share one dashboard, the argument about the bill becomes a shared question about which services are earning their keep. The six habits only work together, and skipping one usually shows up as a weakness somewhere else. If the data is slow, ownership fades. If ownership is missing, the central team turns into a referee instead of a coach. How FinOps runs in three repeating phases. The practice runs on a loop with three phases, and you cycle through them quickly rather than treating any of them as a one time project. The first phase is in form and its job is visibility. You gather the cost and usage data, then split it up so every team can see its own share of the bill. The second phase is optimize, where you look for ways to get more from what you already spend. There are two flavors of savings here. Usage optimization means using fewer resources to reach the same result, such as shutting down a machine that nobody needs. Rate optimization means paying a better price for the resources you really have to keep. The third phase is operate, and this is where talk turns into action. You make the changes, measure what happened, and then loop right back to inform small, frequent steps. Beat one giant cleanup because the cloud changes faster than any annual project can keep up with. New teams usually start at the crawl stage where they react to problems after they show up. As the habit takes hold, they move into a walk stage and then a run stage. Their cost becomes part of how they design things from the beginning, rather than something they chase afterward. A whole cast of people makes this work. Executives set the priorities. Engineers own the usage they create. Which brings us to the mistakes that sink almost every practice. The mistakes that sink a FinOps practice. The first mistake is treating FinOps as a pure, cost cutting exercise. When savings are the only scoreboard, teams learn to hide their spending instead of talking about it, and the numbers get worse rather than better. The second is running the practice without ownership or tags. If nobody can say which project caused a charge. Every conversation about cost turns into a guessing game and the fix never sticks for long. The third mistake is buying a tool and calling the job done. Software can show you the numbers, but it can't repair a conversation that nobody wants to have. The fourth is keeping all the work inside one central team. If engineers never see their own costs, they never build the instinct to design with cost in mind, and the central team becomes a bottleneck instead of an enabler. The fifth mistake is chasing the last few percent of savings while ignoring the big calls. The foundation's twenty twenty six report captured this well, quoting a practitioner who said the big rocks of waste are gone. What remains, in their words, is a high volume of smaller opportunities that take far more effort to capture. The sixth is trying to measure value without agreeing on what value means. Teams end up arguing about whether a tool is worth the money when they never define the outcome they were buying in the first place. Mistakes like these rarely announce themselves.
