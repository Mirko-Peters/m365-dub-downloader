If you're leading this kind of environment, your first reaction might be to worry about launching another giant transformation program. You don't need a six month project that produces more documents than actual change. You need to start smaller. Start exactly where governance failure is already expensive and visible to the business. Usually, this means picking one high value path that the organization depends on, but currently doesn't trust enough. Maybe that path is how you provision teams in SharePoint workspaces or perhaps it's a co-pilot rollout in a specific delivery function. It could even be a power platform pattern that is growing faster than your current controls can handle. The point is not to fix every single problem at once. You want to redesign one governed path from end to end so the organization can see what good actually looks like in reality. That is step one. Pick a path where the value is high and the pain of weak governance is already causing delays or hesitation. Step two is to baseline the two outcomes that actually matter. Delivery, velocity and AI time to value. You need to know exactly how long it takes to get a governed tool into the hands of a user. Don't guess at these numbers, measure them. If you don't have a before state, your governance work will disappear into abstraction and people will go back to arguing based on opinion. Step three is about ownership and you have to name it clearly. You need to know who owns the path, who owns the data and who owns the business outcome. This sounds basic but it removes a massive amount of organizational drag. Unknown systems create endless conversational traffic where every decision becomes a hunt for authority. But ownership turns that ambiguity into operating flow. Step four is to automate your top controls first. Don't try to automate everything. Focus on the ones that remove the most recurring negotiation. This includes things like labels at creation, DLP where the risk is obvious and provisioning template. You are looking for controls that shift the burden from human memory to system design. This is where you get your first dividend because your teams can suddenly govern more without the manual stress. Step five is to make the safe path visibly faster than the alternative. This matters more than any policy language you could write. If the governed route feels slower or more confusing than the risky one, people will find ways to work around it. Use pre-approved environments and clear request categories to make the path legible. When the safe way is the easy way, you don't need a culture campaign to change behavior. Finally, there is step six. Review your exceptions every single month. Don't look at them as proof that users are being difficult. Look at them as proof that your design still has gaps. Every recurring exception is a piece of telemetry telling you that a standard pattern doesn't fit or a policy is colliding with reality.
