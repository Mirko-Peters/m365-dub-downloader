In enterprise AI, teams still treat logs like a premium feature. Nice later, not required now. That's how you end up with agents that can act but can't be explained. And an agent that can't be explained is an agent you can't defend. Your observability becomes the third pillar of control alongside grounding and identity. It is what makes intent enforceable over time. Start with the minimum. Decision logs. Not chat transcripts. Decision logs. A transcript tells you what the user said. It does not tell you why the agent chose an intent, why it called a tool, why it escalated or why it refused. You need a record of the agent's decision points. Classification result. Confidence. Retrieved sources. Tool calls attempted. Tool results. Verification outcomes. Proofers requested approvals received and the final disposition. That means every meaningful step produces a log event you can query. Not an export you have to dig up later. A queryable record. And yes, the platform gives you pieces, power platform admin views, agent registries, purview DSPM for AI and defender signals. But those surfaces only help if you treat observability as a design requirement, not a retrospective cleanup task. Audit logging is the first non-negotiable. If the agent can access enterprise data or execute actions, its activity must land in audit logs with a stable identity anchor. This is where agent ID stops being theoretical. It becomes the principle you can search for when you need to know what happened. The next surface is monitoring. Not just is it up, but is it behaving? So the metrics that matter are not messages sent. Their operational outcomes, containment rate, how often the interaction ends without a human, escalation rate, how often it puns to a human, and why. Days one and two, pick the use case, lock the KPI targets, and draw the escalation boundary in ink. You're not selecting AI opportunities. You're selecting one operational flow, where deflection and cycle time can move inside 30 days. IT Ticket triage wins because it's measurable, high volume, and politically survivable. Then you set targets that can't be gamed. 20% to 40% deflection for the chosen intake channel, 15% to 30% SLA reduction for the subset of tickets the agent touches and 10% to 25% fewer escalations caused by mis-routing. You also define the hard line, what the agent must never do. Anything involving access grants, privilege, changes, or policy exceptions escalates by design. No debates later. The agent doesn't try. It routes. Days three through five, design the intents and topics, then implement the fail-safe behaviors. This is the week where topics brawl either starts or gets prevented. You create 10 to 15 intents, not departments, not everything users might ask, stable intents that map to a containment boundary. For each intent, you write three things. Success criteria, allowed actions, and escalation triggers. If you can't express those in one screen of text, the intent is too broad, then you implement fallback. One fallback. Fallback is not be helpful. Fallback is controlled uncertainty. Ask one clarifying question, then escalate. If your fallback tries to answer anyway, you're building confident wrongness into the core. And you set kill criteria now, not after the pilot embarrasses you, low usage, high confusion, high escalation rate, low containment. If a topic fails, it gets merged or retired. Dead topics aren't harmless. They create ambiguous rooting forever. Days six through eight, implement orchestration and enrichment, connect ITSM with deterministic automation. This is where you build the actual loop, classify, enrich, retrieve, propose, confirm, execute, verify, handoff. The orchestration lives in co-pilot studio because you need a control plane, not just a chat interface. The enrichment is minimal, identity, device state, if relevant, recent incidents, and service status. Don't horde context. Context hoarding becomes privacy risk and retrieval confusion. Then you connect your ITSM system with power automate, not because it's pretty, but because it's deterministic. Ticket creation, assignment, notifications, and logging belong in a workflow engine, not in an LLM's improvisation. At this stage, tool discipline matters more than cleverness. Start read only where possible. Check service health, list known incidents, pull user ticket history. If you must write, keep it reversible, create a ticket, add a note, post an update, anything privileged waits. Days nine and ten, pilot users create an evaluation set and baseline containment and accuracy. Pick a small pilot group that represents real usage, not enthusiasts. You want normal people with normal impatience, give them one clear instruction, use this for these issues and if it escalates, that's expected. Now build the evaluation set, this is not test cases. It's a fixed list of the top questions and intends the agent must handle. VPN access, password resets, device compliance questions, known outages, ticket status, and basic how-to procedures. You run the same set every week to see drift and you measure three numbers immediately, containment rate, escalation reasons, and grounded accuracy for anything that produced an answer with a source. If you don't have sources yet, you still measure refusal correctness, did it escalate when it should? Now the gate to proceed. You do not advance to days 11 through 20 because the team feels good. You advance because the system meets three conditions. Measureable lift against the baseline, no access violations, and logging turned on with traceable outcomes. Lift means the pilot produced a real reduction in human touches for the selected intents, even if it's small. No access violations means the agent didn't retrieve restricted content or execute actions outside boundary. Logging means you can explain every escalation and every tool call. If you can't pass that gate in 10 days, the program doesn't need more features. It needs tighter scope because the first 10 days are about proving the system boundary holds. Once it does, you're allowed to solve the next problem, trust at scale. And trust only comes from grounding plus tool discipline, enforced relentlessly. Days 11 to 20, ground stabilize and reduce entropy. Days 11 through 20 are where most programs either become a system or become a clever demo that everyone quietly stops using. Week 2 proved you can root and contain within a boundary. Now you have to make the answers defensible, the tools predictable, and the failure modes measurable. This is the phase where entropy shows up as small exceptions. And small exceptions are how agent programs die. Days 11 through 13, build the Azure AI search index and make the chunking strategy non-negotiable. You're not connecting SharePoint, you're building an operational knowledge index, so you start by choosing what qualifies as truth. Approved runbooks, SOPs, known issue articles and policies with owners, if it's a draft it doesn't go in. If it's ownerless it doesn't go in. If it changes without change control it doesn't go in. Then chunking, don't overthink it. The unit of retrieval is the decision unit. One procedure, one exception clause, one policy section. If your chunk contains multiple outcomes, you've created ambiguity on purpose. Metadata follows, service, audience, region, risk tier and last reviewed date. Make metadata mandatory in the content pipeline because optional metadata is metadata that won't exist where you need it. Finally, design refresh cadence like you mean it. Policies refresh on publish, known issues refresh frequently, runbooks refresh on change, and you keep last indexed. Visible in telemetry, because stale answers and hallucinations look identical to the user. Days 14 through 16 integrate MCP tools in read-only mode and enforce no source, no answer. This is where programs get tempted to move fast by making the agent do things. Don't, not yet. Start with MCP tools that only read check service health, list known incidents, look up a user's open tickets, retrieve a service catalog entry, pull a ticket template. This makes the agent more accurate without letting it change state. And it gives you tool telemetry without operational risk. Then you enforce the grounding rule with teeth. If the response is non-trivial and there's no retrieved source it refuses and escalates. Not a friendly guess, not based on my knowledge. A controlled refusal with an escalation path. This is also where you explicitly separate knowledge from action. The agent can answer from grounded content. The agent can propose an action, but execution only happens through a tool call and only after you hit a decision boundary. That separation is what stops healthfulness from becoming unauthorized change. Days 17 and 18 add approvals for write actions and introduce adaptive cards. Now you're allowed to add write tools but only inside a governed pattern. Propose, confirm, approve, execute, log, pick one or two write actions that are low risk and reversible. Ticket creation is the obvious one. Updating a ticket with context is another. Anything involving identity, access, finance or deletion stays out. Adaptive cards and teams become the approval surface because they force clarity. The approver sees who requested, what will change, why, and which policy applies. They click approve, deny or request more info, no paragraph debates, no hidden side channels. And the card isn't just UX, it's control. The approval decision triggers a deterministic workflow, records the payload, stamps who approved, and then executes through the approved two path. If you can't show the approval record, you didn't approve anything, you just delayed it. Days 19 and 20. Red team prompts, injection tests and tighten fallbacks. This is where you stop pretending users behave nicely. You test prompt injection, you test instructions that try to override policy, you test ignore previous instructions patterns, you test misleading inputs designed to force retrieval of restricted content, you test social engineering prompts that try to get the agent to generate an action it should never take. And you don't treat failures as model problems, you treat them as boundary problems. If the agent retrieved the wrong content, fixed chunking and metadata, if it answered without sources, tighten the response policy. If it tried to call a write tool when it shouldn't, tighten orchestration. If it got stuck in two retry loops, add hard stop conditions and escalation triggers. Now the gate to proceed in today's 21 through 30 is strict because this is where scale becomes liability. You proceed only when three conditions are true. Grounded accuracy in your evaluator set is above 85%, rooting stability holds under real usage, and escalation rates are trending down for the right reasons. Not because the agent refuses everything, because it retrieves correctly, sites correctly and escalates only at real boundaries. Once you pass that gate, you're ready for the final phase, scale without panic. Days 21 through 30 are where leadership usually ruins the win. Week one and two produced a working system boundary, week three proved grounding and approvals. Now leadership sees momentum and asks for more agents across more domains, more connectors, more autonomy, more channels. That request is how liability gets invited into production. So this final phase is not build more. It's make the first one survivable, then expand with discipline. Days 21 through 23. Align the agent with enterprise identity and least privilege. This is where entry agent ID stops being conceptual and becomes a dependency. The agent needs a stable identity anchor for audit, conditional access and incident response. That identity also forces the uncomfortable question. What permissions does this agent actually need? And what permissions did it accidentally inherit because someone clicked allow all? So you do a least privilege review as a gate, not as a cleanup task. You separate read capability from write capability. You keep write actions behind approvals. And if the agent touches sensitive systems, you apply conditional access constraints that match reality, restrict where the identity can be used, restrict session behavior and block high-risk sign-in conditions. This is where you prevent the classic failure. A helpful agent becomes a privileged actor without anyone explicitly deciding it should. Days 24 through 26. Production ALM and controlled publishing. If the agent is business critical, it doesn't ship like a maker project. You enforce dev, test and proc separation so you can change behavior without gambling in production. You publish through a controlled path, so builders can't accidentally make something global. You lock down who can update the production agent and you keep rollback real. If containment drops or escalation spike, you revert and investigate. No hero debugging, no late night prompt edits in prod. And you formalize the, you can build, but you can't publish posture into a workflow. Publishing requires a named owner, named sponsor, tool-allow-list confirmation, grounding policy confirmation, and audit logging verification. Not to slow teams down, to keep the system deterministic as its scales. Days 27 and 28, roll out to a target group with adoption tied to workflows, not training theatre. This is where most orgs waste time. They run a co-pilot training, teach people how to prompt, and then wonder why adoption stalls. People don't adopt prompts, they adopt outcomes. So you roll out by workflow, you put the agent in the channel where the work already starts. You give users three things, what issues it handles, what it will escalate, and what evidence it will show when it answers, that's it. Then you measure real usage, task completion without handoff, containment rate and escalation reasons. If adoption is low, you don't fix it with more training. You fix it by tightening, rooting, shortening the answer format and adding the next action buttons that remove friction. Days 29 and 30, executive readout in the next 90-day plan.
