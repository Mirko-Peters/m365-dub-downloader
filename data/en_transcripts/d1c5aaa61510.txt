The four dimensions of relational design. Relational intelligence is built on four specific dimensions and if you miss even one of them, the entire system will eventually fail at scale. These real world connections determine how you model the data so you must choose your logic based on how the business actually functions. Then come invariants. These are the rules that must never be broken. An order cannot exist without a customer and an invoice cannot exist without an order. These are not just suggestions or best practices they are structural requirements. Relational design enforces these rules at the data layer so that no app, flow or form can ever violate them. Finally, there is integration. This is how data flows in and out without creating new silos. You won't have to reconcile data manually because the structure itself enforces consistency. If you skip any of these dimensions, failure is coming for you. If a user has read access to every case in the system because they asked for it once, that isn't security, that isn't accommodation. If their job actually requires seeing every case to perform a task, that is a function. You have to distinguish between these two ruthlessly. Next, identify your security gaps. Look for places where people are seeing data that should be private. If a support case contains salary information that is visible to staff outside of HR, you have a gap. If accounts belonging to specific regions are visible to everyone in the company, you have another gap. Document these gaps before you try to fix them. Map the natural boundaries that already exist in your organization. Think about business units, departments and geographic regions. In a healthcare organization, regional officers manage their own cases which creates a natural data boundary. Your data structure should respect those boundaries. When you design the role structure, focus on actual job functions. Don't just copy a standard template because it's easy. A salesperson needs to read accounts in their territory, along with contacts and opportunities. A manager might need those same permissions plus the ability to create new accounts. A field rep might only need to see their own assigned cases. Every role should be a direct reflection of a function. Now build the relationships. A role grants a permission and that permission defines an action like "create" or "read" on a table. The scope determines who they can see that data for. A salesperson has a read permission on opportunities but the scope is territorial. The logic follows a clear path. User to role, role to permission and permission to table. This exercise forces you to do a reality check. Try designing security for a new business process from scratch. If support tickets need to be segregated by region, you'll need a region field on the case and a role with regional scope. Diagram this out before you build anything. The diagram will show you exactly what you need before you waste time in the tool. The checkpoint here is Forensic. If an auditor asked you to prove why a specific person can see a specific piece of data, could you do it? You should be able to draw a straight line from their job function through a role definition to their permissions. If that explanation is messy or confusing, your design isn't ready for production. Security becomes much easier once it is structural. When a new user joins the team, you assign them a role and they automatically see the cases for their unit. You aren't setting individual permissions on records because the structure is doing the work for you. Changes are safer in this model. If a credit limit rule moves to a calculated field and you need to hide it, you just apply a security profile. The model supports the change because it was built into the foundation not bolted on as an afterthought. The patterns that scale, pattern one, the master data model. Large organizations eventually realize they all need the same thing. Canonical data entities. These are core business concepts to find once and use everywhere. Take the concept of a customer. In a massive company, you might find customer defined five different ways because sales has their version while finance and operations build their own. Each team created a definition that matched their specific needs but the result is five separate tables doing the same work. This leads to data conflicts and reconciliation nightmares where updates made in one system never reach the others. The master data model flips this logic. Customer is defined once for the entire organization rather than for a single department. It includes every attribute and relationship the business cares about in one single source of truth. This same rule applies to your products, orders, and contracts. These aren't just tables in a database. They are the way your organization actually thinks. When you build a new app, you don't redesign the customer entity from scratch. You simply extend the master model or add a single field if it's missing. The real benefit here is velocity. If a new sales tool needs customer info, you don't spend three weeks designing a schema and building relationships. You spend one week wiring it into the existing foundation. Building becomes faster and data quality improves automatically because every app is looking at the exact same information. But there is a risk. If your master model is wrong, then every single thing you build on top of it will be wrong too. This approach requires serious discipline and intentional design. You can't rush it. The upfront investment is much higher but the payoff down the road compounds. I saw this happen with a mid-market company that had 50 power apps running at once. Every single app had its own customer table with different fields and different rules. When they finally move to a master data model, they merge 12 different versions of customer into one entity. Development time for new apps dropped from six weeks down to two weeks. Data quality jumped 40%. Not because the apps were better but because the redundancy finally collapsed. Don't try to do everything at once. Start with your most critical concept, like customer or product and get the business to agree on a design. Deploy that first, then move to the next one. Pattern 2, the transactional outbox. Data integration usually fails quietly. It doesn't scream for help, it just stops working. You create an order record in Dataverse and expect an event to fire so your warehouse stays updated. But the event gets lost in transit. The warehouse never sees the order. Days go by before anyone notices the mismatch and by then your other systems have already made decisions based on bad data. The transactional outbox pattern exists to stop the silent failure. The logic is straightforward. Every time a critical record changes in Dataverse, the system writes a new entry into a dedicated outbox table. This write happens inside the exact same transaction as the data change itself. Either both of them succeed or both of them fail. There is no middle ground where your data updates but the event record vanishes. Later a downstream service checks that outbox table at regular intervals. It picks up each entry and processes it. Once the work is done, the service marks that entry is complete. If the processing fails for any reason, the entry stays in a pending state and the service simply tries again later. Eventually, every single integration is guaranteed to fire. The pattern is simple but the impact on your architecture is massive. Your integration has become reliable without you having to build custom orchestration logic for every single move. You don't need to bake complex retry loops into every individual flow because the structure of the pattern handles the heavy lifting for you. I saw this play out with a retail company that kept their order system in Dataverse. When a customer placed an order that data had to reach the fulfillment center, the accounting software, and the shipping provider all at once. Without an outbox, things were constantly falling through the cracks. They would have an order in Dataverse that never made it to fulfillment or a package would ship but the customer was never built. It was pure chaos. Everything changed when they implemented the transactional outbox. Now every order change writes to that outbox table in one clean transaction. Downstream services read the table, process the orders, and mark them finished. The mysterious gaps in their data disappeared overnight. To build this, you only need one extra table called outbox. You'll need columns for the record ID, the record type, the operation, a timestamp, and a processed flag. When a record changes, you insert an entry, then you just set up a flow or an azure function to read those unprocessed entries and handle the rest. Pattern 3. The saga pattern for distributed transactions. Modern systems don't live in a vacuum. An order isn't just a row in one database. It spans across Dataverse for your CRM, an ERP for your inventory and a separate billing system. Because these systems are distributed, a traditional database transaction won't work across those boundaries. You can't just hit save and expect three different clouds to stay in sync. The saga pattern is how you manage this complexity. It breaks a large process into smaller individual steps that target different systems. Crucially, every step has a matching, compensating action. If you get to step three and it fails, the system automatically runs the compensations for steps one and two to undo what they did. Think about a customer placing an order. In step one, you reserve the inventory in your ERP. If that works, step two creates an invoice in the billing system. If that also works, step three creates a fulfillment record in the warehouse. But if that warehouse step fails, the compensation kicks in. It cancels the invoice and releases the inventory reservation. The customer sees a clean result because the order either succeeded everywhere or it failed everywhere. This pattern does require high observability. Every step has to log exactly what happened, so the compensation logic knows what to undo. You also need a correlation ID for each saga instance, so you can trace the path of an order as it moves across your different systems. Your compensation steps must also be idempotent, meaning if you run them twice by mistake, the result stays the same. In a dataverse environment, you usually implement sagas using a workflow orchestrator. You might have an Azure function watching a sagas step table in dataverse. It reads the pending steps, calls the external systems and logs the results. If any system sends back a failure, the orchestrator triggers the compensation steps immediately. The real benefit here is reliability in a world where systems are scattered. You aren't pretending that transactions work across different platforms. Instead, you are acknowledging those boundaries and designing a system that respects them. Your compensation is explicit, your testing is easier, and your debugging is finally clear. Pattern 4. The normalized reference data strategy. Reference data is the glue that holds your entire system together. Think about status values, country codes, product categories, or currency definitions. These small data points show up in almost every table you build and how you handle them determines whether your system stays consistent or falls apart. The weak approach is to simply copy reference values into every table that needs them. You store a status as a text field in your orders table, then do it again in cases and again in invoices. Eventually different values start appearing in different tables for the exact same concept and the result is total chaos. The better pattern is to define canonical reference tables where each concept, like a status, lives in its own single source of truth. When a column needs a status value, it uses a lookup to that specific table instead of a manual text entry. This means you can change a status name in one single place and it instantly updates across your entire environment. I saw this play out in a real scenario where different departments use different names for the same thing. Sales called a record active, finance called it open, and operations insisted on calling it live. Because they had one data set with three different names, reports couldn't aggregate anything because the values simply didn't match. Implementing a canonical status table forced everyone to align and once active, open, and live all became a single active status. The reports suddenly started working. Building this pattern takes discipline because you have to identify these reference concepts early in the process. You need to create a dedicated table for each one, document the values clearly, and use lookups instead of letting people type into text fields. Once you've built this foundation, you have to enforce it during design reviews to ensure new apps use the canonical data rather than creating their own local versions. These patterns aren't new inventions, but they are proven methods for organizations that need to operate at a massive scale. The master data model speeds up your development, the transactional outbox makes your integrations reliable and the saga pattern manages your distributed complexity. When you combine those with normalized reference data to keep your values consistent, you finally have the infrastructure that lets relational intelligence actually work. Organizational metrics tell you if the cultural shift is actually sticking. Look at your architects. If your architects are staying and taking on more responsibility, it means relational thinking is becoming a reality. You should also check in with your citizen developers. If they tell you that building on a relational foundation makes their jobs easier, then the system is working. For the best results, you should measure your velocity every month to get quick feedback on whether things are improving or sliding backward. Business outcomes change more slowly, so those are better suited for quarterly reviews while organizational health can be measured once a year. Where you share these results is just as important as the data itself. The Executive Steering Committee needs to see the financial impact, while the Architecture Review Board needs to see the technical patterns. Individual teams should see how their work compares to the rest of the company. This kind of open communication creates a natural sense of accountability. When it's time to celebrate, make sure you recognize the teams that are getting it right. Share their success stories and be specific about the hours they saved or the bugs they prevented.
