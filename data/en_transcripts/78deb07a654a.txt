You aren't building custom query logic for every system, and your governance and permissions are baked into the foundation. This allows you to add new data sources without redesigning the entire architecture. This is where Jarvis stops being confined to Microsoft 365 chat experiences and becomes a true operating system that can orchestrate across your entire organization. Graph is the plumbing, but now we need to talk about what actually flows through it. Grounding. Teaching Jarvis your context. Grounding is the mechanism that separates reasoning from hallucination. When co-pilot answers a question without access to your data, it's working from training data that is broad but generic. It knows what a CRM is, in theory, and it understands common sales methodologies or how businesses typically structure pipelines. But it doesn't know your pipeline. It doesn't know what a qualified lead means in your specific office, and it doesn't know your typical deal size. When co-pilot answers without your data, it invents plausible answers that might be correct by accident, but they are often confidently wrong. This is hallucination. It doesn't happen because the model is broken, but because it's reasoning without any anchor points. Grounding means anchoring reasoning in your actual data. When Jarvis needs to make a decision, it first retrieves relevant information from your systems and then reasons over that specific information instead of generic training data. This changes everything about what the system can do. An ungrounded system might suggest a standard pipeline review process for you to consider, but a grounded system looks at the facts. It sees that your Q3 pipeline has 32 opportunities with 18 in discovery and 8 stalled in negotiation – it tells you why that's unusual based on your historical patterns and points out exactly what you should investigate first. The difference is the data. The grounded system retrieved information about your actual pipeline, compared it against your history, and provided intelligence specific to your situation. Building grounded reasoning requires deliberate data architecture. First, your data needs to be discoverable. Co-pilot can't use information it can't find. If your project documentation is scattered across 17 different sharepoint sites with inconsistent naming, Jarvis will struggle to find what it needs. If your customer data is split between three different systems with no connection between them, the system will fail to see the full picture. You need structure with categories that make sense to both humans and machines. Predictable naming conventions and metadata that describes what the data represents are essential. While this sounds tedious, it compounds into a competitive advantage because it makes your data usable by any AI system you build. Second, your data needs to be current. Stale data is worse than no data because the system operates with false confidence. Reasoning over outdated information produces decisions that seem reasonable but are built on facts that aren't true anymore. Your customer contact list from six months ago or your risk assessments from before new regulations changed everything will lead to bad outcomes. Stale data requires governance. You have to decide what information needs real-time updates and what can be refreshed on a schedule. You must determine the acceptable lateness for information before Jarvis should flag uncertainty rather than treating it as a fact. Third, sensitive data needs clear boundaries. What everything should be accessible to every grounding query and financial details might be off limits for certain decisions. Personal information needs protection and competitive information needs classification so you can decide what Jarvis can see and what's protected from its reasoning. This is where graph connectors matter for external systems. Your CRM and your ticketing system live outside Microsoft 365 and they contain critical context for decision making. These systems aren't automatically connected to graph so you have to bridge that gap. Some connectors expose your external data through the same graph interface that already accesses Microsoft 365 data. Now when Jarvis reasons about whether a customer issue is critical it can access historical tickets. When it decides whether to escalate a prospect it can see the full customer history and search your knowledge base for a solution. The compounding effect is profound. One well-organized data source makes Jarvis slightly smarter but five connected sources make the system intelligently aware of context most humans would miss. When you reach 20 connected sources you've created a reasoning engine that has more context about your business than any individual on your team. But grounding isn't just about having data it's about being able to use that data without drowning in irrelevant information. When Jarvis needs context about a specific customer it shouldn't retrieve the entire customer database but only the information relevant to the current decision. This is where structured search and semantic indexing matter. Your data needs to be indexed in ways that support discovery through semantic understanding rather than just keywords. Looking out why a customer was unhappy last quarter shouldn't require you to manually search through complaint emails. The system should find it because it understands the relationships between the data. This is where Jarvis stops being superficially intelligent and becomes contextually aware. The proactive layer Jarvis doesn't wait. Everything we have built up to this point follows a very specific predictable pattern. You ask a question and the system gives you an answer. You give co-pilot a prompt. The system thinks about it. It gives you a response and then you decide what to do next. You take action but the system has no idea what happened after that interaction ended. This is what we call reactive architecture. It is the standard way almost every AI system works today. But it is also the exact thing that stops Jarvis from becoming what it needs to be. Proactive architecture flips that model on its head. Instead of waiting the system watches. It looks for patterns and starts moving before you even think to ask. You are still the one in control but the system is now the one finding opportunities instead of just responding to things you already noticed. The difference might sound small but in practice it changes everything. Most companies use co-pilot in a purely reactive way. You finish a meeting with a client, open up co-pilot and ask it to write a follow-up email. It writes the draft, you send it and the job is done. The problem is that the system did absolutely nothing until you told it to. Jarvis works proactively. When that meeting ends the system sees the transcript show up in the Microsoft Graph. It recognizes that this person is a new prospect in your sales pipeline. It checks your skill, MD file and sees that new leads always need a follow-up within 24 hours. It knows you are usually too busy to remember these small details. It doesn't wait for your command. It drafts the email, flags it for your review and sets a reminder so you don't miss the window. You never had to ask because the system saw the pattern and took the first step. The technology that makes this work is different from the prompts or grounding we have talked about so far. This isn't about being smarter, it is about triggers and timing. Graph webhooks are the first part of this trigger system. Whenever something changes in your Microsoft 365 environment like a new email arriving or a meeting being scheduled, Graph sends a notification to Jarvis. This happens in real time, not minutes later or only when you log in. This creates a completely different way for the system to run. Instead of sitting idle, Jarvis can react to events the moment they happen. If a high priority email from a major customer hits your inbox, the webhook fires and Jarvis sees it immediately. It checks your rules to see if this needs special handling. If it does, the system drafts a reply and creates a task for you before you even have a chance to open your email client. That is what real proactivity looks like. The system saw something important and it moved first. The second part of this is the heartbeat pattern. You can't rely on webhooks for everything because you don't get a notification when something fails to happen. There is no alert for a task that is simply sitting there overdue or a project that hasn't been updated in two weeks. The heartbeat solves this by running jobs on a set schedule. Every hour or every day, Jarvis wakes up and scans your entire workspace. It runs flows that check if anything is moving off track. It looks for stalled projects, missed decisions or stakeholders who haven't heard from you in a while. The heartbeat is how Jarvis stays aware of your world even when nothing is actively triggering an alert. The result is a system that doesn't force you to manage every single detail yourself. You can stay focused on deep work while the system handles the situational awareness. It finds the gaps and starts the work wherever your rules allow it to act. Of course this requires very strict governance. Proactive systems can get noisy very fast. If every tiny change causes Jarvis to jump into action, your inbox will be buried under system suggestions that you eventually just start to ignore. To prevent this, the system needs to be smart about which patterns actually matter. Some emails are just noise and some stalled projects are actually paused on purpose. Jarvis has to learn what is actually worth your limited time and attention. That learning happens through a constant feedback loop. As you interact with the system, it starts to understand your preferences. If you ignore certain suggestions, the system notices and stops making them. If you act on others immediately, it learns to make those a priority. This is the point where proactivity turns into true intelligence. It isn't because the underlying AI got smarter. It is because Jarvis stopped just answering your questions and started predicting what you need before you even realized you needed. Proactivity only works when memory and reasoning are tied together. Multi-agent orchestration. Specialized systems working together. A single Jarvis is a powerful tool, but any single agent is going to have its limits. If you have only one agent, that agent has to know everything about every part of your business. It needs to be an expert in scheduling, compliance, finance and project management all at the same time. When you try to cram into one brain, the more of a generalist it becomes. We all know that generalists usually make average decisions when they are faced with highly specialized problems. Real companies don't work like that. They are built on specialized knowledge where different teams own different areas. Your finance team doesn't hire people and your HR team doesn't review technical architecture. Every department has its own logic and its own set of rules. Jarvis needs to be built to reflect that same reality. This is why we use multi-agent orchestration. Instead of one giant agent trying to do it all, you build a network of specialists. Each agent owns one specific domain and understands the tiny nuances and rules that matter in that space. Then you simply connect them. A scheduling agent knows exactly how to handle the logistics of a calendar. It understands different time zones and your specific travel habits. It knows which meetings need a conference room and how to fix a double booking, but that agent shouldn't be deciding if a meeting is actually worth having. That is a business judgment, not a logistics problem. A compliance agent knows all of your legal obligations. It knows which data is allowed to leave the company and who needs to sign off on a specific deal. It knows when an audit is required, but it shouldn't be making business moves on its own. Compliance is a guardrail, not the driver. A communication agent knows your stakeholders and how they like to be reached. It can adjust its tone for different audiences and handle the actual drafting of messages. It knows the how, but it doesn't decide the what or the when. This kind of specialization gives you a massive advantage because each agent stays fast and focused. Scheduling agent that only thinks about calendars will always be better than a generalist trying to check compliance rules at the same time. The whole architecture turns into a clear hierarchy. At the very top you have an orchestration agent. This agent understands your high level goals and takes in your requests. It doesn't do the work itself. Instead it acts like a manager and roots the job to the right specialists. If you tell the system you want to reschedule a team meeting and update everyone, the orchestrator sees that this needs both scheduling and communication. It goes to the scheduling agent first to find a new time. Once it has those options it passes them to the communication agent to draft the messages. Finally the orchestrator shows you the whole plan for a quick approval. With one click the work of two different agents is finished and coordinated. This is what we call delegation in series. One agent finishes its part and hands the result to the next one in line. But sometimes work needs to happen in parallel. If you are doing a project review you might need three specialists to work at the exact same time. The scheduling agent finds a time for the leads. The project agent pulls the data and the communication agent writes the invites. They all work at once and send their results back to the orchestrator to be combined. Parallel work is much faster but it is also more complicated to manage. These agents have to coordinate so they don't get in each other's way. They need to share the same context so their final outputs don't clash. The core design principle here is very simple. You should specialize whenever you can and only coordinate when you must. Every agent becomes a master of its own world. But none of them are working in a vacuum. They all live inside the JavaScript architecture. They share the same memory, follow the same rules, and report back through the same system. The end result is an operating system that is far more capable than any single AI could ever be. True intelligence in a complex business isn't about one smart engine. It is about having a team of specialists who know exactly when to work together and when to stay in their lane.
