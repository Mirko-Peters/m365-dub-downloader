You just off-boarded a senior engineer and everything seemed fine until Monday morning, but that's when you realize every critical automation in your Microsoft 365 environment is dead. The HR onboarding flow has stalled and the nightly security report was never sent, which leaves your team scrambling to find out why. This is the identity rot caused by tethering enterprise workflows to personal credentials, and it means we have built our most important systems on the shadow of individual users. It is a model built on quicksand. Transitioning to service principles is no longer a nice to have cleanup project because it has become a mandate for survival as we head toward 2026. Today, we stop treating identity as a human attribute and we start treating it as infrastructure. If you want an environment that scales without breaking every time someone resigns, you need to master the service principle, but to fix the system, we first have to understand why it is currently breaking. The shadow account trap, we fall into the user account fallacy because it feels convenient when you are building a power automate flow and need to move files from SharePoint to a legacy database. The system asks for a connection and you click sign in, but you use your own account without realizing you just planted a time bomb in your infrastructure. We default to personal credentials because they already have the licenses and permissions, but we pay for that convenience in resilience. This is where authentication rot begins. Think about how Microsoft 365 manages security today as the entire system is designed for humans and expects MFA prompts or password resets every 90 days. It applies conditional access policies based on where you are and what device you are using, and this creates a massive problem for headless flows. These automated processes cannot answer an authenticate or prompt or navigate a password expired screen. When you tether an automated process to a human identity, you are forcing a machine to act like a person and the moment that person changes their password, the flow dies. The moment that person travels to a new country and triggers a risky sign in block, the flow dies again and the result is a silent strangulation of your headless workloads, but the real disaster happens during offboarding. Recent research shows that one in three enterprises experience a critical system failure during staff turnover because of identity tethering. When an admin leaves, IT disables their account as part of the standard procedure, but if that admin was the identity anchor for 20 different power BI refreshes and five logic apps, those services go dark, the second that account is flipped to disabled. We try to bypass this by creating service accounts, but in most organizations, a service account is just a personal account in disguise. It is usually a licensed user named SVC_automation that has a user name in a password sitting in a spreadsheet or a shared vault. This creates a massive security hole where there is zero accountability, because if that password leaks, you have no way of knowing who used it. You do not have the protection of MFA because MFA breaks the automation, so you exclude the account from your security policies and create a high privilege account with no modern protection. It is the weakest link in your entire tenant. Microsoft has recognized this structural flow and the reality check officially arrived when they announced that as of March 31st, 2026, things are changing. Microsoft retired service principle less authentication, which means the error of just signing in with a user account to run a background process is over. Legacy engines like SharePoint 2013 workflows are being fully removed, and new apps are being blocked from using the older 080 graph API. The message is clear, if your automation depends on a human shadow, it is no longer supported by the platform. You are not just fighting technical debt anymore, you are fighting the platform itself. The old model was about who was running the script, but the new model is about the script having its own identity. We have to move away from the idea that an automation is an extension of a person. It is a standalone entity that needs its own life cycle and its own security boundary. If user accounts are the trap, then service principles are the escape hatch, but they only work if you understand how to manage them as infrastructure. You cannot just create them and forget them because you have to orchestrate them. You have to shift your mindset from managing users to managing runtimes. That shift starts with a fundamental change in how we define what an identity actually is. It is no longer an employee record, it is a deployment artifact, and once you realize that, the way you build in M365 changes forever. Identity as infrastructure. We need to stop thinking of identity as just a set of login credentials. In the modern Microsoft 365 ecosystem, identity is a structural component that functions more like a runtime instance of an application than a person. When you create a service principle, you aren't just making a user that doesn't have a desk, but rather you are creating a non-interactive security principle. It lives in the directory, it has no mailbox, it has no password in the traditional sense, it is a service side representation of your code. The core of this shift is the decoupling principle. In the old world, we asked who was running a process, but in the new world, we asked what resource is authorized to do the work. This is the move from human-centric to resource-centric security. You aren't delegating your power to a script, but instead, you are granting that script its own authority. But here's the problem, not all service principles are built the same. To build a resilient core, you have to follow the hierarchy of truth. At the very top of that hierarchy is the managed identity. If your workload is running inside Azure, managed identities are the gold standard because they represent the ultimate realization of identity as infrastructure. The reason they work so well is that there are no credentials for you to manage. Azure handles the creation, Azure handles the storage, Azure handles the rotation. You never see a client secret and you never touch a certificate, because the identity is literally tied to the life cycle of the resource. If you delete the virtual machine or the logic app, the identity vanishes with it, which means you've achieved zero secret authentication. This eliminates the biggest risk in your environment, which is human error during credential handling. But we know that M365 doesn't always live entirely inside an Azure resource. You have CICD pipelines in GitHub, you have on-premises scripts, you have multi-cloud integrations. This is where federated credentials change the game. We used to rely on long-lived secrets for these external connections. Which meant you'd generate a client secret and paste it into a GitHub secret while hoping nobody ever saw it. That secret was a liability. It sat there for a year or maybe forever, just waiting to be leaked. Federated credentials use OpenID Connect or OIDC to perform a token exchange. Your GitHub action says to enter ID that it has a token from GitHub proving its identity and then enter ID looks at the trust relationship you've configured. It validates the issuer, it validates the subject, and then it hands back a short-lived access token. No secrets are stored in your pipeline and no secrets are stored in your code because you've replaced a permanent password with a temporary trust. This is what it means to treat identity as infrastructure. It becomes a deployment artifact. When you define your automation, the identity is part of the template. So it exists right there in the bicep file or the terraform code. You aren't setting up an account after the fact because the identity is provisioned as part of the system's birth. It's just like a virtual network or storage account. If you need a process to read from a SharePoint list, you don't find a person with access, but instead you define a principle with that specific scope. You treat that identity with the same rigor you treat your production service, you monitor its sign-ins, you audit its permissions, you version its configuration. This mindset shift is what separates a lab environment from an enterprise environment. In a lab you use what's easy, but in an enterprise you use what's durable. Service principles provide that durability. They don't get married and change their names. They don't go on vacation, they don't get fired. They are the stable anchors of your automation strategy. But having the right identity is only step one. The real danger, the thing that actually kills your security, is how much power you give that identity once it exists. Because an infrastructure identity with too much power is just a high speed vulnerability. The permission creep crisis. We've established that identity is infrastructure, but here is the problem. Infrastructure needs boundaries. Without them, your resilient service principle becomes a silent weapon for an attacker. The biggest mistake architects make is falling into the application. Readright.allTrap It is the path of least resistance. You're trying to get a script to work and you're tired of seeing 403 forbidden errors in your logs, so you go into the entraportal to find the API permissions. You check the box for the broadest scope available, and just like that you've created a monster. Research shows that 25% of service principles in the wild are massively over-privileged, which means they have permission to read every email in the tenant. They can modify every group. They can delete entire SharePoint sites. This happens because we treat these identities like global admins for apps. We assume that because it's a machine it's safe. But in reality it's the opposite. A compromised user account is limited by human speed, where an attacker has to click and browse, but a compromised service principle token is limited only by the speed of the API. If an attacker steals a token with directory, readright. On all, they can create 10 new accounts and grant them administrative rights in seconds. They can automate the exfiltration of your entire document library before your security team even gets the first alert. This is the blast radius problem. When you give a principle broad directory roles, you are removing the walls between your data silos. One single leaked secret in a misconfigured key vault can escalate into a full tenant takeover. Audit findings from 2025 are terrifying. They show that 40% of persistence vectors in modern breaches involve unmonetored service principles. Attacters aren't just looking for your password anymore. They are looking for your ghost identities. They want the principles that have app only exchange access and they want the ones that can bypass MFA because they are trusted background processes. These are the crown jewels. To stop this, we have to move toward our back for applications. You need to stop using broad directory-level roles for specific tasks. If your application only needs to read files from one specific sharepoint site, don't give it sites. Read all, but instead use the graph API to granted permission to that specific site ID. If your bot needs to send emails from a specific shared mailbox, use an application access policy in exchange online. Limit that principle so it can only see that one mailbox. This is how you scope the blast radius. You have to move from the mindset of enablement to the mindset of restriction. Every permission you grant is a potential bridge for an intruder. If you can't justify why a principle needs a specific scope, it shouldn't have it. We also have to stop ignoring these identities in our audits. Most companies spend all their time reviewing who is in the global admin group. But they never look at who has app only permissions to their financial records. Those app only scopes are more dangerous because they don't require a user to be present. They run in the dark. If you want to secure your infrastructure, you have to treat service-principal permissions with the same fear you treat root access on a server because at the end of the day, that's exactly what they are. To manage this risk at scale, we have to stop doing it by hand. We need a system for the life cycle. Orchestrating the life cycle. You cannot secure what you don't track. The real danger of moving to a service-principal model isn't actually the technology itself, but rather the sprawl that comes with it. Because these identities don't have faces, they don't have managers and they never show up in the company directory, they tend to linger long after their purpose has vanished. This is the orphaned identity problem. In a typical enterprise tenant, between 20 and 50% of your current service principles are likely ghosts that represent remnants of a project that ended three years ago, or perhaps they are the leftovers of a proof of concept that a consultant built and then abandoned. But here is the catch. Those ghosts still have active permissions and they still have secrets that might be valid, which means they are essentially open doors in a house where nobody lives. To solve this, we have to stop treating identity creation as a one-way street. You need an automated cleaner policy that treats your directory like a living garden because if something isn't growing, it needs to be pruned. We do this by using the Azure Resource Graph and PowerShell. You shouldn't be hunting for these in the portal, but instead you should be running queries that cross-reference your principles against their actual usage. If a service principle hasn't signed in for 90 days, why does it still exist? You can use the service principle sign-in logs to find these dormant entities, but you have to be careful. Some identities are identity-not-found remnants that happen when you delete an app registration while the service principle object or the role assignment stays stuck in the IAM blade. These remnants consume your quota. Azure has a limit of 4,000 role assignments per subscription, so if you let these orphans pile up, you will eventually hit a wall where you can't provision new legitimate infrastructure because your identity graveyard is full. Managed identities have a 30-day soft delete window, meaning if you delete the owning resource, the identity enters a recycle bin. But for standard service principles, the life cycle is often manual and this is where re-certification workflows become mandatory. But before you hit that delete key, you have to map your dependencies, which is the most critical step in orchestration. You need to know exactly which flows, which scripts and which logic apps are calling that specific client ID. If you delete a principle that is hard coded into a legacy production system without a backup, you've just caused the very outage we are trying to prevent. Orchestration is about moving identity management from a manual ticket-based process to a policy-based life cycle, ensuring that every identity has an expiration date, a clear owner, and a documented purpose. When you treat identity as infrastructure, you accept the responsibility of maintaining that infrastructure. You don't just build it, but you manage it from birth to retirement. And even with clean permissions and a managed life cycle, a static secret is still a liability, which is why we have to automate the rotation. The zero downtime rotation model, even the most perfectly scoped identity eventually hits the 90-day expiry wall. This is the moment where your security policy becomes your operational enemy. If you are managing secrets manually, you are essentially scheduling your own future outages. You are waiting for a calendar alert that you might miss, or perhaps a notification email that goes to a service desk inbox that nobody monitors. When that secret expires, your automation stops, and this isn't just a security failure, but a failure of basic system maintenance. To solve this, we have to adopt the dual credential pattern, which is the secret to zero downtime operations. Most people think of rotation as a single event where you delete the old secret and paste in the new one, but that is exactly how you break things. In a high availability environment, you always want an overlap. Microsoft EntraID allows you to have multiple valid credentials for a single service principle simultaneously, making the workflow simple but powerful. First, you generate a new secret without touching the old one yet. You add the new secret to your configuration, so your application has two ways to get in, and then you test the connection. Once you've verified that the new credential is being used successfully, only then do you go back and prune the old one. This ad before-delete strategy ensures that there is never a millisecond where your script is locked out of the tenant, but doing this by hand is still too risky. Human fingers are clumsy, and we often copy and paste the wrong string, or simply forget to save the change. This is why you must integrate with Azure Key Vault, because Key Vault isn't just a digital safe but a dynamic configuration provider. Your power automate flows or your Azure functions should never have a hard-coded password. Instead, they should have a managed identity that gives them permission to read a specific secret from the vault at runtime. When the secret rotates in EntraID, your automation runbook updates the value in Key Vault, and the next time your flow runs, it asks the vault for the latest password and gets the new one automatically. Your code doesn't change, your environment variables don't change, and the plumbing of your identity is handled entirely behind the scenes by Microsoft Graph APIs. For high-security environments, you should move away from secrets entirely and use certificate-based authentication. Secrets are just strings that can be written down or accidentally logged in plain text. Certificates, however, require a private key that never needs to leave your secure storage. By using certificates, you eliminate the risk of credential harvesting from your logs or your source code. This automation is what turns identity from a chore into a capability.
